Competing Witnesses

Rubruck, Polo, Ibn Battuta and de Mussis · Who do we believe, and why?

PART 1

A fourth witness: Caffa, 1346

Caffa (today's Feodosia, in Crimea) was a Genoese trading port on the Black Sea, inside the lands of the Golden Horde. In 1346 the Golden Horde's khan, Jani Beg, had it under siege when plague broke out in his army.

An Italian notary, Gabriele de' Mussis, wrote a famous account a year or two later. He said the dying besiegers flung their dead over the walls with siege engines, the stench and disease spread inside, and Genoese ships fleeing Caffa carried the plague to Italy. It's often called the first act of germ warfare.

PART 2

But was he there?

For a long time, readers assumed de Mussis wrote as an eyewitness. Then historians checked the notary records of his home town, Piacenza. He was signing documents there from 1344 to 1356. He never went to Caffa. He was passing on a story.

Other evidence from the Black Sea region doesn't fit his story either. Some scholars doubt a siege engine could have thrown bodies that way. Even scholars who think the corpse-throwing might have happened doubt it was how the plague reached Europe. Rats, fleas and grain ships are the likelier path. De Mussis may have been right that plague came from the Black Sea and wrong about how.

PART 3

Four tests for any witness

You've now met four witnesses: William of Rubruck, Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta and de Mussis. Every one of them tells some things we can check and some we can't. Historians run each claim through tests like these:

1. Was the witness there? De Mussis wasn't.
2. Does an independent witness agree? Polo and Ibn Battuta, about two generations apart, both noticed paper money. Careful: Day 6 said Ibn Battuta's China chapters may be secondhand. As far as historians can tell, he didn't read Polo, and he stressed different things, so the match still counts, but with a caution.
3. Is there physical evidence? The craftsmen's quarter Rubruck described has been dug up.
4. Is a number suspiciously big, or secondhand? Polo took his twelve thousand bridges of Kinsay from a written statement sent by the Song queen. He says he later saw it was true, but no one else backs the number.

One more rule: an expert is more likely to be right in their own field, but experts can still be wrong. "Trust me" from anyone, even a famous professor, is not evidence. Ask what the claim rests on.

CASE FILE CLOSED

Look back at your vote

You voted on: "When two witnesses disagree, the one writing closer to the time is usually right."

The real answer: not always. De Mussis wrote within a year or two of Caffa, close in time but nowhere near the place. Being close in time helps. Being there, being independent, and having physical evidence matter just as much. Time is one test, not the whole test.
