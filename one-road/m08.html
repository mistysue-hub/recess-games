PART 1

From Lake Issyk Kul to the Mediterranean

In 1338 and 1339 a Christian trading community near Lake Issyk Kul, in today's Kyrgyzstan, buried so many people that their gravestones record the cause: "pestilence." In 2022 scientists recovered plague DNA from those graves. Their strain sits right at the root of the Black Death's family tree. It's strong evidence, though not every scientist agrees it settles the question, that the pandemic began in Central Asia, on the Mongol trade road.

By 1346 plague was in the Golden Horde's lands around the Black Sea. In 1347 ships from the Black Sea carried it into the Mediterranean. Over the next few years it killed perhaps half the people in parts of Europe and the Middle East. (The full story is its own course, The Plague Year.)

PART 2

The road closes

The khanates were already cracking. In 1335 the Ilkhan Abu Sa'id died without an heir, and the Ilkhanate broke apart. In China, famine, floods and rebellions shook the Yuan. In 1368 the armies of Zhu Yuanzhang, a rebel leader who founded the Ming dynasty, took Khanbaliq, and the last Yuan emperor fled north to the steppe. The Golden Horde lasted longer, but the one road across Asia was no longer one road.

PART 3

What the road carried

Envoys. In 1287 Rabban Sauma, a Christian monk born near Khanbaliq, traveled to Europe as the Ilkhan's ambassador. He met the kings of France and England and Pope Nicholas IV.
Science. In 1259 Hulagu funded an observatory at Maragha, in Persia, led by the astronomer Nasir al-Din al-Tusi. A Chinese astronomer worked there alongside Persian and Arab scholars.
Ideas that failed. In 1294 the Ilkhan copied Chinese paper money in Tabriz. Markets stopped and riots broke out, and the notes were soon withdrawn.
Ideas that lasted. Marco Polo's book reached readers across Europe. Christopher Columbus owned a copy and wrote notes in its margins.
Debated. Historians still argue about how much gunpowder knowledge reached Europe through Mongol contact, and how much arrived by other routes.
