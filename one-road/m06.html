One Road · Day 6

Ibn Battuta Crosses the Khanates

1325 to 1349 · The Mongol world through a Muslim traveler's eyes

Before you ride

Seventy years after Rubruck, a young Muslim judge from Morocco set out on the same road. He saw the Mongol world from inside the Islamic world. Today: what did he notice that the Europeans didn't?
PART 1

The judge from Tangier

In June 1325 a 21-year-old student of Islamic law named Ibn Battuta left Tangier, in Morocco, to make the pilgrimage to Mecca. He didn't come home for twenty-four years. He crossed the Islamic world and beyond, working as a judge for sultans, and logged far more miles than Marco Polo.

He kept no journal. Back in Morocco, the sultan had him dictate his travels to a scholar, Ibn Juzayy, who finished the book, the Rihla, around 1355. Like Rustichello with Polo, Ibn Juzayy shaped the story, and some passages seem borrowed from earlier writers.

PART 2

At the court of Özbeg Khan

Around 1332 to 1334 he crossed the steppe of the Golden Horde, whose khan, Özbeg, was a Muslim. What struck him most was the khan's wives and the women of the steppe:

A remarkable thing which I saw in this country was the respect shown to women by the TurksIbn Battuta, trans. H. A. R. Gibb (1929)

Women there, he added, "hold a more dignified position than the men," and they didn't veil their faces. The khan's chief wife poured him a cup of fermented mare's milk with her own hand, which, he wrote, was the highest of honors among them. Then he traveled to Constantinople with another of Özbeg's wives, Bayalun, the daughter of the Byzantine emperor. Further east he met the Chagatai khan Tarmashirin, also a Muslim. Mongol rulers had joined his world.

PART 3

China, 1345 to 1346

Ibn Battuta reached China by sea. He was impressed by its order:

China is the safest and best regulated of countries for a traveller.Ibn Battuta, trans. H. A. R. Gibb (1929)

Like Polo, he noticed the money: "Their buying and selling is carried on exclusively by means of pieces of paper." But he was uneasy too. He called the Chinese "infidels, who worship idols," and he stayed close to the Muslim merchants: "In every Chinese city there is a quarter for Muslims."

How do we know? His China chapters are much vaguer than the rest of the book. Many historians doubt he really traveled as far north as Beijing, as he claims. He may have filled gaps with what other merchants told him.
PART 4

Home through the plague

Heading home, he walked into a catastrophe. In July 1348 he reached Damascus as the Black Death raged there. Back in Morocco he learned that his own mother had died of the plague. The road that had carried him across the world had carried the disease too. You'll follow its route on Day 8.

Whose Eyes?

Eight real lines from this week's three travelers. Who wrote each one? Your pick locks in, then the clue shows.

THREE WITNESSES

SAME PLACE, TWO EYES

Paper money in China

MARCO POLO, 1270s TO 1290sA merchant amazed that one kind of paper works everywhere in the khan's lands, and that the khan makes it from tree bark.
IBN BATTUTA, 1340sA judge from a world of gold dinars and silver dirhams, struck that the Chinese use neither.

Two witnesses, about two generations apart, saw the same thing. That makes it strong evidence. What each one noticed about it tells you about the witness.

Look back at your vote

You voted on: "A traveler who shares a land's religion will describe it more fairly than one who doesn't."

The real answer: not necessarily. Ibn Battuta was most at ease among fellow Muslims, and he still called the Chinese idol-worshippers. William of Rubruck, a Christian, was harshest about other Christians, the Nestorians. Every witness looks through a lens. Sharing a faith changes the lens. It doesn't remove it.
