PART 1

An army counted in tens

Before 1206 the steppe was a tangle of rival clans. Chinggis Khan reorganized his people into units of ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand (a tümen). Old clans were often split up and mixed together, and each unit answered to a commander picked for loyalty, much like the followers who had once chosen Temüjin.

He ruled through orders and laws later called the Yasa. No full copy of the Yasa has ever been found. Historians piece it together from later writers, and they still argue about what it really said.

PART 2

The yam: the game you just played

An empire this size runs on news. Under Chinggis's son Ögedei (ruled 1229 to 1241), the Mongols built out a chain of relay stations called the yam, taking over older post stations in northern China along the way. Stations usually stood about 20 to 40 miles apart, stocked with spare horses, food and shelter.

A rider on the khan's business carried a paiza, a metal tablet showing the khan's authority. Showing it meant fresh horses and food at every station. That's why the yam route beat the short route in your game: a rider never had to wait for a tired horse. Merchants rode the system free at first, until Möngke Khan made them pay for it.

PART 3

The storm in the west

In 1218 a governor of the Khwarazm Empire (in today's Iran, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan) killed a Mongol trade caravan. From 1219 to 1221 Mongol armies destroyed that empire. Cities that resisted, such as Merv and Nishapur, were stormed and their people killed or carried off.

The historian Ibn al-Athir, writing in Mosul, put off describing it for years:

O would that my mother had not born meIbn al-Athir, trans. E. G. Browne (1902)

He passed on stories of terror he had been told: a single rider killing people in a village while none dared resist, and a group of eighteen captured men ordered to tie each other up. One of them urged the others to fight:

"He is but one man; wherefore, then, should we not kill him and flee?" They replied, "We are afraid."Ibn al-Athir, trans. E. G. Browne (1902)
How do we know? Ibn al-Athir says these stories were related to him. He wasn't there. Chroniclers also claimed death tolls such as 700,000 at Merv, and modern historians think numbers like this are hugely inflated, since the cities couldn't hold that many people. The destruction was real. The exact numbers are guesses.
PART 4

To the edge of Europe

The Jin dynasty of northern China fell in 1234. Mongol armies took the cities of the Rus from 1237 to 1240. In April 1241 they crushed a Polish army at Legnica and a Hungarian army at Mohi, two days apart. At the same time, other Mongol armies were fighting the Song dynasty in southern China, all of them steered from Mongolia by riders on the yam.

Then, in 1242, the armies in Hungary turned around and went home. Why? No Mongol source says. The traditional answer: Ögedei had died in December 1241, and the princes went back to choose a new khan. In 2016 a tree-ring study argued that a cold, wet spring turned Hungary's plains to marsh, ruining pasture for the horses. A 2017 reply argued the evidence didn't support that. Historians are still arguing.
