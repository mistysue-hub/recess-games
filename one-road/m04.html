Four Khanates, One Road

1258 to 1279 · The empire splits, and the road holds

Before you ride

Between 1258 and 1279 the Mongols took Baghdad, lost their first big battle in the west, went to war with each other, and split into four khanates. Today you meet all four, and decide whether a broken empire can still keep a road open.
PART 1

Baghdad, 1258

In February 1258 the army of Hulagu, brother of the great khan Möngke, took Baghdad. The last Abbasid caliph, al-Musta'sim, was put to death, and with him ended a caliphate that had ruled from Baghdad for nearly five hundred years. The city was looted and much of it burned.

How many died? Later chroniclers gave figures from about 200,000 to 800,000. No one counted. Historians treat these numbers as rough guesses, and some as wild overstatements. What isn't in doubt is that one of the great cities of the Islamic world was devastated.
PART 2

Brother against brother

In 1259 Möngke died on campaign in China. Two of his brothers, Kublai and Ariq Böke, each had himself named great khan, and they fought for four years (1260 to 1264) until Kublai won.

The split cost the Mongols in the west. Hulagu pulled most of his army back from Syria, and in September 1260, at Ain Jalut, the Mamluk army of Egypt beat the force he had left behind. The Mongol advance toward Egypt stopped there.

Then Mongols turned on Mongols. In 1262 Berke of the Golden Horde, a Muslim since 1252, went to war with Hulagu. They fought over land in the Caucasus, and both sides massacred the other's merchants. The single Mongol Empire was over. Four khanates took its place.

PART 3

Four khanates, four personalities

GOLDEN HORDE
Founded in 1243 by Batu, a grandson of Chinggis, on the steppes of the Volga River. Capital: Sarai. Russian princes paid it tribute. Khan Berke became a Muslim in 1252, and Islam became the khanate's faith under Özbeg (1313). Lands today: Russia, Ukraine, Kazakhstan.
ILKHANATE
Founded by Hulagu, Möngke's brother, after his campaigns in Persia and Iraq. Capital: Maragha at first, then Tabriz. Its khans turned Muslim with Ghazan in 1295. Lands today: Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan.
CHAGATAI KHANATE
Ruled by the line of Chagatai, Chinggis's second son, in the heart of Central Asia. Capital: Almaliq. From here, Kaidu (a grandson of Ögedei) fought Kublai for about thirty years. Its khan Tarmashirin became a Muslim around 1331. Lands today: Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, western China.
YUAN DYNASTY
Founded by Kublai, who built a new capital, Khanbaliq (on the site of today's Beijing), from 1267, took the Chinese dynasty name Yuan in 1271, and conquered the last of the Song in 1279. Kublai favored Tibetan Buddhist teachers such as Phags-pa. Lands today: China and Mongolia.
PART 4

Did the road survive?

The khanates often fought each other: Berke against Hulagu, and Kaidu against Kublai until Kaidu's death in 1301. Merchants were robbed and killed when rulers quarrelled.

Yet the road didn't close. In 1271 two Venetian merchants, Niccolò and Maffeo Polo, set out east again with Niccolò's teenage son, Marco. According to Marco's own book, they crossed the lands of rival khanates and reached Kublai's court. Whether to believe him is a real argument, and you'll judge it on Day 5.

Look back at your vote

You voted on: "When an empire splits apart, trade along its roads must collapse."

The real answer: not quite. The khanates fought each other, and rulers did kill each other's merchants in wartime. But travel and trade kept going: by Marco Polo's own account, his family crossed from the Mediterranean to Kublai's court in the 1270s. The empire split. The road bent, but it didn't break.
