Where Does Knowledge Come From?

Tap the arrows, the page corner, or swipe to turn the page.

A puzzle in the sand

In Plato's dialogue Meno, written around 2,400 years ago, Socrates draws a square in the sand. Each side is two feet long, so the square covers four square feet. He calls over a boy who has never studied geometry and asks him: how long must the sides be to make a square twice as big, eight square feet?

The boy answers at once: double the sides. Four feet.

Socrates draws it. The new square holds four of the old squares, not two. Sixteen square feet. The boy tries three feet instead. Nine square feet: still too big. Now he's stuck, and he admits he doesn't know.

Socrates says the boy is better off: before, he thought he knew; now he knows he doesn't. He has had "the torpedo's shock" (Jowett's 1871 translation), like the sting of a numbing torpedo fish.

The boy finds the answer

Then Socrates draws a line from corner to corner across each small square. Those diagonals make a tilted square in the middle. Each diagonal cuts a four-foot square in half, and the tilted square is made of four of those halves. Eight square feet. The answer is the square built on the diagonal.

Socrates insists he only asked questions. So where did the answer come from?

Learning as remembering

Socrates' answer is startling. He says the boy already had the knowledge inside him, from before he was born, and the questions just woke it up:

these notions have just been stirred up in him, as in a dream

Plato's view is that learning is really recollection. Few people today believe in remembering a life before birth. But the puzzle he raised is still alive. Some things seem knowable just by thinking hard, without looking at the world. How?

Key terms

A priori knowledge doesn't depend on experience of the world: 2 + 2 = 4, or "all bachelors are unmarried."

A posteriori knowledge does depend on experience: water boils at 100 °C at sea level; some swans are black.

The blank sheet

In 1689 the English philosopher John Locke disagreed sharply. He asked his readers to

suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas

So where do all our ideas come from? "To this I answer, in one word, from experience." For Locke, everything in the mind arrives either through the senses or through noticing the mind's own workings, which he called reflection. People who follow this line are called empiricists. Those who think reason alone can give us real knowledge, like Plato and Descartes, are called rationalists.

ExtendedLocke's picture is often summed up with the Latin tabula rasa, "scraped tablet." The image of the mind as a blank writing tablet goes back to Aristotle.

Molyneux's question

Here is a puzzle that tested the empiricist view. William Molyneux, the Irish scientist whose Descartes translation you read in Lock 2, sent Locke a question. Imagine a man born blind who has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch. If he suddenly gained his sight, could he tell which was which just by looking, before touching them?

Molyneux and Locke both said no: he would have to learn the link between how things feel and how they look. A rationalist might say yes: the idea of a cube is the same idea, whichever sense delivers it.

For three hundred years this was a thought experiment. Then, in 2011, a team studied children in India who had been blind from birth and had their sight restored by surgery. Right after surgery, they could not match shapes they had felt to shapes they saw. Within days, they could.

Veins in the marble

The German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz wrote a long reply to Locke. He agreed that experience matters, but he said the mind is not blank paper. It is more like a block of marble with veins running through it. The veins already mark where a statue of Hercules could be carved. Experience is the sculptor, but the marble has a grain.

Leibniz added a famous twist to an old slogan. "Nothing is in the mind that was not first in the senses," the empiricists said. Leibniz agreed, except the mind itself.

ExtendedLeibniz wrote his New Essays on Human Understanding around 1704, but held them back after Locke died that year. They were published in 1765.

Hume's fork

The Scottish philosopher David Hume pushed empiricism further. In 1748 he split everything we can know into two kinds:

All the objects of human reason or enquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to wit, Relations of Ideas, and Matters of Fact.

Relations of ideas, like math and logic, are certain and need no experience, but they tell us nothing about what actually exists. Matters of fact tell us about the world, but only experience can teach them, and they could have been otherwise. Philosophers call this split Hume's fork.

He ended his book with a challenge. If a book contains neither reasoning about quantity or number nor reasoning from experience about matters of fact, then: "Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

Kant's third way

The German philosopher Immanuel Kant said Hume woke him from a "dogmatic slumber." In 1781 he opened his Critique of Pure Reason with a careful line, here in J. M. D. Meiklejohn's translation:

though all our knowledge begins with experience, it by no means follows that all arises out of experience

Key terms

An analytic claim is true just because of what its words mean: "all bachelors are unmarried."

A synthetic claim adds something new that the words alone don't contain: "this bachelor is tall."

Kant argued that some knowledge is both synthetic and a priori. His example was 7 + 5 = 12: he said the idea of "twelve" isn't contained in the idea of "seven plus five," yet we know the sum without any experiment. His explanation was that the mind shapes all experience with its own built-in forms, such as space, time and cause and effect.

ExtendedLater thinkers pushed back. Gottlob Frege argued in 1884 that arithmetic is analytic after all, rooted in logic. Einstein's general relativity (1915) described real space as curved, unsettling Kant's view that Euclid's geometry was known a priori. In 1951 W. V. O. Quine argued that the line between analytic and synthetic can't be drawn cleanly at all.

Perception, reason and what you already know don't always agree, and the arguments in this lock are about which one gets the final word. Later locks add more sources: memory, and other people's word.
