The thousandth morning

Picture a chicken on a farm. Every morning, for its whole life, the farmer has come out and fed it. By now the chicken has a lot of evidence. Day after day, the same thing. If chickens could reason, this one would be very confident about tomorrow.

In 1912 Bertrand Russell finished the story in The Problems of Philosophy:

The man who has fed the chicken every day throughout its life at last wrings its neck instead, showing that more refined views as to the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken.

It's a dark joke with a serious point. We are all, in a way, that chicken. Almost everything we expect about tomorrow rests on what happened yesterday.

Two kinds of argument

Key terms

A deductive argument guarantees its conclusion: if the premises are true, the conclusion has to be true. "All squares have four sides; this is a square; so it has four sides."

An inductive argument makes its conclusion likely, not certain. It reasons from cases you've seen to cases you haven't. "Every swan I've seen is white; so the next swan will be white."

Science, cooking, crossing the street and trusting your chair all run on induction. You've never tested the chair you're sitting on today. You've tested chairs.

Induction is so ordinary that it seems strange to ask what justifies it. The Scottish philosopher David Hume, whom you met in Lock 3, asked anyway.

Hume's problem

Hume pointed out that the future going differently from the past is not a contradiction. In 1748 he wrote:

That the sun will not rise to-morrow is no less intelligible a proposition, and implies no more contradiction than the affirmation, that it will rise.

So no deductive proof can show the sun will rise. What about evidence? Every argument from evidence assumes that the future will resemble the past. But how do we know that? Only from the past, where it has always worked. Using induction to prove that induction works goes in a circle, the kind you met in Lock 4.

Hume's conclusion was not that we should stop expecting sunrises. It was that the expectation comes from habit, not from reason. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life."

ExtendedPhilosophers call this the problem of induction. Hume didn't use that name, and it's still argued about. Some say the circle is harmless, some say induction is justified because it's reliable (Lock 4's reliabilism), and some say it can't be justified at all but we can't help using it.

Betting on the sunrise

One reply is to give up on certainty and talk about probability. In 1814 the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace worked through the sunrise example. Taking about 5,000 years of recorded sunrises as the evidence, his formula gave odds of roughly 1.8 million to one that the sun would rise the next day. He added that anyone who understands the principle that regulates days and seasons should be far more confident than that.

Updating your confidence

The idea behind this goes back to the English minister Thomas Bayes, whose essay on probability was published in 1763, after his death. On a Bayesian view, you never get certainty. You start with a degree of confidence and update it, up or down, each time new evidence arrives.

Key term

Credence: how confident you are in a belief, from 0 (certainly false) to 1 (certainly true). A Bayesian updates credences as evidence comes in.

Critics answer that this doesn't escape Hume. Your probabilities still assume the future will behave like the past.

One black swan

For centuries, Europeans used "a black swan" as a figure of speech for something that doesn't exist. Every swan anyone had seen was white. Then, in 1697, Dutch sailors exploring the west coast of Australia saw black swans.

The Austrian-British philosopher Karl Popper built a whole view of science on this lesson, starting with his 1934 book later translated as The Logic of Scientific Discovery. No number of white swans can prove "all swans are white." One black swan can disprove it.

So, Popper said, science doesn't work by piling up confirmations. It works by making bold guesses and trying hard to knock them down. A good theory is one that says clearly what would prove it wrong, and survives every honest attempt. On his view a theory is never proved, only not yet refuted.

ExtendedPopper's falsificationism has critics too. Real scientists don't usually throw out a theory after one bad result; they check the equipment first. And Popper's view still seems to lean on induction when we choose which surviving theory to rely on tomorrow.

The new riddle

In 1955 the American philosopher Nelson Goodman made the problem stranger. He invented a word: grue. Something is grue if it's green and first examined before some future date, say the year 2100, or blue and first examined after that.

Every emerald anyone has ever examined was green. So every one of them was also grue. The evidence fits "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" equally well. But the two predict different colors for emeralds first examined after 2100.

We all feel sure "green" is the right word to project into the future and "grue" is a cheat. Goodman's question is why. "Grue mentions a date" isn't enough: to someone who grew up with "grue" and "bleen," our word "green" would be the one that mentions a date.

ExtendedGoodman's own answer, in Fact, Fiction, and Forecast, was that "green" is entrenched: it has a long history of being used successfully in predictions. Critics say that just describes our habits, which takes us back to Hume.

Carry this with you

Nobody has solved the problem of induction to everyone's satisfaction. Yet nobody stops using induction either. The honest position may be the one in between: hold expectations about the future with confidence that matches your evidence, look for the observations that could prove you wrong, and stay ready to be the chicken who notices something has changed.

Lock 8 will come back to that idea: how well your confidence matches how often you're right.
