What Counts as Knowing?

The stopped clock

You glance at the clock in the hallway. It says three o'clock, and it is three o'clock. You believe it's three, your belief is true, and you have a decent reason: that clock has been right every day for years. But yesterday its battery died at exactly three, and it hasn't moved since. Do you know what time it is, or did you just get lucky?

Most people say you got lucky. Hold on to that feeling. This whole lock is about where it comes from, and why it has been so hard to pin down.

ExtendedBertrand Russell used a stopped clock in Human Knowledge: Its Scope and Limits (1948) to show that a true belief, held for a reasonable-looking reason, can still fall short of knowledge.

Two kinds of knowing

The word know covers more than one thing.

Key terms

Knowing-how is a skill you show by doing: riding a bike, parallel parking, tying a bowline.

Knowing-that is a claim you can state, one that is either true or false: water boils at 100 °C at sea level.

You can know how to ride a bike without being able to explain the physics, and you can know every fact about bikes and still fall off. There is also knowing a person or a place ("I know Chicago"), sometimes called knowledge by familiarity or acquaintance. This course is about knowing-that, because that is where the arguments are fiercest.

ExtendedGilbert Ryle drew the how/that line in a 1946 paper and developed it in The Concept of Mind (1949), and argued that knowing-how can't be reduced to a pile of facts. In 2001 Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson argued the opposite: that knowing how to do something is knowing a fact about a way to do it. The debate is still open.

Plato's first try

About 2,400 years ago Plato wrote a dialogue called Theaetetus. In it, Socrates and a gifted young mathematician named Theaetetus test one definition of knowledge after another. Knowledge is perception? Knocked down. Knowledge is true belief? Knocked down too: a clever lawyer can talk a jury into a true belief about a crime only an eyewitness could know about, and the jury still doesn't know what happened. Near the end they try this, put simply: knowledge is true belief plus an account.

An "account" (in Greek, logos) means something like a reason or an explanation. Even this one doesn't survive. The dialogue ends with nobody able to say what knowledge is. Socrates says the effort still helped: Theaetetus will no longer think he knows what he doesn't.

The three-part recipe

Twentieth-century philosophers boiled Plato's idea down to a recipe. You know a claim when:

  1. you believe it,
  2. it is true, and
  3. you are justified in believing it, meaning you have good reasons.

This is called justified true belief, or JTB. Each part does a job. Belief: you can't know something you don't even accept. Truth: you can't know something false; you can only think you know it. Justification: a lucky guess isn't knowledge, even when it's right. By the middle of the 1900s, many philosophers treated JTB as the standard definition.

ExtendedHistorians of philosophy argue about how standard it really was before then. Some say the "traditional" JTB view was partly a creation of the people who later attacked it.

Three pages that broke it

In 1963 a philosopher named Edmund Gettier published a paper three pages long. It told two short stories. Here is the first, retold.

Smith and Jones have both applied for the same job. The company president has told Smith that Jones will get it, and ten minutes ago Smith counted the coins in Jones's pocket: ten. So Smith reasons his way to a new belief: The person who gets the job has ten coins in his pocket.

Then the surprise. Smith gets the job. And without realizing it, Smith also had ten coins in his own pocket.

Why that's a problem

Run the recipe. Smith believes the sentence. It is true: the man who got the job did have ten coins. And he was justified: the president's word and his own count are good reasons.

All three parts are there, and it still isn't knowledge. His reasons were all about Jones. The sentence came out true because of facts about Smith. The reasons and the truth never touched. It was true by luck.

Stories like this are now called Gettier cases. They share one shape: a belief with good reasons, a truth, and a lucky gap between the two. The stopped clock has the same shape.

ExtendedGettier's second story works the same way. Smith has good evidence that Jones owns a Ford, so he believes "Either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona," with no idea where Brown is. Jones doesn't own a Ford. By pure chance, Brown is in Barcelona.

Can the recipe be fixed?

Philosophers spent decades trying to add a fourth ingredient. One early idea: no false steps. Smith reasoned through a false belief (Jones will get the job), so that rule would rule him out.

The trouble is that some Gettier cases have no false step. Picture Henry driving through a county where the locals have put up dozens of fake barn fronts, flat facades that look real from the road. Henry happens to look at the one real barn and thinks, That's a barn. True, justified, no false step, and still lucky: almost any other barn he looked at would have fooled him.

ExtendedAlvin Goldman published the fake barn case in 1976, crediting the idea to Carl Ginet. Other fixes ask whether you would stop believing the claim if it were false (sensitivity, from Robert Nozick in 1981), or whether your belief could easily have been wrong (safety). In 1994 Linda Zagzebski argued that any recipe allowing even a small gap between reasons and truth can be beaten by a new Gettier case. In 2000 Timothy Williamson suggested giving up the recipe altogether and treating knowledge as basic.

Nobody has a fix that everyone accepts. That is part of what makes this lock hard.

Carry this with you

Knowing seems to need more than being right, and more than having reasons. It needs the reasons and the truth to be connected, not lucky.

Every lock ahead comes back to that question in a new form. Where do reasons come from? How far down do they go? Can you trust someone else's? And what makes something true in the first place?
