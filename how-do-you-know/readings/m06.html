Should You Take Their Word for It?

Most of what you know

Do you know that the Earth goes around the Sun? That you were born on your birthday? That Antarctica exists? Almost certainly. Have you checked any of these yourself? Almost certainly not.

You learned them from parents, teachers, books, maps and videos. Philosophers call this source of knowledge testimony: believing something because someone told you. Without it you would know almost nothing beyond your own street, and nothing about anything before you were born.

So testimony is everywhere. The hard question is when it gives you knowledge, and when it just passes along someone else's mistake.

Two Scottish answers

In the 1700s two Scottish philosophers gave opposite answers.

David Hume, back again from Locks 3 and 5, said testimony is just another kind of evidence. You trust a witness only as far as experience has shown that witnesses like this are reliable. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."

Trust as the starting point

Thomas Reid answered in 1764 that this can't be how it works. A small child believes what she's told long before she has any experience of how reliable people are. Reid thought we are built with two matching instincts: a principle of veracity, a natural tendency to tell the truth, and a principle of credulity, a natural tendency to believe what we're told. Trust is the starting point. We pull it back only when we have a reason.

Key terms

Reductionism (Hume's side): testimony is justified only by other evidence, such as your experience of the speaker's reliability.

Anti-reductionism (Reid's side): you're entitled to believe what you're told unless you have reason to doubt it.

Miracles and the weight of evidence

Hume applied his view to the hardest case: reports of miracles. In 1748 he proposed a rule:

That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish

In plain words: weigh which is more likely, that this report is mistaken or dishonest, or that the laws of nature were broken. Believe the more likely one. Hume thought reports of miracles almost always lose that contest.

This chapter of Hume's book is one of the most argued-over pieces of philosophy ever written. Critics say it stacks the deck: if you've decided in advance that miracles are near-impossible, no evidence could ever change your mind. Defenders say the rule simply asks you to weigh evidence honestly, and it works just as well for everyday wild claims.

Which expert?

Most of us can't check a climate model, a medical trial or a court ruling ourselves. So we rely on experts. But experts sometimes disagree. How can a non-expert choose?

In 2001 the American philosopher Alvin Goldman, whom you've met twice before, listed five kinds of clues a non-expert can still use:

  1. Arguments: can you follow at least part of each side's reasoning?
  2. Agreement: how many other experts agree?
  3. Credentials: what do other experts say about this person's training and work?
  4. Interests and biases: does this person gain from your believing them?
  5. Track record: how often have they been right before?

None of these is perfect. Together they're far better than going with whoever sounds most confident.

When the experts were wrong

For most of the 1900s, doctors were taught that stomach ulcers were caused by stress and acid. In the early 1980s two Australian doctors, Robin Warren and Barry Marshall, argued that most ulcers are caused by a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori. Many experts dismissed the idea.

In 1984, frustrated, Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria. Within days he was ill with the stomach inflammation that can lead to ulcers, and antibiotics cleared it up. Over the next decade the evidence piled up, and treatment for ulcers changed around the world. In 2005 Warren and Marshall won the Nobel Prize.

This story is real. Its lesson isn't "ignore experts." Most of the time, the experts in a field are far more likely to be right about it than anyone else. The lesson is that experts can still be wrong, and when they are, it's evidence that changes the answer, not confidence or credentials.

Bubbles and echo chambers

In 2020 the philosopher C. Thi Nguyen pointed out two different ways a group can go wrong together.

Key terms

In an epistemic bubble, other voices are simply missing. You never happen to hear them. Show people the missing evidence and the bubble can pop.

In an echo chamber, other voices are actively distrusted. Members are taught in advance that outsiders lie. Show them outside evidence and they may trust their group more.

Nguyen's point is that the cures are different. A bubble needs more information. An echo chamber needs something harder: rebuilding trust in sources outside the group.

When a friend disagrees

Here's a puzzle that's harder than it looks. You and a friend are equally good at mental math. You split a restaurant bill in your heads and come up with slightly different amounts. Neither of you has more information than the other. What should you do?

Conciliationists say the disagreement itself is evidence: you should both become less confident, maybe meet in the middle. Steadfast thinkers say that if you actually did the math right, you can keep your answer; your friend's mistake doesn't make you wrong. Most people's instincts change depending on the case: with the bill, check again; on a deep moral question, many feel entitled to stand firm.

ExtendedThe restaurant-bill case comes from the philosopher David Christensen (2007), a leading conciliationist. Thomas Kelly (2005) defended a more steadfast view. The debate about "peer disagreement" is one of the liveliest in recent epistemology.

Carry this with you

Testimony is how almost all knowledge travels. You can't avoid it, and you shouldn't want to. But you can use it well: notice who is telling you, check their track record and their interests, ask what the evidence is, and be most careful of anyone who asks you to trust them instead of showing you.
