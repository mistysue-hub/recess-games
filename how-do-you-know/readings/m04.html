What Holds a Belief Up?

Tap the arrows, the page corner, or swipe to turn the page.

Why? Why? Why?

Every small child discovers the same game. "Why is the sky blue?" Because of how sunlight scatters. "Why does it scatter?" Because of the air. "Why?" Sooner or later the grown-up says, "Because I said so."

The child has found one of the oldest problems in philosophy. In Lock 1, knowledge needed justification: good reasons. But a reason only helps if you're justified in believing the reason too. And that reason needs a reason. Where does it stop?

Key term

The regress problem: if every belief needs a reason, and every reason is itself a belief that needs a reason, the chain of reasons seems to have no end.

Three ways out, all bad

Around 1,800 years ago the Greek writer Sextus Empiricus reported five arguments that a later writer, Diogenes Laertius, credits to a skeptic named Agrippa. Squeezed down, they say a chain of reasons can end in only three ways:

  1. It goes on forever. Every reason gets another reason, with no last one. But then, the skeptic says, you never actually finish justifying anything.
  2. It goes in a circle. Somewhere down the chain, a belief is supported by itself. That looks like cheating.
  3. It just stops. At some point you say "that's simply true." But then the last belief has no support, so neither does anything resting on it.

This is called Agrippa's trilemma: three options, each with a problem. Each of the main theories of justification picks one of the three and argues it isn't so bad after all.

ExtendedThe German philosopher Hans Albert renamed it the Münchhausen trilemma in 1968, after the fictional Baron Münchhausen, who claimed to have pulled himself and his horse out of a swamp by his own hair.

Aristotle draws a line

Aristotle had already faced the problem in his Posterior Analytics, in the 300s BC. Some people of his time said real knowledge was impossible, because the chain of proof never ends. Others said everything can be proved, even if the proofs go in a circle. Aristotle rejected both:

Our own doctrine is that not all knowledge is demonstrative

Some truths, he said, are known directly, without being proved from anything else. Proofs start from them. They are the first principles.

So Aristotle took the third way out: the chain stops, and stopping is fine, as long as it stops at the right kind of belief.

Building on foundations

That view is called foundationalism. Picture knowledge as a building. Most beliefs rest on other beliefs, like upper floors resting on lower ones. But at the bottom sit basic beliefs that don't need support from other beliefs.

What could be basic? Foundationalists have proposed things like "I'm in pain," "this looks red to me," "I exist" (Descartes's foundation from Lock 2), and the simplest truths of logic. You don't infer that you're in pain from evidence. You just know.

The worries come quickly. A few "this looks red to me" beliefs seem too thin to hold up physics, history and everything else. And why do those beliefs get to skip the line? Critics say calling them basic just names the problem without solving it.

ExtendedTwentieth-century foundationalists often split into "strong" versions, where basic beliefs must be certain, and "modest" versions, where basic beliefs only need to be reasonable without support from other beliefs.

Rebuilding the ship at sea

Coherentism takes the circle option and argues it isn't vicious if it's big enough. Beliefs aren't a building with a basement. They're more like a web, where each belief is supported by its fit with all the others.

The Austrian philosopher Otto Neurath gave the view its best-known picture in 1932. We are like sailors who have to rebuild their ship on the open sea, never able to haul it into dry dock and start fresh from the bottom. In 1951 the American W. V. O. Quine described everything we believe as a man-made fabric that touches experience only along its edges; a surprise at the edge can be absorbed by adjusting many different places inside. Later he called it a web of belief.

The big worry: a well-written fantasy novel is perfectly coherent, and it isn't true. If fit is all that matters, what ties the web to the actual world?

Reasons forever

The least popular option is infinitism: the chain of reasons really can go on forever, and that's fine. The American philosopher Peter Klein defended it in 1999. You don't need to have thought of every reason. You need there to always be a further reason available if someone asks.

Critics point out that our minds are finite. Can a chain we could never finish really do the work of justifying anything? Klein's answer is that justification comes in degrees and grows the further you go, the way a detective's case gets stronger with each piece of evidence.

ExtendedKlein's paper "Human Knowledge and the Infinite Regress of Reasons" appeared in Philosophical Perspectives in 1999. Infinitism remains a minority view, but it forced the other camps to sharpen their arguments.

A different question

All three theories ask: what reasons do you have? In 1979 the American philosopher Alvin Goldman, whose fake barns you met in Lock 1, asked a different question: how was the belief produced?

His view is called reliabilism. A belief is justified if it comes from a process that usually gets things right, like normal vision in good light, or careful memory, even if you couldn't put your reasons into words. An experienced radiologist can often spot a problem on an X-ray before she could say exactly why.

Inside or outside?

Reliabilism splits philosophers into two camps.

Key terms

Internalism: justification depends on reasons you can access from the inside, by reflecting.

Externalism: justification can depend on facts you may not be aware of, like how reliable your eyes or memory actually are.

Internalists object: if a belief-forming process happened to be reliable but you had every reason to distrust it, would your belief really be justified?

Carry this with you

In 1993 the British philosopher Susan Haack suggested a middle path she called foundherentism, and she compared it to a crossword puzzle. Each answer is supported by its clue, which is like experience, and by how it crosses the answers around it, which is like coherence. Neither alone is enough. Together they can make you very confident.

So when someone asks "how do you know?", there may be more than one good kind of answer: what you saw, how it fits with everything else you know, and whether the way you came to believe it tends to be trustworthy.
