Virtues of the mind

In the 1980s and 1990s some philosophers suggested that epistemology should look less at single beliefs and more at the people who hold them. Ernest Sosa focused on reliable abilities, like good eyesight and memory. Linda Zagzebski, who argued that no recipe for knowledge can fully escape Gettier cases, focused on character. Just as there are moral virtues like honesty and courage, she argued, there are intellectual virtues:

Key terms

Curiosity: wanting to find out, not just to be right.

Intellectual humility: owning the limits of what you know.

Open-mindedness: taking other views seriously enough to test them.

Intellectual courage: saying what the evidence shows when it's unpopular.

Carefulness: checking before you claim.

Knowing well

On Zagzebski's view, knowledge is true belief that comes from these virtues at work, not from luck. That's one more answer to Gettier: the lucky believer got the truth, but not because of good thinking.

How sure should you be?

Here's a practical test of all seven locks at once. Imagine a forecaster who says "70% chance of rain" on a hundred different days. If she's well calibrated, it rains on about 70 of those days. When her confidence matches how often she's right, she is calibrated.

Weather forecasters are a famous success story. Studies going back to the 1970s found that U.S. National Weather Service forecasters' probabilities of rain were remarkably reliable. Most people are not like that. In many studies, when ordinary people say they're 90% sure of an answer, they turn out to be right far less often than nine times in ten. We tend to be overconfident.

The good news is that calibration can be practiced. In forecasting tournaments in the 2010s, the psychologist Philip Tetlock found people he called "superforecasters," who got better by keeping score, breaking questions into parts and changing their minds in small steps as evidence arrived. That's the Bayesian updating from Lock 5, done by hand.

Who gets believed

The English philosopher Miranda Fricker pointed out in 2007 that knowledge also has an ethical side. She called it epistemic injustice: being wronged specifically in your role as a knower.

Key terms

Testimonial injustice: someone is believed less than they deserve because of a prejudice about who they are.

Hermeneutical injustice: someone can't make sense of, or explain, their own experience because the shared concepts for it don't exist yet, since people like them have been left out of shaping those concepts.

Two examples

One of Fricker's main examples of the first kind comes from the trial in Harper Lee's novel To Kill a Mockingbird, where an all-white jury refuses to believe Tom Robinson, a truthful Black defendant, because of racial prejudice. For the second kind, she points to the 1970s, when the term "sexual harassment" was first coined: before that, people who experienced it lacked a shared name for what was happening to them.

The lesson connects to Lock 6. Weighing testimony well means giving people the credibility their evidence deserves, no more and no less, whoever they are.

Can a machine know?

Here is a question nobody could have asked when most of this course's philosophers were writing. AI systems that write fluent text can answer questions, explain ideas and, as this course admits openly, help write course readings. Do they know anything?

Running the tools

Run the tools from this course. Lock 1's recipe asks whether there is a belief: some say a program has no beliefs at all, while others say believing is a matter of how a system stores and uses information, which these systems arguably do. Lock 4's reliabilism asks only whether the process tends to produce truths: some say a reliable system could count, while others point out that these systems can produce confident, fluent statements that are simply false. Lock 6 suggests treating an AI's answer as testimony from a source whose answers can be wrong, one you check rather than simply trust.

Key term

A useful habit with any source, human or machine: trace the claim. Where did it come from, and what evidence would show it's wrong?

Philosophers, computer scientists and the public are actively arguing about all of this. You now have the tools to join in.

Your own theory

Every philosopher in this course left an answer behind: Plato's account, Descartes's foundation, Locke's blank sheet, Hume's habit, Popper's black swan, Haack's crossword. None of them settled the question for good, and that's part of what makes it worth asking.

Your final job, after the Vault, is to write your own theory of knowledge in five sentences. Say what you think knowledge is, what makes a belief justified, and how your view answers at least one challenge from this course: the skeptic, the regress, induction or testimony. You can borrow from anyone you've met, as long as you say why.

Carry this with you

"How do you know?" turned out to be several questions at once: about truth, reasons, sources, other people, the future and yourself. You won't always be able to answer all of them. But you can always ask them, of other people's claims and, hardest of all, of your own.
