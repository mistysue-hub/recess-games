Could It All Be a Dream?

Tap the arrows, the page corner, or swipe to turn the page.

Are you sure you're awake?

Think of the most real dream you've ever had. While it was happening, did you know it was a dream? Probably not. You believed the falling, the test you hadn't studied for, the friend who was somehow also your cousin.

So here's an uncomfortable question: what tells you that this isn't one of those times?

In 1641 the French philosopher René Descartes published Meditations on First Philosophy. His plan was bold. He would tear down every belief he held, then rebuild knowledge using only what could not be doubted at all.

ExtendedAnswering critics later, Descartes compared the method to tipping out a whole basket of apples to find the rotten ones, then putting back only the sound ones.

Doubt, level one: the senses

Descartes starts with the senses. They sometimes fool us; think of an oar dipped in water that looks bent. Later in the book Descartes gives his own example: towers that looked round from far away and turned out to be square up close.

Key term

Skepticism is the view that we know much less than we think, or nothing at all. Descartes uses it as a tool, not a conclusion: he doubts in order to find what survives.

But this first doubt is weak. The senses fool us about things that are tiny, far away or seen in bad light. They don't seem to fool Descartes about the fact that he's sitting by the fire in his winter dressing gown, holding a sheet of paper. That belief survives level one.

Doubt, level two: the dream

Then Descartes remembers how often he has dreamed that very scene: sitting by the fire, dressed, when really he was undressed in bed. In the 1680 translation he writes:

I am so far convinced of the difficulty of distinguishing sleep from waking…

If no sure sign separates waking from dreaming, then "I'm sitting by the fire" falls. So does "I have hands." Dreams can fake all of that.

What survives? Descartes points out that even painters who invent sirens and satyrs still have to use real colors. Dreams are built from simple pieces: shape, size, number, time. And some truths hold asleep or awake. Two plus three is five in a dream too. A square still has four sides.

Doubt, level three: the deceiver

So Descartes reaches for the strongest doubt he can imagine. Suppose, he says,

that some Evil Spirit which is very Powerful and crafty has used all his endeavours to deceive me

This deceiver could make the sky, the earth, his own body and every sound and color into illusions. Worse, it could make him go wrong every single time he adds two and three, or counts the sides of a square. Now even arithmetic falls.

At this point it looks as if nothing is left. Descartes says he feels like someone who has fallen into deep water and can neither touch the bottom nor swim to the surface.

ExtendedAt first Descartes wonders whether God might be the one deceiving him. Since God is supposed to be supremely good, he swaps in the evil spirit instead, a deceiver he can imagine without that objection.

The one thing left standing

In the Second Meditation, Descartes finds a floor. If the deceiver is fooling him, then he must exist to be fooled. Even doubting is a kind of thinking, and something has to be doing it.

whenever this sentence I am, I exist, is spoken or thought of by Me, 'tis necessarily True

So what is this "I" that can't be doubted away? Not a body, since the deceiver could fake that. Descartes concludes he is a thinking thing: something that doubts, understands, wants, refuses, imagines and senses.

ExtendedThe famous slogan "I think, therefore I am" doesn't appear in the Meditations in those words. It appears in French in Descartes's Discourse on the Method (1637) and in Latin, cogito, ergo sum, in his Principles of Philosophy (1644).

The modern deceiver

Philosophers have since swapped the evil spirit for something more modern. Picture your brain kept alive in a vat of nutrients, wired to a computer that feeds it perfect experiences of a life. Every test you could run would come back looking normal. Movies have made versions of this idea famous.

Hilary Putnam made the "brain in a vat" a classic in 1981, and then gave it a twist. He argued that a brain that had only ever lived in the vat couldn't even think "I'm a brain in a vat" and be right. Its word "vat" would be about the images the computer feeds it, not about real vats.

ExtendedIn 2003 Nick Bostrom published "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", arguing that if advanced civilizations run many detailed simulations of minds like ours, it could be likely that we are in one. Many philosophers dispute the premises, and the debate is lively.

Here is one hand

In 1939 the English philosopher G. E. Moore gave a lecture in London that included a famous gesture. He held up his hands and said, in effect, "Here is one hand, and here is another." So there are at least two things outside his mind, and the skeptic is wrong.

In that lecture Moore admitted he couldn't prove he wasn't dreaming, but said he could know things he couldn't prove. In later papers he sharpened the point: he was more certain he had hands than he was of any clever premise in a skeptic's argument. If an argument ends with "you don't know you have hands," something in the argument must be broken.

Critics answer that Moore just assumed what was in question. The skeptic asked how he knows he isn't dreaming his hands, and showing them doesn't answer that. Defenders reply that it is perfectly reasonable to trust your firmest beliefs over a clever puzzle. Philosophers still argue about who wins.

Carry this with you

Descartes didn't stay in the deep water. In the later Meditations he argues that a perfect God exists and would not deceive him, so whatever he perceives very clearly and distinctly must be true. Few philosophers today accept that way back out.

Most don't think you need to defeat the skeptic before you can know anything. They disagree about why. But doubt remains one of the sharpest tools philosophy has: push hard on a belief and see what survives.

Next up is the game, the Deceiver's Ladder. Each belief faces three levels of doubt. Your job is to find the weakest doubt that knocks it out, or to spot the beliefs nothing can touch.
