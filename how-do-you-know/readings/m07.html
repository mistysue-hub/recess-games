What Is Truth, Anyway?

Tap the arrows, the page corner, or swipe to turn the page.

"That's true for me"

You've probably heard someone end an argument with "Well, that's true for me." Sometimes it's a fair point: "pineapple on pizza is delicious" really might be a matter of taste. Other times it sounds strange: is "the Earth is round" only true for some people?

Every lock so far has leaned on truth. In Lock 1, knowledge needed a belief that was true. Justification, testimony and induction are all ways of trying to get to the truth. So what is it we're trying to get to?

This lock looks at the main answers, and at a few sentences that seem to break the whole idea.

Saying it like it is

Aristotle gave the oldest and simplest answer, here in W. D. Ross's 1908 translation of his Metaphysics:

To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not, is true.

This became the correspondence theory: a belief or sentence is true when it matches the way things are. "The cat is on the mat" is true if, and only if, the cat is actually on the mat. A true belief is like an accurate map.

The worries come from the hard cases. What fact does "7 + 5 = 12" match? Or "cruelty for fun is wrong"? Or a claim about the future, which hasn't happened yet? Correspondence theorists have answers, but each one takes work.

Truth as fit

The coherence theory says a belief is true when it fits into a whole consistent system of beliefs, with no contradictions and lots of mutual support. Some idealist philosophers of the late 1800s and early 1900s held views like this.

This is a different question from Lock 4. There, coherence was a theory of justification: what makes you reasonable to believe something. Here, it's a theory of truth: what makes the belief right.

The big problem is the one from Lock 4, with higher stakes. Two different fantasy worlds could each be perfectly coherent and contradict each other. If truth is just coherence, are they both true?

Truth that works

In the United States around 1900, the pragmatists asked a practical question: what difference does it make whether a belief is true?

Charles Sanders Peirce, in 1878, said truth is where honest inquiry would end up: "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate." William James went further in 1907. He wrote that the true is "only the expedient in the way of our thinking": the ideas that hold up, guide us well and keep working as we test them.

Critics pounced on James. Lots of false beliefs are useful, and some true ones are useless or painful. Isn't a belief true because of how things are, not because believing it pays off? James's defenders say he meant something richer than "useful": ideas that survive verification in the long run.

ExtendedIn 1933 the Polish logician Alfred Tarski gave a precise rule any theory of truth must meet, often stated as: "Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white. Some later philosophers, called deflationists, argue that's nearly all there is to say about truth.

Man is the measure

Long before any of these, the Greek thinker Protagoras made the most radical claim. In Benjamin Jowett's translation of Plato's Theaetetus:

Man, he says, is the measure of all things, of the existence of things that are, and of the non-existence of things that are not

Plato took this to mean that whatever seems true to each person is true for that person. There's no truth beyond how things seem to each of us. That's relativism about truth.

Key term

Relativism about truth: the view that truth isn't the same for everyone; a claim can be true for one person and false for another.

The trap Protagoras sets for himself

Plato's Socrates found a trap built into the view. If every person's opinion is true for them, then the many people who think Protagoras is wrong are also right. In the dialogue Socrates says:

he acknowledges the truth of their opinion who believe his own opinion to be false

This is called self-refutation: a claim that undermines itself. "There is no truth" has the same problem. If it's true, there's at least one truth, so it's false.

Not every "true for me" is a trap. Matters of taste, or facts that depend on us, like what money is worth or what the rules of a game are, can vary between people or places. The trap springs only when every truth is said to be relative, including that one.

ExtendedSome modern relativists limit their view to particular areas, such as taste or etiquette, precisely to avoid Plato's argument. Whether moral truths belong in that list is one of philosophy's biggest open questions.

The liar

Some sentences make trouble for every theory at once. Around 2,400 years ago the Greek philosopher Eubulides asked about a man who says, "What I am saying now is a lie."

Try the modern version: "This sentence is false." If it's true, then what it says is right, so it's false. If it's false, then what it says is wrong, so it's true. It seems to be true exactly when it's false.

This is the liar paradox. It isn't just a party trick. It shaped modern logic: Tarski's work on truth was partly an attempt to keep paradoxes like it from wrecking formal languages. Philosophers still disagree about the best way out.

Carry this with you

Whatever truth turns out to be, the ideas in this course need it. Knowledge needs it, doubt aims at it, evidence points toward it, and testimony passes it along. You can believe that some things depend on taste or culture and still hold that "the Earth is round" isn't one of them. And the next time someone says "there's no such thing as truth," you'll know to ask: is that true?
