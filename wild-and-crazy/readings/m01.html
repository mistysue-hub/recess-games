Set Card 1
What If There Were No Punchlines?

Heads up: This course teaches comedy by studying Steve Martin's career and what he's said in public about his work. He isn't involved, and nothing here is copied from his books or albums.

The man who wouldn't finish the joke

In the 1970s a comedian in a white suit walked on stage, tuned his banjo for far too long, announced a trick that never arrived, and said absurd things with a perfectly straight face, then moved on as if nothing had happened. Rooms didn't know what to do. Then they laughed, harder than they expected, and couldn't say why.

That was the plan.

The question behind it

Every joke runs on one machine: a setup builds pressure, a punchline pops it, the pressure escapes as a laugh. Steve learned that in a college psychology class and asked a strange question: what if you build the pressure and never pop it? No punchline, no wink, no pause that says "laugh now." The audience has to pick its own moment, and a laugh you choose feels stronger than one you were told to have.

Tonight's clip: King Tut

In 1978 he brought a silly song to Saturday Night Live. There is no joke to "get." There is a man committing completely to something ridiculous and refusing to blink. Watch where you laugh.

Your turn

Write one joke twice: the normal way, with a punchline, then Steve's way, with the punchline taken out and something flat put in its place. Both go to Rocky.

Backstage

Steve did stand-up for about eighteen years and quit in 1981 at the top. The no-punchline idea played to near-empty rooms for years before it filled arenas. Where this went: Andy Kaufman, Norm Macdonald's famously long moth joke, and most anti-comedy you've seen online all start here.
