Set Card 3
The Confident Fool

Things from the trousers

In 1992, in the final weeks of Johnny Carson's Tonight Show, Carson asked Steve to do a bit he usually saved for parties. Steve comes out as a magician in a tuxedo, says nothing, and produces things from the fly of his trousers: eggs, a scarf, bubbles, a ringing telephone, and finally a tiny puppet of the opera singer Pavarotti, who sings. The magician believes this is the finest magic ever performed. That belief is the whole act.

The man in the white suit

That's the character Steve played for years: a man certain he is brilliant, slick and in control, and wrong about all three. Arrow through the head, bunny ears, "Excuse me!" like he's been deeply wronged. He called himself a wild and crazy guy and then did nothing wild or crazy.

An unsure fool is just sad. A confident fool is funny, because the gap between how he sees himself and how we see him is the joke. The character can never know. The second he winks, it's over.

Who the laugh lands on

All the foolishness was pointed at the man in the suit, who was happy to take it. He never needed a target in the front row. Comedy that points at a character you built, especially one that looks like you, is harder than pointing at someone who can't answer back, and it lasts longer.

Your turn

Write about six lines for a character who is one hundred percent confident and one hundred percent wrong. A chef who can't cook. A tour guide who's lost. They never, ever realize.

Backstage

Where this went: Nathan Fielder's whole career is a confident fool who never winks, and the question of who the laugh lands on is the argument people have about his shows.
