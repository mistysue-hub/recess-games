Your Five Minutes

Walking away at the top

In 1981 Steve Martin was the biggest stand-up comedian in the world, selling out arenas, and he stopped. Not for a break. For good. He has said the act had become something he could do in his sleep, the audience was laughing at the catchphrases before he said them, and that's not real laughter. He didn't want to perform a copy of himself. So he quit, and went to make movies.

It looked like an ending. It was the start of four or five more careers: actor, screenwriter, novelist, playwright, banjo player with real Grammys, and most recently a TV detective in Only Murders in the Building, alongside his best friend.

The friend

Steve met Martin Short on the set of Three Amigos in 1985. They've been close ever since. On stage they roast each other constantly, each pretending the other is washed up, and the audience howls, because every insult is obviously a love letter. Short has said the friendship is the whole basis of the act. Steve has said having a friend makes all the difference.

There's a kind of comedy that only works between people who trust each other, and it might be the best kind. You can tease someone exactly as hard as they know you love them.

What the whole course was about

Look back at the set cards. No punchlines. Empty rooms. The confident fool. Holding the tension. High and low. Every word. The notebook. Underneath all of it is one idea: comedy is not a gift that lands on you. It's a craft, built by someone who decided to keep showing up, keep cutting, keep noticing, and stay kind about who the joke is on.

You've now written a no-punchline joke, an opener, a confident fool, a tension bit, a juxtaposition, a cut-down joke, and a mini set with a callback. That's a five-minute set, hiding in pieces.
