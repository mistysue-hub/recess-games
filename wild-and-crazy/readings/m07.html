The Notebook

The show after the show

Every night for years, after Steve walked off stage, the work started. What got a laugh, what got silence, where the pause ran a half-second long, which bit should move earlier. He changed one thing. Then he did it again the next night.

Not because he was unsure of himself. Because he'd decided his own opinion of the show mattered less than what the show did in the room. The room is the data. The notebook is where you keep it.

Notes, not verdicts

When a joke doesn't land, most people hear "I'm not funny" or "they didn't get it." Both are useless. A writer hears a third thing: that beat didn't work; what, exactly, happened? Was the setup long? Did they laugh in the wrong place? That's a note. Notes are fixable. Verdicts aren't.

His friend Martin Short says Steve has the louder inner critic of the two of them. You need the critic; it's what makes the second draft better. But it works for you. It makes notes. It doesn't decide whether you go on tomorrow.

Building a set

A set has an opener (short, confident, tells the room who you are), a build (bits that get a little bigger), and a callback near the end: a line that brings back the opener so the whole thing feels planned. It was.

Tonight's clip: the acceptance speech

Steve accepting the Mark Twain Prize for American Humor in 2005. A tiny set: short opener, a list with a twist on every item, a callback. Watch how little he does.

Your turn

Write a three-bit mini set: opener, a bigger second bit, a closer that calls back the opener. Then one note, like Steve's: the thing you'd fix next time.
