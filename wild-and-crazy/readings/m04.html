Hold It... Hold It...

The balloon

Think of tension as a balloon. Each beat of a bit (one line, one look, one pause) adds a little air. A punchline is a pin. Steve's idea was to keep adding air and never bring out the pin, so the audience is squirming, grinning, waiting, and the laugh leaks out around the edges.

One of his old bits: announce a song, start tuning the banjo, and keep tuning. Past the point where it was a joke, through the point where it was annoying, out the other side into something else. The room would laugh, stop, get uncomfortable, and laugh harder, at nothing, because nothing was still happening.

Silence is a tool

Beginners rush. A quiet room feels like failure, so they fill it. Steve let the quiet stretch until the quiet was the material: a long stare, a promise never kept, a story that circles the point and never lands.

Counting beats

Writers count beats. A tension bit might go: promise (1), delay (2), delay with a tiny change (3), almost (4), nope (5). Every beat has to add a half-inch more air. The same line five times isn't tension; it's a loop. The hardest skill is knowing which beat to walk off on.

Tonight's clip: the song that never gets sung

Steve, in a forest with a guitar, wants to sing to Stephen Colbert. Colbert keeps stopping him. Each try gets a little further. It never happens. Count the beats.

Your turn

Write a bit where the tension builds for five beats and the release never comes. Then decide which beat you'd walk off on.
