Set Card 4
Hold It... Hold It...

The longest banjo tuning in history

One of Steve's old bits went like this: he'd announce a song, start tuning the banjo, and keep tuning. And keep tuning. Past the point where it was a joke, through the point where it was annoying, out the other side into something else. The audience would laugh, stop, get uncomfortable, and then laugh harder than before, at nothing, because nothing was still happening.

That's tension. In Set Card 1 you learned his theory that if you never release tension with a punchline, the audience has to release it themselves. Tonight is about building the tension on purpose.

Silence is a tool

Most beginning comedians are terrified of silence. They rush. They fill every gap with words because a quiet room feels like failure. Steve did the opposite: he let the quiet stretch until the quiet itself became the material. A long stare. A promise that never gets kept. A story that circles the point and never lands on it.

Think of tension as a balloon. Each beat (each line, each pause) adds a little air. A punchline is a pin. Steve's idea is to keep adding air and never bring out the pin, so the audience is squirming, grinning, waiting, and the laugh leaks out around the edges.

Counting beats

A beat is the smallest unit of a bit: one line, one look, one pause. Writers count them. A good tension bit might go: promise (1), delay (2), delay with a tiny change (3), almost (4), nope (5). The trick is that every beat has to add something, even if it's only a half-inch more air. Repeating the exact same line five times isn't tension; it's a loop. The fifth "and keep tuning" has to be slightly different from the fourth.

Tonight's clip: the song that never gets sung

In 2020, with everyone stuck at home, Steve appeared on The Late Show with Stephen Colbert from a forest with a guitar, ready to sing. Colbert does not want him to sing. Steve keeps trying. Colbert keeps stopping him. Every beat he gets a little closer and is cut off again. There is no punchline; there is a man who wants to sing and is never allowed to. Watch how the beats change: each attempt is slightly different, and the frustration builds in a way that is also, somehow, warm. Two friends, one of them denying the other a tiny joy, both of them delighted.

Your turn

Tonight you write a bit where the tension builds for five beats and the release never comes. Someone about to reveal a secret. A waiter about to bring the food. A magician about to do the trick. Five beats, each one a little more air, and then you walk off stage.
