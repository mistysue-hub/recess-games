Set Card 6
Every Word Counts

The comedian who was really a writer

People who know Steve Martin say that off stage he's quiet, and that at heart he's a writer. He started as one, writing jokes for TV at 22. He wrote his own stand-up word by word. He wrote the movies he starred in, including The Jerk and Roxanne, where he turned a 100-year-old French play into a story about a small-town fire chief with a huge nose. He wrote plays, novels, and funny pieces for The New Yorker. And in 2020 he started writing cartoons with the cartoonist Harry Bliss.

Someone who worked with him on a documentary passed along something his wife said: if you ask Steve to say a few words at an event, you think you're asking for five minutes. You're actually asking for a week, because he'll spend a week on those five minutes.

Why a cartoon is the hardest joke

A cartoon has one picture and one line. There is nowhere to hide. If the line has one extra word, the joke sags. If it's missing one word, nobody gets it. Steve has talked about the precision this demands, and about waking up in the middle of the night to write down an idea before it vanishes.

That's the rule for tonight: every word in a joke has to earn its place. Comedy is the one kind of writing where shorter is almost always funnier, because the laugh is a surprise, and surprises don't survive padding.

Diligence, not lightning

Steve has joked that writer's block is a fancy name people invent for not sitting down and working. He's not being mean about it; he's describing his own method. Ideas don't strike him. He shows up, writes a lot of bad versions, and cuts. The funny version is usually hiding inside the long version, waiting for someone to remove everything around it.

How to cut

Read your joke out loud. Find every word that explains the joke instead of being the joke. Find every "very," "really," "basically," and "like." Find the setup that says the same thing twice. Cut them. Then read it again. If it's still funny, cut more. Stop one word before it stops making sense.

Tonight's clip: the concise joy of cartoons

This is a CBS Sunday Morning segment from 2020. Mo Rocca sits with Steve and Harry Bliss and they talk about their cartoon collection, the precision a single line demands, and where ideas come from. Watch for how Steve talks about the work: not as inspiration, as craft.
