Step 2 of 6 · Set Card

A kid with a stack of maps

Before anyone had heard of him, Steve Martin, about ten years old, sold guidebooks at Disneyland to people who were already lost. As a teenager he worked the counter at the park's magic shop, doing the same tricks a hundred times a day for strangers who mostly wandered off. At Knott's Berry Farm's little Bird Cage Theatre he acted in old-time melodramas and comedy skits, about twenty-five shows a week, for whoever sat down.

That was his comedy school: not a class, a counter. Thousands of tiny audiences, most of them unimpressed.

The years that weren't a montage

He got a good job writing jokes for TV, shared an Emmy with the writing team, and quit to perform his own act in clubs. His writing agent told him to stick to writing. He took that as encouragement. What followed was years of small rooms, late slots, and audiences that didn't laugh, followed by a drive to the next town to do it again.

What he did with those rooms: he kept notebooks on his act, even as a teenager, and kept adjusting it. He treated each audience as information, not a verdict.

Asked later how to make it as a performer, he gave the same answer every time: be so good they can't ignore you. (His words, on the Charlie Rose show, 2007.)

Backstage (optional)

The TV job was The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour; he was 21, and shared an Emmy at 23. Where this went: every comedian you like has a version of these years. Most just don't talk about them.
