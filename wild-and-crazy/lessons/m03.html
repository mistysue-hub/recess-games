The Confident Fool

Step 1 of 6

Tonight's question

In Steve's act, who was the joke on: the audience or himself?

Himself — or really, the character he played. A man certain he was brilliant, slick, and in control, and wrong about all three. The performer was in on it; the character never found out. That gap is the joke.
Step 2 of 6 · Set Card

Things from the trousers

In 1992, in the final weeks of Johnny Carson's Tonight Show, Carson talked Steve into an old favorite. Steve comes out as a magician in a tuxedo, says nothing, and produces things from the fly of his trousers: a handkerchief, eggs, a ringing telephone, a glass of wine, a tiny puppet of the opera singer Pavarotti, who sings, and, for an encore, bubbles. The magician believes this is the finest magic ever performed. That belief is the whole act.

The man in the white suit

That's the character Steve played for years: a man certain he is brilliant, slick and in control, and wrong about all three. Arrow through the head, bunny ears, "Excuse me!" like he's been deeply wronged. He called himself a wild and crazy guy, but in the character's mind, nothing was wild at all — it was all perfectly reasonable.

An unsure fool is just sad. A confident fool is funny, because the gap between how he sees himself and how we see him is the joke. The performer is in on it; the character never finds out. That gap is the joke. The second the character winks (shows he knows he's being silly), it's over.

Who the laugh lands on

All the foolishness was pointed at the man in the suit, who was happy to take it. He never needed a target in the front row. Comedy that points at a character you built, especially one that looks like you, is harder than pointing at someone who can't answer back, and it lasts longer.

Backstage (optional)

You can see something similar in Nathan Fielder (Nathan for You, The Rehearsal): a confident fool who never winks, and the question of who the laugh lands on is the argument people have about his shows.

Step 5 of 6 · Your Turn

Pick your character

Pick a character who is totally confident and totally wrong. A coach who has never won. A pilot who's afraid of heights. A lifeguard who can't swim.

Write the monologue

Write about six lines in that character's voice. They never, ever realize they're wrong.

Stuck? Ask yourself: what would this character brag about? Start there.

Need a spark? Tap for an example
