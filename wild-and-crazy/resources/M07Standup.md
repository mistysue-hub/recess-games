# Set Card 7: The Notebook

## The show after the show

Every night for eighteen years, after Steve Martin walked off stage, the work started. He went over the set: what got a laugh, what got silence, where the pause was a half-second too long, which bit should move earlier. He changed one thing. Then he did it again the next night.

He wasn't doing this because he was unsure of himself. He was doing it because he had decided that his own opinion of the show mattered less than what the show actually did in the room. The room is the data. The notebook is where you keep it.

## The loud inner critic

His friend Martin Short has said, in a joint interview, that of the two of them, Steve has the louder inner critic: he tends to dwell on the performances that went badly. Short, on the other hand, learned early on to let go of his reactions and move on. They've been close friends for almost forty years, and they're still different that way.

There's a lesson in the pair of them. You need the critic: it's what makes the second draft better than the first. But the critic works for you, not the other way around. It gets to make notes. It doesn't get to decide whether you go on tomorrow.

## Feedback without crumbling

When a joke doesn't land, most people hear one of two things: "I'm not funny," or "they didn't get it." Both are useless. A writer hears a third thing: "that beat didn't work; what, exactly, happened?" Was the setup too long? Did they laugh in the wrong place? Did a word trip them? That question is a note. Notes are fixable. Verdicts aren't.

## Building a set

A set is more than a pile of jokes. It has an opener (short, confident, tells the room who you are), a build (bits that get a little bigger), and, if you can manage it, a callback near the end: a line that brings back something from the opener so the audience feels the whole thing was planned. It was. Steve's sets ended on callbacks all the time; the last bit would quietly reuse the first.

## Tonight's clip: the acceptance speech

In 2005 the Kennedy Center gave Steve the Mark Twain Prize for American Humor, the biggest award in American comedy. His acceptance speech is a tiny set: a short opener, a list of other awards he's won with a twist at the end of each, a callback. Watch for how little he does. He never rushes. Every laugh is earned by structure, not volume.

## Your turn

Tonight you write a three-bit mini set: an opener, a second bit that's a little bigger, and a closer with a callback to the opener. Then, like Steve, you write one note: the one thing you'd fix next time.
