# Set Card 3: The Confident Fool

## The man in the white suit

By the late 1970s Steve Martin walked on stage in a white three-piece suit, sometimes with a fake arrow through his head, sometimes with bunny ears, and announced himself as a very serious, very professional entertainer. He played the banjo badly on purpose. He did magic tricks that didn't work. He said "Excuse me!" like he'd been deeply wronged. He called himself a wild and crazy guy and then did nothing wild or crazy.

The character was a man who is absolutely certain he is brilliant, slick, and in control, and who is wrong about all three. The audience was never laughing at Steve. They were laughing at the guy in the suit, who was, in a way, a different person.

## Why the fool has to be confident

An unsure fool is just sad. A confident fool is funny, because the gap between how he sees himself and how we see him is the joke, and the wider the gap, the bigger the laugh. The character can't know he's the joke. The second he winks, it's over.

That's a writing rule you can use tonight: give your character total certainty, then make the world disagree with him.

## Who the laugh lands on

Here's the heartwarming part, and it's also the craft part. Steve's character was the butt of his own jokes. He didn't need to make fun of a person in the front row, or a group of people, or anyone who couldn't answer back. All the foolishness was pointed at the man in the white suit, who was happy to take it.

Comedy that points down at real people is easy and gets a cheap laugh. Comedy that points at a character you built, especially one that looks a lot like you, is harder and lasts. It's also a lot kinder to be around.

## Tonight's clip: The Great Flydini

In 1992, in the final weeks of Johnny Carson's Tonight Show, Carson asked Steve to perform a bit he usually saved for parties. Steve comes out as a magician in a tuxedo, says nothing, and produces things from the fly of his trousers: eggs, a scarf, bubbles, a ringing telephone, and finally a tiny puppet of the opera singer Pavarotti, who sings. It's silly in the most committed way possible. Watch his face. The magician believes this is the finest magic ever performed. That belief is the whole act.
