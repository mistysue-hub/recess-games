# Set Card 1: What If There Were No Punchlines?

**A note before we start.** This course was written to teach comedy by studying Steve Martin's career and the things he has said in public about his work. He is not involved in it, and nothing here is copied from his books or albums.

## The rule every joke follows

Picture the oldest joke shape in the world. A setup builds a little pressure: "A man walks into a shop and asks for a..." Then the punchline pops it, and the pressure escapes as a laugh. Setup, tension, release. That's the machine.

Steve Martin learned that machine in a college psychology class in the 1960s. Comedy books explained it the same way: tension, then a punchline to let it out. Most comedians still build everything on it.

## The question that changed his act

Steve looked at the machine and asked a strange question. What if a comedian built up the tension and then just... never released it? No punchline. No wink. No pause that says "laugh now." What would the audience do with all that pressure?

His theory: the tension has to come out somewhere. If you never hand people a punchline, they eventually pick their own moment to laugh, almost out of desperation. And a laugh you choose yourself feels stronger than a laugh you were told to have. He wanted people to laugh and then be unable to explain what, exactly, had been funny.

So he built an act that was all setup. He'd tune a banjo for far too long. He'd announce a trick that never arrived. He'd say something absurd with a completely straight face and move right on, as if it were an aside, while the audience scrambled to catch up.

## It did not work at first

This is the part people skip. For years, the no-punchline act played to nearly empty rooms. Audiences did not know what they were looking at. He kept doing it anyway, show after show, until by the late 1970s he was selling out arenas, the biggest draw in the history of stand-up. We'll come back to those empty rooms in Module 2.

## Tonight's clip: King Tut

In 1978 he brought a silly song to Saturday Night Live. He stepped out in a gold headdress and asked to speak seriously, for just a moment, about an art exhibit. Then he sang. Watch for how there is no joke to "get." There is only a man committing completely to something ridiculous and refusing to blink. The audience has to decide, on its own, when the laughing starts.
