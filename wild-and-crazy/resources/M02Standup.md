# Set Card 2: The Ten Years Nobody Came

## A ten-year-old in a magic shop

Before anyone had heard of him, Steve Martin was a kid in Southern California who rode his bike to Disneyland to sell guidebooks. By his teens he was working the counter at the park's magic shop, demonstrating tricks a hundred times a day for strangers who mostly wandered off. Across town at Knott's Berry Farm he did a little stage show, four times a day, in a tiny theater, to whoever sat down.

That was his comedy school: not a class, a counter. Thousands of tiny audiences, most of them unimpressed.

## The job everyone told him to keep

At 22 he landed a job writing jokes for The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour, a big network show. At 23 he shared an Emmy for it. Then he told his agents he was quitting television writing to go perform his own act in clubs. They told him not to. He went anyway.

What followed was not a montage. It was years. Small rooms, late slots, opening for bands whose fans wanted the band. The no-punchline act from Set Card 1 confused people. He has described playing to audiences that didn't laugh, and then driving to the next town to do it again.

## What he did with the empty rooms

He kept notes. After shows he wrote down what landed and what didn't, and he changed small things, one at a time. He treated every tiny audience as information, not as a verdict on whether he was funny.

Years later, asked how to make it in show business, he gave the same answer every time, and he's said nobody likes hearing it because they want a shortcut: be so good they can't ignore you. (That one is a direct quote; he said it on the Charlie Rose show in 2007.) His point: if you're busy becoming great at the thing, people eventually come to you. If you're busy chasing people, you never get great at the thing.

## Tonight's clip: the magic act

This is Steve on The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour, his first TV job, doing a magic act. Watch it knowing what you know now: this is the magic-shop kid, a decade in, still doing tricks for a room, except the tricks are the joke. Watch for how he keeps going when a trick "fails." The failure is the act.

## Why this matters for your writing

Your first jokes will not be good. Steve's weren't. The difference between people who get funny and people who don't isn't talent on day one; it's whether they write the next one anyway, and whether they keep notes on what happened.
