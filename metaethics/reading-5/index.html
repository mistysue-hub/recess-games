Is "Wrong" Just "Boo!"?

Is Anything Really Wrong? · The Philosopher's Path, reading 5 of 6

I

Card 1: A different kind of answer

So far, almost everyone you've met agrees on one thing. Realists think moral claims state facts and some are true. Error theorists think moral claims try to state facts but always fail. Relativists think moral claims state facts about a group's standards.

Today's view breaks from all of them. Expressivists think moral claims aren't trying to state facts at all. When you say "cheating is wrong," you're not describing the world. You're doing something more like cheering or booing.

II

Card 2: Ayer's boo

In 1936, a young British philosopher named A. J. Ayer published a short, bold book called Language, Truth and Logic. He thought a sentence only states a fact if experience could, in principle, show it to be true or false. Moral sentences fail that test.

His example, in this course's words: if I say "You were wrong to steal that money," I've told you nothing more than "You stole that money." The word "wrong" adds no new fact. It's as if I'd said "You stole that money!" in a tone of horror, or written it with special exclamation marks.

Critics nicknamed this the "boo-hurrah theory." "Stealing is wrong" means roughly "Stealing: boo!" "Helping people is good" means "Helping people: hooray!" Ayer's view is called emotivism, because moral words express emotion.

Careful: "Stealing: boo!" isn't the same as "I disapprove of stealing." The second one is a report about you, which could be true or false. Ayer's boo isn't a report at all; it's the booing itself.

III

Card 3: Why it's tempting

Emotivism explains some things very neatly.

Moral talk moves us. If you really think lying is wrong, you're usually against lying, not just informed about it. If moral claims express attitudes, that's no surprise.

No strange facts needed. Remember Mackie's worry that moral facts would be too strange to exist? Expressivists agree there are no such facts, but say moral talk was never trying to describe them, so it isn't full of errors.

Disagreement makes sense. When two people argue about whether something is wrong, it can feel like rival fans shouting for different teams. Attitudes clash, and argument can still change them.

IV

Card 4: From cheering to planning

Modern expressivists have built far more careful versions than Ayer's. The philosopher Allan Gibbard, for example, suggested (in this course's words) that calling something wrong is less like cheering and more like making a plan: "don't do that, in any situation like this, whoever I am."

Plans can be compared and argued about. They can be consistent or inconsistent: you can't plan both to go to the party and to skip it. That gives expressivists a way to explain why moral reasoning feels like real reasoning, even if there are no moral facts underneath.

V

Card 5: The if-then puzzle

In 1965, the philosopher Peter Geach raised a problem that expressivists still argue about. It's often called the Frege-Geach problem. Here's a simple version.

  1. If cheating is wrong, then helping someone cheat is wrong.
  2. Cheating is wrong.
  3. So helping someone cheat is wrong.

That argument obviously works. But look at line 1. When you say "if cheating is wrong," you aren't booing cheating. You might not even think it's wrong; you're just saying what would follow if it were.

So on the boo theory, "cheating is wrong" means "boo!" in line 2 but means something else in line 1. And an argument that uses the same words with two different meanings usually doesn't work, like "Banks hold money. Rivers have banks. So rivers hold money." Yet this one clearly does.

The same trouble shows up outside if-then sentences. A question ("Is cheating wrong?") and a doubt ("I'm not sure white lies are wrong") both use "wrong," but neither one is booing anything.

The expressivist's reply: line 1 expresses a commitment: you can't boo cheating without also booing helping someone cheat. Accept line 1 and line 2, and you're committed to line 3. Whether that fully solves the puzzle is still debated.

VI

Card 6: Does wrongness depend on us?

Here's another worry, raised by the philosopher Nick Zangwill among others. Bullying is wrong because of what it does to the person being bullied, not because people disapprove of it. If everyone somehow stopped disapproving, bullying would still be wrong.

Expressivism can sound as if wrongness depends on our attitudes. Expressivists reply: when I say "bullying would still be wrong even if nobody disapproved," I'm expressing an attitude too. I boo bullying even in imagined worlds where nobody else does. Critics wonder whether that answers the worry or just restates it.

VII

Card 7: A puzzle that helps the expressivist

Try saying this: "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." It sounds absurd, even though it could be true.

Now try this: "Lying is wrong, but I don't disapprove of lying at all." That sounds strange in a similar way.

An expressivist can explain why. If "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval, then the sentence expresses disapproval and denies it in the same breath. Some philosophers count this as a point in expressivism's favour; others think realists can explain the strangeness just as well.

VIII

Card 8: Real-world spark:Emoji ethics

Emoji reactions are Ayer's theory in action. A 👍 or a 👎 doesn't state a fact. You can't say "that thumbs-down is false." It just shows how someone feels.

So try this. Is "Cheating is wrong" really just "Cheating 👎"?

Now try the if-then version: "If cheating is 👎, then helping someone cheat is 👎." Does that even mean anything? You've just run into Geach's puzzle using a keyboard.

IX

Card 9: What to carry into the game

  • Emotivism (Ayer): moral claims express feelings, like "boo" and "hooray."
  • Expressivism (Gibbard and others): moral claims express attitudes or plans, not facts.
  • Strengths: explains why moral talk moves us; needs no strange facts.
  • The if-then puzzle: in "if cheating is wrong..." nobody is booing, yet the argument works.
  • Does wrongness depend on us? Expressivists say their view doesn't make it so.
