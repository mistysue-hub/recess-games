If Morality Is Real, Why Do We Disagree?

Is Anything Really Wrong? · The Philosopher's Path, reading 3 of 6

Follow the owl down the path. Read every card to the violet one at the end, then tell Rocky you're done.

I

Card 1: Two puzzles about moral facts

Last time you met people who think moral facts are real, whether natural or not. Today you'll meet the strongest arguments against them.

There are two. The first asks whether we ever actually need moral facts to explain anything. The second asks why, if moral facts are real, people disagree about them so much, and what strange things moral facts would have to be.

Part 1Do we see wrongness?
II

Card 2: Harman's challenge

Picture this. In the hallway, a senior knocks a freshman's books out of their hands and laughs while everyone watches. Instantly, without thinking, you know: that's wrong.

The philosopher Gilbert Harman asked: what explains that reaction?

Compare a scientist who sees a trail of vapour in a cloud chamber (a box of cold mist where speeding particles leave trails, like tiny jet contrails) and thinks there goes a proton. To explain why she sees that trail, we need to mention the proton. The particle really is part of the best explanation of what she observed.

But in the hallway case, Harman said, you don't seem to need a moral fact. Your reaction can be fully explained by your upbringing, your feelings, and what you believe about bullying. Whether or not the bullying is really wrong in some deeper sense, you'd react the same way. If moral facts never show up in the best explanation of anything, Harman asked, why think they exist?

III

Card 3: Sturgeon's reply

Nicholas Sturgeon disagreed. He pointed out that we explain things with moral facts all the time, and the explanations seem to work.

Suppose a team keeps losing its best players, and everyone eventually discovers the coach was cruel to them in private. What's the best explanation for the players leaving? Plausibly: the coach was cruel. Take away the cruelty and the explanation falls apart. Or suppose a historian asks why so many people turned against a particular ruler. Often the best answer is: because the ruler was unjust, and people came to see it.

Sturgeon's point: if a real moral feature (cruelty, injustice) helps explain what happens, that's evidence moral facts are real, just as the proton's role is evidence protons are real.

Harman's side replies that "cruel" in these stories might just be shorthand for the natural facts underneath (what the coach said and did), with no extra moral fact needed. You'll get to judge this one yourself.

Sturgeon, a naturalist like the ones you met in Session 2, would answer: fine, then moral facts are natural facts. Cruelty is real, and it's made of exactly the things the coach said and did.

Part 2Disagreement and oddness
IV

Card 4: An old observation

Plato noticed something about disagreement long ago. In the same dialogue you read part of in Session 1, Socrates points out that some disagreements are easy to settle and some aren't:

SOCRATES: And what sort of difference creates enmity and anger? Suppose for example that you and I, my good friend, differ about a number; do differences of this sort make us enemies and set us at variance with one another? Do we not go at once to arithmetic, and put an end to them by a sum?

EUTHYPHRO: True.

SOCRATES: Or suppose that we differ about magnitudes, do we not quickly end the differences by measuring?

EUTHYPHRO: Very true.

Socrates goes on to say that the disagreements we can't settle this way are the ones about just and unjust, good and evil.

From Euthyphro by Plato, translated by Benjamin Jowett. V

Card 5: Mackie's first argument: too much disagreement

In 1977 the Australian philosopher J. L. Mackie turned that observation into an argument against moral facts.

Different societies, and different people within them, disagree deeply about morality, and these disagreements don't seem to shrink the way scientific disagreements do. Mackie thought the best explanation is that moral views come from people's ways of life, not from noticing real moral facts. His example was marriage customs: people approve of monogamy because they grew up in a society that practices it, he suggested, rather than practicing it because they discovered it's right.

The realist's reply: lots of disagreement is really about facts, not values. Two people might both believe "don't cause needless harm" and still disagree because they believe different things about what causes harm. Some practices also happen in very different situations; what looks like a clash of values can be the same value applied to different circumstances. And disagreement alone doesn't prove there's no truth: people disagreed about whether Earth goes around the Sun, too.

VI

Card 6: Mackie's second argument: moral facts would be strange

Mackie's second argument he called the argument from queerness, using an older meaning of "queer": odd or strange.

Think about what a moral fact would have to be like. Wrongness wouldn't just be a feature of an action, like being loud; it would somehow demand that you avoid the action, whatever you happen to want. It would have "must-not-be-done-ness" built right into it. Nothing else we know about works like that. Atoms don't command you. Numbers don't scold you.

And how would we ever detect such a thing? We'd need some special moral sense, unlike seeing, hearing, or reasoning. Mackie thought it's much simpler to conclude that there are no such strange facts.

The realist's reply: lots of real things are strange to us until we understand them (quantum physics, for a start), and our knowledge of logic and maths doesn't come through the five senses either. Philosophers like David Enoch argue that moral facts are no stranger than those.

VII

Card 7: Error theory

So where does Mackie end up? He agreed that when people say "stealing is wrong," they're trying to state a fact. He just thought there are no moral facts for them to get right. So every moral claim is false, the way every claim about a ghost's favourite colour is false. This view is called error theory: ordinary moral talk rests on an error.

That sounds alarming, but Mackie wasn't telling people to start stealing. He thought we should keep making moral rules for good practical reasons; we just shouldn't think they're written into the universe.

Some philosophers, like Richard Joyce, suggest a variation called fictionalism: keep using moral talk the way you'd talk about a story you know isn't literally true, because the story is useful.

VIII

Card 8: Real-world spark:The comment-section test

Ever watched an argument in a comment section that goes on for 400 replies and never ends? Mackie would say: of course, moral views come from ways of life, so there's nothing to settle them.

But look closer at the next one you see. Often people are really fighting about facts (what happened in the video, what a rule actually says, what the person meant) and only sound like they're fighting about values. That's the realist's reply in real life.

Try it next time: ask "Do we disagree about what happened, or about what matters?" Sometimes the second argument disappears once the first one is settled.

IX

Card 9: What to carry into the game

  • Harman: we don't need moral facts to explain moral reactions.
  • Sturgeon: sometimes real moral features are the best explanation.
  • Mackie: there's too much disagreement, and moral facts would be too strange, so there are none (error theory).
  • Realists' replies: many disagreements are about facts, and strange doesn't mean unreal.
