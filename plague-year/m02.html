Florence, 1348: Boccaccio's Story

Heads up: today's reading is a real 14th-century description of a city during the plague. It describes the disease's signs, people abandoning the sick, and mass graves, in a few short lines with no gore. You can pause any time.

Who is telling this?

Giovanni Boccaccio was a Florentine writer. Some years after 1348 he finished the Decameron, a book of one hundred stories, many of them funny. It opens with the plague. Ten young people flee Florence and pass the time telling stories. Boccaccio wrote this description afterwards, and historians still debate whether he was in Florence while the plague raged. Read it as a powerful account by a writer, not a camera recording.

Words to know: pestilence = deadly epidemic · gavocciolo (plural gavoccioli) = a hard swelling, what doctors now call a bubo · livid = bluish-black · kinsfolk = relatives · divers = various.

Part 1 of 3: The plague arrives

I say, then, that the years of the beatific incarnation of the Son of God had reached the tale of one thousand three hundred and forty-eight when in the illustrious city of Florence, the fairest of all the cities of Italy, there made its appearance that deadly pestilence, which, whether disseminated by the influence of the celestial bodies, or sent upon us mortals by God in His just wrath by way of retribution for our iniquities, had had its origin some years before in the East, whence, after destroying an innumerable multitude of living beings, it had propagated itself without respite from place to place, and so, calamitously, had spread into the West.

In Florence, despite all that human wisdom and forethought could devise to avert it, as the cleansing of the city from many impurities by officials appointed for the purpose, the refusal of entrance to all sick folk, and the adoption of many precautions for the preservation of health; despite also humble supplications addressed to God, and often repeated both in public procession and otherwise, by the devout; towards the beginning of the spring of the said year the doleful effects of the pestilence began to be horribly apparent by symptoms that shewed as if miraculous.

[…] in men and women alike it first betrayed itself by the emergence of certain tumours in the groin or the armpits, some of which grew as large as a common apple, others as an egg, […] which the common folk called gavoccioli. […] black spots or livid making their appearance in many cases on the arm or the thigh or elsewhere […]. And as the gavocciolo had been and still was an infallible token of approaching death, such also were these spots on whomsoever they shewed themselves.

[…] marks words left out of this abridged reading.

Find it 1.Boccaccio offers two possible causes for the plague in his first sentence. What are they?
The stars, or God's anger at sin. "…whether disseminated by the influence of the celestial bodies, or sent upon us mortals by God in His just wrath…" Keep these in mind. You'll meet both theories again in Leaf III.

Part 2 of 3: Four ways to react

[…] divers apprehensions and imaginations were engendered in the minds of such as were left alive, inclining almost all of them to the same harsh resolution, to wit, to shun and abhor all contact with the sick and all that belonged to them, thinking thereby to make each his own health secure. Among whom there were those who thought that to live temperately and avoid all excess would count for much as a preservative against seizures of this kind. Wherefore they banded together, and, dissociating themselves from all others, formed communities in houses where there were no sick, and lived a separate and secluded life […], holding converse with none but one another, lest tidings of sickness or death should reach them, and diverting their minds with music and such other delights as they could devise.

Others, the bias of whose minds was in the opposite direction, maintained, that to drink freely, frequent places of public resort, and take their pleasure with song and revel, sparing to satisfy no appetite, and to laugh and mock at no event, was the sovereign remedy for so great an evil […].

Not a few there were who belonged to neither of the two said parties, but kept a middle course between them […]. They therefore walked abroad, carrying in their hands flowers or fragrant herbs or divers sorts of spices, which they frequently raised to their noses, deeming it an excellent thing thus to comfort the brain with such perfumes, because the air seemed to be everywhere laden and reeking with the stench emitted by the dead and the dying and the odours of drugs.

Some again, the most sound, perhaps, in judgment, as they [were] also the most harsh in temper, of all, affirmed that there was no medicine for the disease superior or equal in efficacy to flight; following which prescription a multitude of men and women, negligent of all but themselves, deserted their city, their houses, their estate, their kinsfolk, their goods, and went into voluntary exile, or migrated to the country parts […].

Find it 2.Name Boccaccio's four groups in your own words. Then look back: which group did your hook vote match?
1. Shut themselves away and lived moderately with music. 2. Partied: drank and revelled. 3. Took a middle course: walked about holding flowers, herbs or spices to their noses against the foul air. 4. Fled the city for the countryside. Whatever you voted, Boccaccio says Florentines did it. All four happened.

Part 3 of 3: What fear did to families

Of the adherents of these divers opinions not all died, neither did all escape […]. Tedious were it to recount, how citizen avoided citizen, how among neighbours was scarce found any that shewed fellow-feeling for another, how kinsfolk held aloof, and never met, or but rarely; enough that this sore affliction entered so deep into the minds of men and women, that in the horror thereof brother was forsaken by brother, nephew by uncle, brother by sister, and oftentimes husband by wife; nay, what is more, and scarcely to be believed, fathers and mothers were found to abandon their own children, untended, unvisited, to their fate, as if they had been strangers.

[…] they dug, for each graveyard, as soon as it was full, a huge trench, in which they laid the corpses as they arrived by hundreds at a time […] tier upon tier, each covered with a little earth, until the trench would hold no more.

Find it 3.According to Part 3, what happened to many of the sick, and what does Boccaccio say drove people to it?
They were abandoned, even by close family (brothers, husbands and wives, even parents), out of horror and fear of the disease: "this sore affliction entered so deep into the minds of men and women…" (Part 2 also says people shunned "all contact with the sick" to keep themselves safe.)

Florence, March 1348: your choices

You live in Florence. Make four choices. There are no right answers; after each one, Boccaccio tells you what people who chose that way did.

Choice 1 of 4: Your street has its first sick household.

Boccaccio's verdict on all four groups: "Of the adherents of these divers opinions not all died, neither did all escape." No choice was safe. He calls those who fled "the most sound, perhaps, in judgment," but also "the most harsh in temper," because they left their families behind.

Choice 2 of 4: Your brother falls sick.

Boccaccio says the sick were "left without resource but in the charity of friends (and few such there were), or the interest of servants, who were hardly to be had at high rates," and those servants "not seldom perished with their gains." Even paid help was scarce and dangerous. Keep the words "high rates" in mind for Leaf VII.

Choice 3 of 4: Your neighbour has died.

Before the plague, Boccaccio says, the dead were carried "with funeral pomp of taper and dirge, to the church selected by him before his death." Now neighbours dragged bodies to their doors, hired carriers took them "not to the church of the dead man's choice, but to that which was nearest at hand," and many were buried with no mourners at all. Old customs collapsed.

Choice 4 of 4: Seven young women you know are leaving for a country villa with three young men. They invite you.

This is the brigata (the group) of the Decameron. The young women meet by chance in the church of Santa Maria Novella. Pampinea, the eldest, argues that defending your own life is the "dictate of natural reason." They leave with three young men for the hills. There they live in an orderly way and tell stories, ten a day for ten days. It looks a lot like Boccaccio's first and fourth groups combined.

Talk-back.The Decameron is mostly lively, often funny stories. Why do you think Boccaccio opened it with the plague?

Boccaccio explains himself. The grim opening is "even such as to wayfarers is a steep and rugged mountain, beyond which stretches a plain most fair and delectable." Readers climb through the horror to reach the pleasure of the stories. He also says he needs it to explain why the storytellers left the city at all. This is an interpretation question: your reason is what counts, even if it differs from his.

Map stop.After Constantinople, merchant ships sailed west. Where do you think the plague made its first landfall in western Europe?
New stops: Messina in Sicily (October 1347), Marseille (November 1347), Genoa (winter 1347–48), Florence (March 1348). No score; it's a prediction.
Deeper seal (optional): Was Boccaccio there?

J. M. Rigg, who translated this text in 1903, declared it "certain" that Boccaccio was not in Florence during the plague. Many later scholars are less sure. He may have been there. Either way, he wrote the description afterwards, and it borrows the shape of famous older plague descriptions. Rigg also notes that Boccaccio's father was dead by 1350, probably of the plague.

So is the account unreliable? Rigg argued that Boccaccio could easily get details from eyewitnesses, and that plague towns across Italy looked much alike. A good historian reads it as both: a vivid literary picture and a source to check against others, like the town records you'll meet in Leaf III and Leaf VI.

Think about this: does it matter more whether Boccaccio saw it, or whether what he describes matches other evidence?
