I. The bacterium

Plague is caused by a bacterium, Yersinia pestis. It takes three main forms:

Bubonic: the bacteria reach the lymph nodes, causing the painful swellings Boccaccio called gavoccioli (doctors call them buboes). Usually caught from an infected flea's bite.

Pneumonic: the infection reaches the lungs. It can then spread directly from person to person through coughing, and without treatment it is almost always fatal.

Septicaemic: the infection is in the blood.

How do we know the Black Death was this disease? From ancient DNA. In 2010 a team led by Stephanie Haensch found Y. pestis DNA in plague graves from several countries. In 2011 a team led by Kirsten Bos rebuilt most of its genetic code from skeletons in a London plague cemetery, East Smithfield, used in 1348–50.

Find it 1.Which form of plague can spread directly from person to person, and how?
Pneumonic plague, through coughing, once the infection reaches the lungs. (Section I.)

II. How did it spread? Still debated

The rat-flea view. Historian Ole Benedictow and others argue that black rats and their fleas drove the spread. When infected rats died, their hungry fleas jumped to people.

The human-parasite view. In 2018 Katharine Dean and colleagues built computer models of outbreaks in nine European cities and compared them with the cities' death records. A model where human fleas and body lice passed plague between people fit best in seven of the nine cities. In the other two, the models couldn't be told apart.

A model that fits the records is evidence, but it isn't direct proof of how people caught it. The debate is still open, and many historians think more than one route was at work.

Either way, the disease travelled with people, goods and the fleas they carried, along roads, rivers and shipping lanes. So rules that held back people and goods targeted the real route, even if those who made the rules thought the danger was bad air.

III. After the course years: the birth of quarantine

Plague kept returning for centuries, and cities learned. On 27 July 1377, the port city of Ragusa (today Dubrovnik, in Croatia) ordered that people arriving from plague areas must stay apart for 30 days (a trentino) before entering. The waiting period was later stretched to 40 days, notably in Venice. In Italian, "forty days" is quaranta giorni, the root of our word quarantine. In 1423 Venice opened a plague hospital on an island in its lagoon, often called the first permanent one of its kind.

In both runs the ports reached half infected first (week 7). Quarantine at the gate does nothing for people already inside the port. In Run A the towns were hit hard: half infected by week 9, all of them by the end. In Run B the towns were hit later and less (half by week 12, about 60% by the end), because fewer infected arrivals got through before cold weather slowed everything.

So in this model quarantine protects the places behind the barrier and buys time. It doesn't save the place where the disease already is. And your hook vote? Isolating arrivals is the choice that targets the real route (people, goods, fleas). Real quarantines leaked, though, and plague still returned for centuries.

Optional video: Yale historian Frank Snowden on what plague is (about 10 minutes)

From Yale's open course HIST 234, Lecture 3, "Plague (I): Pestilence as Disease" (the "Etiology" chapter, 23:39–34:10). Rocky will only talk about this clip if you say you watched it.

What he says, in order: plague has four "protagonists": the bacterium, two carriers (fleas and rodents, especially rats) and humans. Alexandre Yersin and Shibasaburo Kitasato identified the bacterium in Hong Kong. Plague starts among wild rodents, and the black rat lived close to people. The flea is a very efficient carrier: the bacteria block its gut, so it bites again and again. Trade spread it: rats in grain shipments, and victims' clothing, recycled and resold, carrying fleas. It usually arrived by sea at ports and moved inland along roads and rivers. In big cities it typically arrived in spring and lasted two to four months.

Note: Snowden presents the rat-flea view. Section II of today's reading shows that some researchers now argue human fleas and lice mattered more.

Deeper seal (optional): Was it even plague?

For about twenty years, some scholars doubted that the Black Death was plague at all. In 1984 the biologist Graham Twigg argued it spread too fast for rat-borne plague and suggested anthrax. In 2001 Susan Scott and Christopher Duncan proposed a virus like Ebola. In 2002 the historian Samuel Cohn (whom you'll meet in Leaf VIII) argued that the medieval disease behaved too differently from modern plague to be the same thing.

Ancient DNA settled the question. The Haensch (2010) and Bos (2011) studies found Yersinia pestis in the victims' remains. But the doubters had pointed to real puzzles, about speed, seasons and death rates. Those puzzles are why researchers are still debating how it spread (Section II).

Think about this: the doubters were wrong about the disease, yet their questions moved the field forward. Is a wrong answer still useful?

Deeper seal (optional): What a model can and can't prove

Dean's team didn't observe medieval fleas. They wrote equations for three ways plague might spread (rats, human parasites, coughing) and asked which equations best reproduced the shape of real death counts. A good fit makes a theory more likely, but other untested models might fit too, and death records are themselves imperfect.

Benedictow argues from what's known about plague in more recent outbreaks, where rats and rat fleas were central. Critics reply that the speed and seasons of the medieval spread don't match rat plague well everywhere.

Today's model of ports and towns is the same kind of tool: it can show how a system could behave, not prove what happened. Notice how much you trusted it anyway.

Map stop.Where was the first known law making arrivals wait apart before entering a city?
Ragusa, in 1377, with 30 days. It's marked in blue on your chart because it came after the course years. Venice later used 40.
