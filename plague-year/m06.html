Hook vote.There was no census in 1348. How would you work out how many people died in a town?

I. A father's chronicle: Siena

Agnolo di Tura kept a chronicle of his city, Siena, in Tuscany. He wrote that the dying began there in May 1348. So many died, he wrote, that no one could be found to bury the dead, for money or for friendship. He buried five of his own children with his own hands.

He also gave a number: about 52,000 dead in the city of Siena, and about 80,000 counting the surrounding suburbs. (English translations of his chronicle differ slightly on the suburbs, but all give about 80,000 in total.)

How big was Siena? Historians' estimates for the whole city before 1348 range from about 25,000 to 50,000 people. William Bowsky (1964) gave a range of about 25,000 to 47,500, and many books round it to about 50,000.

II. A priest's register: Givry

Givry is a village in Burgundy, in eastern France, near Chalon-sur-Saône. Its parish priest wrote down every burial. In normal years before the plague, he recorded about 30 burials a year. Between 5 August and 19 November 1348, about 15 weeks, he recorded 615.

A register like this is precious: it was written at the time, burial by burial, by someone whose job was to keep count.

III. Priests replacing priests: England

English bishops kept registers of every new priest appointed to a parish that had lost its priest. A flood of new appointments in 1349 shows where priests had died. (A parish could also fall empty when a priest resigned or moved, so historians check carefully.) From records like these, historians estimate that about 40–50% of England's 9,000–10,000 parish priests died.

Caution: priests visited the dying, so they may have caught the plague more often than other people.

IV. All of Europe

For Europe as a whole, the usual estimate is that 30–50% of the population died. The historian Ole Benedictow argues for about 60%. In 2022 a team led by Adam Izdebski tried a new kind of evidence: ancient pollen from 261 sites. Where many people died, farming stopped and wild plants took over, and the pollen shows it. Their finding: the toll varied widely. Some regions were devastated, while others show little or no sign of collapse.

About 20 years' worth: 615 ÷ 30 = 20.5. Anything from 19 to 21 is right. Twenty years of funerals in fifteen weeks, in one village.

Question 2 · Agnolo's number

Agnolo's count for the city of Siena: about 52,000 dead.
Historians' estimate of the whole city's population before 1348: about 25,000 to 50,000.

Put these side by side. What does this tell you about his number?

His count is bigger than the entire estimated population of the city, even the high estimate. So it can't be an exact count. He may have exaggerated, counted people from outside the walls, repeated rumours, or simply had no way to count. Chroniclers' big round numbers often say more about how overwhelming it felt than about how many died. If you said any of this in your own words, that's the point.

Question 3 · Two kinds of source

A grieving father's chronicle (Siena). A priest's burial register (Givry).

Which would you trust more for a count, and which for what it felt like? Why?

Most historians would trust the register for the count: it was written at the time, one entry per burial, by someone whose job was counting. They'd trust the chronicle for the experience: no register tells you a father buried five children with his own hands. Good history uses both, and checks each against the other. There's room to disagree; your reason is what Rocky will ask about. And your hook vote? Historians combine several sources, because each kind of record gets something different right.

Deeper seal (optional): Do the maths on Siena, and meet Boccaccio's number

Recompute Siena. If the city held 47,500 people (the top of Bowsky's range) and lost between 30% and 60%, that's roughly 14,000 to 28,500 dead in the city. Agnolo's 52,000 is about two to four times that. At 25,000 people, even losing everyone wouldn't reach his figure.

Boccaccio did the same. In the passage you read in Leaf II, a few lines later, he claims that more than a hundred thousand died within the walls of Florence between March and July. Then he admits the city "before the deadly visitation would not have been supposed to contain so many people!" (Rigg translation, public domain.)

Pollen as evidence. Izdebski's team didn't count deaths. Pollen shows land use: whether fields were farmed or went wild. That's an indirect sign of how many people were left to farm. It's powerful because it covers places with no written records, and limited because other things (war, famine, trade) also change farming.
