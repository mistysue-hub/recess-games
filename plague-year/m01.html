I. Graves in the Chüy valley

In northern Kyrgyzstan, in Central Asia, the Chüy valley holds two medieval Christian cemeteries called Kara-Djigach and Burana. When Russian archaeologists dug there in the 1880s, they noticed something odd. An unusually large number of the tombstones carried dates from just two years, 1338 and 1339, and several said the person had died of a "pestilence."

In 2022 a team of scientists (Spyrou and colleagues, writing in the journal Nature) tested teeth from people buried there. Three people from Kara-Djigach carried the DNA of Yersinia pestis, the bacterium that causes plague.

The genetic code they found sits at a branching point. It is the ancestor of the plague strains that later swept across Europe. That suggests the pandemic's ancestor strain was in this region in the late 1330s.

How do we know the date? Not from the DNA. DNA tells scientists which strain it was. The date 1338–39 comes from the words carved on the tombstones.

Find it 1.How do historians know these people died in 1338–39? Find the line in the reading and say it in your own words.
From the tombstones. The dates are carved on the graves. The DNA identifies the strain, not the year. ("…the date 1338–39 comes from the words carved on the tombstones.")

II. Across the grasslands

Between 1338 and 1346 the written record goes almost silent. Most historians think the disease moved west across the grasslands ruled by the Golden Horde, a Mongol state, carried along trade routes by people, animals and goods. How it travelled, and how fast, is still debated. By 1346 it had reached the Black Sea.

III. Caffa, 1346

Caffa (today Feodosia, in Crimea) was a walled trading town run by merchants from Genoa, in Italy. In 1346 an army of the Golden Horde was besieging it, and plague broke out among the besiegers.

That much most historians accept. The famous part of the story comes from one writer. Read his account in the activity below before you look at who he was.

IV. Would you believe this witness?

The account (our own retelling)

The besieging army was dying by the thousands. Its leaders ordered the bodies of the dead to be loaded onto catapults and hurled over Caffa's walls, so the stench would kill the people inside. Soon the town's air and water were poisoned, and the defenders sickened. Some of them fled by ship, and wherever those ships landed, the sickness came ashore with them.

Step 1.How much would you trust this account?
The witness: Gabriele de' Mussis, a notary (a legal clerk) in Piacenza, northern Italy. He wrote around 1348. Records place him at home in Piacenza, about 1,250 miles (2,000 km) in a straight line from Caffa. Historians think he built the story from what returning traders told him.
Step 2.Now that you know who he was, how much do you trust it?

Historians disagree.

Mark Wheelis (2002) thinks the corpse-throwing is plausible, and that it may be how plague got into Caffa. But he thinks plague reached Europe from Crimea anyway, along trade routes, whether or not the siege happened.

Zanders (2023, reported in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists) argues it probably never happened. The story doesn't fit what medieval siege engines could do, or Caffa's geography.

No one can prove it either way. Your rating is a judgment, not a right-or-wrong answer. What matters is your reason.

V. By ship

From the Black Sea, trading ships sailed south to Constantinople (1347), the great city guarding the passage into the Mediterranean. From there, merchant ships carried the disease west across the Mediterranean towards Italy and beyond. So the plague came overland to the Black Sea, then by ship into the Mediterranean.

Deeper seal (optional): How strong is this evidence?

Three samples. Only three people from one cemetery tested positive. That is enough to identify the strain, but a thin base for a big story. The Burana samples gave human DNA but no plague.

Ancestor, not starting gun. The study shows an ancestor strain was present in the Tian Shan region around 1338. It does not show that the Black Death "set off" from this valley, or trace its route west.

Why Wheelis doubts the catapults mattered for Europe. Plague was spreading through the whole region. Other Black Sea ports traded with Italy too. So even if corpses were thrown, the disease had many other ways to reach the ships.
