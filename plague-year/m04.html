Hook vote.When disaster strikes and no one understands why, what do you think people tend to do?

I. The flagellants

In 1349 bands of penitents (people doing penance for their sins) marched from town to town, especially in German lands. They prayed, sang, and whipped themselves in public, hoping to turn away God's anger. Crowds came out to watch. The Church grew alarmed. On 20 October 1349 Pope Clement VI condemned the movement.

II. A false accusation

As the plague spread, a rumour spread with it: that Jews were poisoning wells to kill Christians. The accusation was false. But it was believed, and it was used.

In autumn 1348, in Savoy (near Lake Geneva), Jews were arrested and tortured until they "confessed." Reports of these confessions were passed from city to city as if they were proof. A confession under torture proves nothing: people in agony say whatever their questioners want.

Pope Clement VI tried to stop it. In July 1348 he issued an order protecting Jews. On 26 September 1348 he issued a second, stronger one (known by its opening Latin words, Quamvis perfidiam). In it he argued:

the accusation could not be true, because Jews were dying of the plague too;

and the plague was striking places where no Jews lived at all.

He threatened anyone who attacked Jews with excommunication (being cut off from the Church). But in many places, especially in German lands, his orders were little obeyed.

III. Strasbourg, February 1349

In Strasbourg, a rich city on the Rhine, the city council tried to protect the Jewish community. Members of the trade guilds and others turned against the council, and on 9–10 February 1349 the councillors who wanted to save the Jews were removed from office. The new council gave in.

On 14 February 1349 the Jews of Strasbourg were killed: about 2,000 people, by the count of a Strasbourg chronicler. Everything owed to the Jews was cancelled. The chronicler himself wrote that money was the real reason the Jews were killed.

The plague had not yet reached Strasbourg. It arrived that summer.

IV. Why did people do it?

Historians explain the killings with several causes that worked together: fear of a disease nobody understood; debt, since killing lenders erased what people owed; local politics, as groups fought for control of city councils; and older hatred, since there had been attacks on Jews in German lands before 1348. Historians weigh these causes differently.

Explaining is not excusing. Understanding why people did something terrible doesn't make it less terrible. The victims did nothing to cause it. The accusation was false, and people at the time had the evidence to know it.

The messenger

You are a messenger carrying the Pope's September letter to a city on the Rhine. You can't change what happened. That is history. But at each step you choose what evidence to carry and what to say. Then the seal shows what really happened and what people at the time already knew.

Step 1 · Winter 1348: the rumour arrives

Traders bring copies of the "confessions" from Savoy. People in the market are frightened. Which piece of evidence do you put in front of them first?

All three were known in 1348. The Pope's own letter made the second and third points. Any one of them was enough to show the accusation was false. The problem was never a lack of evidence.

Step 2 · January 1349: the council meets

The city council is trying to protect the Jewish community, but it is under pressure. What do you tell the councillors?

Strasbourg's council did try to protect the Jews. Some people in power refused to go along with the accusation. The pressure on them came from guildsmen and others, and money and city politics were part of it, as the chronicler himself admitted.

Step 3 · 9–10 February 1349: the protectors are removed

The councillors who defended the Jews are forced out. A new council takes over. What do you do?

The new council gave in to the pressure. Each choice you could make is one real people made in other places and times: appealing, warning, recording. In Strasbourg, the record survives because a chronicler wrote it down.

Step 4 · 14 February 1349

On this day the Jews of Strasbourg were killed. In one or two sentences: what do you think people today should remember about it?

About 2,000 people were killed, by the chronicler's count. Debts owed to them were cancelled. The plague did not reach Strasbourg until that summer. The accusation was false, the evidence against it was known at the time, and some people, including the Pope and the city's first council, spoke against it.

Deeper seal (optional): Older hatred, and how historians weigh the causes

The violence of 1348–49 did not come from nowhere. German lands had seen waves of attacks on Jewish communities before, such as the Rintfleisch massacres of 1298 and the "Armleder" attacks of 1336–38. The well-poisoning accusation drew on older lies about Jews.

Historians disagree about which cause mattered most. Some stress economics and politics: debts, and struggles over who ran city councils (Strasbourg's own chronicler pointed to money). Others stress religious hatred and fear, which had built up over centuries. Most see a mix that varied from town to town.

What historians do not disagree about: the accusation was false, and the victims were innocent.
