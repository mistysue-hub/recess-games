Hook vote.In 1348, what do you think people mostly blamed for the plague?

Four theories

Nobody in 1348 knew about bacteria. The plague bacterium wasn't identified until 1894. But people did have theories, and they acted on them. Four ideas ran side by side, and many people believed more than one at once.

1. Bad air (miasma). Doctors trained in the teachings of the ancient Greek physicians Hippocrates and Galen believed disease came from corrupted air. Rotting bodies, swamps, filth and foul smells poisoned the air, and breathing it made you sick. The cure: avoid stench, clean the streets, and carry sweet-smelling herbs (remember Boccaccio's third group?).

2. The stars. In October 1348 the medical faculty of the University of Paris, the most respected doctors in Europe, sent a report to the King of France. They blamed a meeting of three planets in the sign of Aquarius at one o'clock in the afternoon on 20 March 1345, which, they said, had corrupted the air. So their theory blends the stars and bad air.

3. God's anger (sin). Many believed the plague was God's punishment for human wickedness. The remedy was prayer, confession and processions through the streets. Boccaccio mentions Florence's processions.

4. Contagion. People also noticed that the sick seemed to pass it on, and so did their belongings. Boccaccio wrote that "any that touched the cloth of the sick… seemed thereby to contract the disease." People acted on this long before anyone could explain it.

Look back.Which hook-vote option was not a 1348 theory? Find the line that shows it.
Germs. It isn't one of the four theories. "Nobody in 1348 knew about bacteria. The plague bacterium wasn't identified until 1894." If you voted bad air, the stars or God's anger, all three were real 1348 theories. Contagion is the closest they came to the germ idea: they saw it spread without knowing why.

The council of Pistoia acts

Pistoia is a small walled city about 20 miles from Florence, between Florence and Lucca. Plague reached the area in early spring 1348. On 2 May 1348 its council passed a set of health rules, then revised them on 23 May. Three of them (in our own words):

Travel ban. No citizen may travel to the plague-struck cities of Pisa or Lucca, and no one from those cities may come into Pistoia.

Used cloth. No one may bring used linen or woollen cloth into the city.

Sealed coffins. The dead must be placed in a wooden coffin nailed shut before leaving the house, and buried deep: two and a half braccia (about 1.5 metres, or 5 feet).

These are among the most detailed early city plague rules to survive.

Some of these rules may have helped for reasons nobody then knew. The clothing of plague victims was often resold, and, as the historian Frank Snowden explains, it could carry fleas.

Match the theories

Physician: bad air or the stars. He believes both, like the Paris doctors.

Gravedigger: no theory. He only counts, and follows the burial rules.

Travel ban: contagion (keep sick people out).
Used cloth: contagion or bad air (cloth was thought to hold either the disease or the poisoned air).
Sealed coffins, deep graves: bad air or contagion (shut in the stench, keep bodies away from the living).

Talk-back.Pistoia banned used cloth because it believed in bad air or contagion. Can a rule be right for the wrong reason? Use the reading.
Yes, it can. The council didn't know about fleas, but resold clothing could carry them, so the ban may have blocked a real route of infection. A rule can work even when the reason behind it is wrong. That makes it tricky to judge people in the past: do we credit their results or their reasons? Your view is what counts here.
Map stop.Which came first: plague in Pistoia's region, or the council's rules?
The plague came first. It had reached the region, including nearby Pisa and Lucca, by early spring 1348. The rules followed on 2 May, partly to keep more of it out. Pistoia is now on your route chart.
Deeper seal (optional): Were medieval doctors fools?

No. Miasma theory fit the evidence they had. Plague hit hardest in crowded, filthy, foul-smelling places. The smell of death was everywhere. And the greatest medical authorities of the ancient world had taught that bad air caused epidemics.

And the remedies of the time weren't all useless. Isolating the sick, keeping out travellers and goods from plague cities, and burying the dead quickly and deeply all targeted real ways the disease spread, even though the reasoning behind them was different.
