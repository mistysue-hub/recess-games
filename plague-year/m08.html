First instinct.Before you read: did the Black Death make the Renaissance possible?

You can change your mind later. Most good historians do at least once.

I. What was the Renaissance, and when?

"Renaissance" means rebirth. Historians use it for a revival of interest in ancient Greek and Roman learning, art and ideas that began in Italy and spread across Europe, roughly from the 1300s to the 1500s. Scholars called humanists hunted for lost ancient books, studied Latin and Greek, and wrote about human life and achievement. Petrarch is often called the first humanist, though earlier scholars in Padua, such as Lovato Lovati and Albertino Mussato, had already begun reviving classical Latin.

A timeline to keep in view:

c. 1305: Giotto paints the Arena Chapel in Padua, with lifelike figures that later artists admired.

c. 1320: Dante finishes the Divine Comedy, in Italian rather than Latin.

1341: Petrarch is crowned poet laureate in Rome.

1347–53: the Black Death.

c. 1353: Boccaccio finishes the Decameron.

1361–63: plague returns.

1420: Brunelleschi begins the great dome of Florence's cathedral.

Find it 1.Which people and works on the timeline came before 1348? What does that suggest for your verdict?
Giotto (c. 1305), Dante (c. 1320) and Petrarch's crowning (1341) all came before 1348. So something new was already under way before the plague. That's a problem for "necessary cause," though not for "sped it up." What you make of it is your call.

II. Four historians, four arguments

Millard Meiss (1951): it turned art backwards. Studying painting in Florence and Siena, Meiss argued that after 1348 art became more somber, more religious and more old-fashioned, a step back from Giotto's lifelike style. Later historians, including Henk van Os, Judith Steinhoff and Diana Norman, challenged him. They pointed to crises that began before 1348, such as bank failures and famine in the 1340s, and argued that religious art had its own reasons.

David Herlihy (1997): it broke a deadlock. Before 1348, Herlihy argued, Europe had more people than its land could comfortably feed: a "Malthusian deadlock." The plague broke it. With labour scarce and dear, Europe was pushed to find new, labour-saving ways of doing things. (His lectures were published after his death, edited by Samuel Cohn.)

Samuel Cohn (1992): the change came with the second wave. Cohn read thousands of wills from six cities in central Italy. After the plague returned in 1363, people stopped scattering many small gifts and started leaving large bequests, such as paintings and chapels, so that they and their families would be remembered. To Cohn, the turn came after the return of plague, and it was about family memory.

Same catastrophe, different recoveries. The plague struck the Islamic world too. In Aleppo, in Syria, the scholar Ibn al-Wardi wrote a literary essay about the plague in rhyming prose, a form called a maqāma. He pictured the plague as a wandering figure moving from city to city. He died of it on 18 March 1349. Like Boccaccio, he answered catastrophe with literature. The historian Stuart Borsch compared Egypt and England. Egypt recovered more slowly, and he argues the reason was how land was owned and run (controlled from the cities by a ruling elite), not culture or religion.

Deeper seal (optional): Individuals, families, and a misread witness

Cohn against the old story. In 1860 the Swiss historian Jacob Burckhardt described the Renaissance as the birth of the modern individual. Cohn's wills point another way: after 1363, people wanted to be remembered as part of a family line, with their names, coats of arms and chapels. Was the Renaissance about individuals, or families?

Petrarch the humanist. Remember Leaf VII: in grief, Petrarch nearly burned his papers, then gathered his letters instead. Collecting, preserving and studying writings was exactly what humanists did. Does a personal loss in 1348 count as evidence about a whole movement?

A witness misread. For decades, some historians read Ibn al-Wardi's essay as an eyewitness map of the plague's route, and built a theory of a very fast journey from Central Asia on it. In 2025 Omar and Fancy, historians at the University of Exeter, argued it is a literary work and was never meant as a route map. Remember de' Mussis in Leaf I? The same lesson applies: know what kind of source you're reading.
