I. "Solitary and bereaved": Petrarch, about 1350

Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch) was the most famous poet and scholar in Italy, who became a close friend of Boccaccio. The plague of 1348 killed many of the people closest to him. About two years later he wrote this letter to a friend he nicknamed "Socrates." It became the preface to his collected letters.

What now, brother? We have tried almost everything, and nowhere have we found peace. When may we hope for that, and where shall we seek it? Time, as the saying is, has slipped between our fingers. Our early hopes are buried with our friends. The year 1348 has left us solitary and bereaved; and has taken from us what all the wealth of Ormus and of Ind could never replace.

Such final losses are irreparable, and the wounds inflicted by death can never be healed. […]

[…] I found, for example, a vast store of scattered and neglected writings of different kinds in the house. I have laboriously exhumed boxes, buried in dust, and bundles of manuscript, half-destroyed by time. […] Surrounded by the confused masses of letters and manuscripts I began, following my first impulse, to consign everything to the flames, with a view to escaping from the inglorious task of assorting the papers. Then, as one thought springs from another, it occurred to me that, like a traveller weary by reason of the long road, I might well look back as from an eminence, and step by step review the history of my younger days.

In plain English

"Brother, we've tried everything and found no peace. Time has slipped away. Our hopes died with our friends. 1348 left us alone and grieving, and took people no amount of money could replace. Those losses can't be undone. ... I found piles of old letters and papers in my house, dusty and half-ruined. At first I wanted to burn them all rather than sort them. Then I decided to look back over my younger life, step by step, as a tired traveller looks back from a hilltop."

Tap any word with a dotted underline to see what it means. "Socrates" here is a nickname for Petrarch's friend, not the Greek philosopher.

Find it 1.What was Petrarch's first impulse about his old papers, and what did he decide to do instead?
At first he wanted to burn everything ("consign everything to the flames"). Then he decided to look back over them and "review the history of my younger days." He kept and collected his letters, which is why we can read this one.

II. Fewer hands: wages and the law in England

With so many dead, there were far fewer people to plough, reap and build. Surviving workers could ask for more. Boccaccio already noticed servants "hardly to be had at high rates."

The English king struck back. His Ordinance of Labourers of 18 June 1349 begins: "Because a great part of the people, and especially of workmen and servants, late died of the pestilence," and complains that some survivors demanded excessive pay. In our own words, it ordered:

every able-bodied person under sixty without their own land or trade must work for anyone who needs them;

only for the wages customary before the plague (in 1346–47, or the five or six years before);

and lords get first claim on the labour of their own serfs and tenants. (A serf was a peasant legally bound to a lord's estate.)

In 1351 Parliament made it stricter, in the Statute of Labourers. Court records show many prosecutions, mostly of workers who took or demanded more than the law allowed. That is good evidence they kept doing it. Many farm workers were paid partly in food, drink or goods as well as coins, which makes "wages" hard to measure. Historians agree the law couldn't hold pay down for long, but they argue about how much, and how fast, workers' real earnings rose.

Find it 2.What did the 1349 law try to do about wages, and why? Then check your hook vote.
It tried to freeze wages at pre-plague levels and force people to work, because so many workers had died and survivors were demanding more. Your hook: pay was pushed up by the shortage, and the law fought it. That's why workers kept being prosecuted.

III. It came back

The Black Death of 1347–53 was only the first wave. Plague returned in 1361–62 in England and in 1363 in Italy. It kept coming back, again and again, for centuries.

In 1381 English peasants and townspeople rose in the Peasants' Revolt and marched on London. Its causes were several: a new poll tax (a tax on every head), anger at the labour laws, and demands to end serfdom.

Deeper seal (optional): The Brenner debate. Did population decide, or power?

Older historians such as Michael Postan and Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie explained the late Middle Ages mainly through population and resources. Fewer people meant land was cheaper and labour dearer, so workers gained.

In 1976 Robert Brenner argued that population alone can't explain it. What mattered was power between lords and peasants. In western Europe peasants won more freedom. In parts of eastern Europe lords later tightened control, the so-called "second serfdom." Same shock, different outcomes, so class power must have decided.

Critics, including Postan with John Hatcher and Le Roy Ladurie, replied that Brenner pushed population aside too far: demography still set the conditions that power struggles played out in. Others noted that the eastern "second serfdom" hardened mainly in the 1400s–1500s, so the contrast is about long-run paths rather than an instant reaction to 1348. And the economist Gregory Clark (2016) argues there's little sign the Black Death changed England's economy beyond what the population loss itself would predict.

Think about this: if the same disaster leads to freedom in one place and serfdom in another, what does that say about "the plague caused X"?
