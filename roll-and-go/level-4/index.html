Level 4: Boss Level, The Playtest

The boss is here

You've planned your game. Now comes the hardest and most important part of game design: finding out whether it actually works. Every real game studio does this. It's called a playtest, and it's this course's boss level.

Why you have to play it

Here's something that surprises a lot of new designers. A designer only gets to write the rules. The fun, the boredom, the excitement and the confusion all happen later, when real people sit down and play.

Game designers who study this explain it with three words: mechanics, dynamics and aesthetics.

  • Mechanics are the rules you write: roll two dice, slide down on a lava space.
  • Dynamics are what happens when people follow those rules: players racing for the zip line, everyone groaning at the earthquake card.
  • Aesthetics are the feelings players have: excitement, laughter, "no way!"

Designer's Secret: you write the rules, but the fun only shows up when people play. That's why game design is hard, and why you have to test.

Build a quick prototype

A prototype is a first version, made fast and cheap, so you can test it. It does NOT need to be beautiful yet. Designers often start with paper and markers.

You can build with:

  • paper, cardboard or a cereal box
  • markers, crayons or colored pencils
  • coins, buttons, LEGO people or clay for pieces
  • index cards or cut paper for your surprise cards
  • real dice

Cut and glue only the way your grown-up says is OK.

How to run a playtest

Find at least one other person to play: a friend, a sibling, a parent, anyone. Then follow the designer's playtest rules:

  1. Explain the basics only. Tell them the goal and how to take a turn. Then let the game do the rest.
  2. Play, and watch closely. Where do people look confused? When do they get bored? When do they get excited?
  3. Don't defend your game. If something breaks, that's good news. You found it!
  4. Take notes. Write down anything that went wrong or dragged.
  5. Ask questions after. "What was your favorite moment?" "What felt unfair?" "Would you play again?"

Change one thing

After the playtest, pick one problem and fix it. Maybe the game was too long, so you shorten the path. Maybe one space was too strong, so you weaken it. Maybe nobody ever landed on your best space, so you move it.

Then, if you can, play again and see if your fix worked. Real designers playtest a game many, many times before it's finished. Every round makes it better.

Design Quest: Beat the Boss

Part 1: Build your prototype: the board, the pieces and the cards.

Part 2: Playtest it with at least one other person.

Part 3: Write down one thing that broke, dragged or felt unfair, and change one rule to fix it.
