Level 2: The Crossroads

Welcome back, Lead Designer

Last time you came up with your pitch: who your players are, what they're trying to do and how someone wins. Now it's time to answer two big questions. Where do the pieces go? And what decides how far they move?

Shape your path

A path doesn't have to be a boring line of squares. Here are some shapes designers use:

  • Loop: the path goes around and around, so players can pass the start again and again.
  • Spiral: the path curls inward toward a prize in the middle. The Game of the Goose, first played in Italy more than 500 years ago and one of the first board games ever sold in shops, used a spiral.
  • Race track: start at one end, finish at the other.
  • Fork: the path splits, and players choose which way to go.
  • Maze: lots of turns, dead ends and shortcuts.
  • Tower: the path climbs up, like stairs or a mountain. You could even build it in 3D!

Your board can be any size too: as small as a sheet of paper or as big as your floor. Just remember that a longer path makes a longer game.

Pick your mover

The mover is whatever decides how far you go. You don't have to use one ordinary die. You could use:

  • one die (1 to 6, every number equally likely)
  • two dice added together (2 to 12)
  • a spinner you draw and cut out
  • coin flips (heads moves 2, tails moves 1)
  • a deck of number cards
  • a die with custom faces, like a die where one side says "go back 1" or "roll again"

Each one feels different. One die is quick and wild. Two dice are a little more predictable, and here's the surprising reason why.

The secret of two dice

When you roll one die, every number from 1 to 6 has the same chance. But when you roll two dice and add them, some totals show up way more than others.

There are 36 different ways two dice can land. Only one of those ways makes a 2 (a 1 and a 1). Only one way makes a 12 (a 6 and a 6). But six different ways make a 7: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4, 4 and 3, 5 and 2, and 6 and 1.

So with two dice, 7 is the most common total, and totals get rarer the farther they are from 7. Designers use this! A space 7 steps from the start will get landed on a lot. A space 2 steps away hardly ever gets hit by a first roll.

You're going to test this yourself with real dice. Scientists call that an experiment.

Luck and choices

Roll-and-go games run on luck, and luck is a good thing. Game designers point out that luck lets anyone win. In a game that's all luck, a little kid has just as good a chance as a grown-up. Luck also keeps every game different, so you never know what will happen.

But luck alone can get boring, because players are just waiting for their turn. That's why a famous video game designer named Sid Meier said a game is a series of interesting choices. A choice is interesting when there's more than one good option and the player's pick actually changes what happens.

Designer's Secret: luck lets anyone win, but choices make a game worth playing.

Here are some ways to add choices to a roll-and-go game:

  • A fork in the path: safe and slow, or risky and fast?
  • Roll two dice, use one: pick which number to move.
  • Two pieces each: choose which one to move.
  • Stop or keep going: move fewer spaces than you rolled, to land somewhere good.

Design Quest: The Crossroads

Part 1: The dice experiment. Grab two real dice, paper and a pencil. Write the numbers 2 to 12 down the side of your page. Roll both dice 36 times, add them, and make a tally mark next to the total each time. Which total won? Which totals barely showed up?

Part 2: Sketch your path. Draw your path's shape for your game. Give it at least one fork or choice for players.

Part 3: Pick your mover. Decide what moves the pieces in your game and why it fits your theme.
