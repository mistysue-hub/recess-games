Level 1: The Idea Spark

Welcome to the studio

Every board game you have ever played started the same way: someone had an idea and grabbed some paper. Today, that someone is you.

You're going to invent a roll-and-go game. In a roll-and-go game, you roll something, move your piece along a path, and land on a space that makes something happen. It's one of the oldest kinds of board game in the world.

A very old game with a very big idea

Long ago in India, people played a game called Moksha Patam. The board was covered in squares. Players rolled and moved upward, hoping to reach the top square.

Here's the clever part. Some squares had ladders that zoomed you upward, and some had snakes that slid you back down. The ladders stood for good deeds, like being generous or humble. The snakes stood for bad choices, like anger or stealing. So the game was really a lesson: good choices lift you up, and bad ones drag you down.

In the 1890s, the game traveled to England and became Snakes and Ladders. Later, in the United States, it became Chutes and Ladders. The lessons faded, but the path, the dice and the ups and downs stayed. People still play it more than a hundred years later.

That's your first clue about what makes a game last: a simple idea that makes people feel something. Hope when you spot a ladder. A groan when you hit a snake.

The parts of every roll-and-go game

Look closely at any roll-and-go game and you'll find the same six parts:

  1. A path: the track your piece follows.
  2. A mover: whatever decides how far you go, like a die, two dice, a spinner or cards.
  3. Spaces: the stops along the path. Some are plain, and some are special.
  4. A goal: what you're trying to do, like reach the top, escape or collect something.
  5. A win condition: the exact moment someone wins.
  6. Twists: surprises that change everything, like a ladder, a snake or a shortcut.

Your game will have all six, but you get to decide what each one looks like.

Why do people love board games?

Game companies and scientists have asked thousands of people why they play board games. The biggest answer isn't winning.

It's being together. In one big survey of people in four countries, most said they play board games to bring family and friends closer, and lots said games help start a conversation. Researchers say a game gives people something to do together, so it's easier to talk, laugh and feel like part of a group, even with people you don't know well yet.

People also love that board games are something you can touch: real dice, real cards, real pieces. And many people play to escape into a different world for a while.

Pick your kinds of fun

Game designers have a secret tool: they name the kind of fun they want players to feel. A famous paper by three game designers lists eight kinds of fun. Here they are in kid words:

  • Challenge: "That was hard, and I did it!"
  • Make-believe: "I'm a dragon now!"
  • Story: "Remember when THAT happened?"
  • Discovery: "Whoa, what's over here?"
  • Showing your style: "I did it my way."
  • Hanging out together: "This is fun because we're all here."
  • The feel of it: the clatter of dice, bright colors, pieces you love to hold.
  • Just relaxing: "This is a nice, easy way to pass the time."

The designers also mentioned a ninth: friendly competition.

No game has all of them. Great games pick a few and do them really well. A game about sneaking past a sleeping giant might go for make-believe, the feel of rolling dice and a big story moment when the giant wakes up.
