# Module 7: Revolution: The Drifting Continents

The Atlantic is widening by a few centimeters a year, roughly the rate your fingernails grow. In 1926, a room full of expert geologists was asked to consider that continents move, and most of them said no. They had good reasons. By the 1960s, almost all of them, or their students, had switched sides.

## An outsider's hypothesis

On January 6, 1912, in Frankfurt, the meteorologist Alfred Wegener presented an idea most geologists would spend half a century rejecting: the continents move. His 1915 book, *The Origin of Continents and Oceans*, went through several revised editions. It argued that the continents were once joined in a single landmass, which he later named Pangaea, which broke apart and drifted.

The evidence Wegener assembled was broad:

- **Geometric fit.** Matched along the edges of their continental shelves rather than the present coastlines, South America and Africa fit closely.
- **Fossils.** *Mesosaurus*, a freshwater reptile, occurs in both Brazil and southern Africa; the seed fern *Glossopteris* is spread across today's southern continents. (Decades later, in 1969, the reptile *Lystrosaurus* turned up in Antarctica too.)
- **Geology.** Rock types and geological structures line up across the Atlantic.
- **Ancient climates.** Glacial deposits in Africa, India and Australia, places now warm, and coal with fossil plants in Antarctica.

## The rejection was not just stubbornness

At a 1926 symposium of the American Association of Petroleum Geologists in New York, most speakers opposed drift; the chairman was one of the few supporters. It is tempting to tell this as closed-minded experts versus a lone genius. The real story is messier.

Wegener's critics had serious objections. He had no workable **mechanism**. The forces he proposed, such as tidal forces and one he called "flight from the poles," were far too weak to move continents. And his picture of continents plowing through solid ocean floor made little physical sense. His estimates of the drift rate, some of them meters a year, were far too fast. And alternative explanations existed for the fossils, such as now-sunken land bridges. Wegener was also an outsider to geology. In 1931 the British geologist Arthur Holmes suggested heat-driven convection in the mantle as a possible engine, but without supporting data it drew little attention. Wegener had already died, in November 1930, on the Greenland ice sheet.

## Evidence from the sea floor

The turn came from the oceans. In 1952 Marie Tharp, plotting sonar depth data with Bruce Heezen at Columbia University, found a rift valley running along the crest of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Heezen dismissed it as "girl talk" for months, until earthquake locations turned out to follow her rift. Harry Hess proposed **seafloor spreading** in a 1960 report, published in 1962: new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and moves outward. The sea floor is striped with magnetic bands that record flips of Earth's magnetic field. In 1963 Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews proposed that spreading made these stripes. If so, the pattern on one side of a ridge should mirror the other. Ship surveys confirmed the symmetry by 1966. (Lawrence Morley had the same idea independently, but two journals rejected his paper.) By 1967–68, with earthquake data added, **plate tectonics** had become the framework of geophysics.

## Revolution, Kuhn-style

Notice what plate tectonics is not: it isn't simply Wegener vindicated. In plate tectonics, continents don't plow through the ocean floor; they ride on plates that include the ocean floor. The vocabulary, the mechanism and the problems geologists worked on all changed. That is closer to what Kuhn meant by a revolution: not one fact added, but a different picture of what Earth is and which questions matter. It also shows why Kuhn rejected the simple "the evidence was always there" story. The same fossils meant one thing to Wegener and another to his critics until a new framework made them fit.

Why it matters now: Plate tectonics is how scientists know where big earthquakes and volcanoes are likely, even though they still can't predict exactly when. Every earthquake-risk map is built on an idea most experts once rejected.

**A complication:** Wegener's critics were not only objecting to a missing mechanism. Arthur Holmes, a British geologist, proposed in 1928–29 that slow heat-driven currents in the mantle could move continents, an idea that later fed into sea-floor spreading. So a candidate mechanism existed decades before acceptance, and still didn't win people over. Evidence needs a community ready to hear it.

**Weird but true:** For roughly two decades Marie Tharp was not allowed aboard her institution's research vessels; women were barred from shipboard work. Heezen and others collected soundings, and Tharp built the maps from their numbers, earthquake records and older profiles. She joined an expedition at sea in 1968.

## Terms

- **Mechanism:** an account of the process that produces an effect.
- **Seafloor spreading:** the formation of new oceanic crust at mid-ocean ridges and its movement away from them.
- **Plate tectonics:** the theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into moving plates.

Side View: Paul Feyerabend, in *Against Method* (1975), argued that no single methodological rule fits the whole history of science, and that great advances often broke the rules of their day. He joked that the only principle that survives is "anything goes," and many readers take that as a warning to rule-makers rather than a recipe. Feyerabend also defended keeping minority ideas alive, because they can be the source of the evidence that later overturns the majority. Wegener's few supporters seem to fit his case. But would a rule of "keep every rejected idea alive" have helped, or would it have kept many wrong ideas going too?
