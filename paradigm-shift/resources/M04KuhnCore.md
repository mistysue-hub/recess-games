# Module 4: Seeing What You Expect

Quick: what color is the six of spades? Black, of course. So what would you see if someone flashed a red one in front of you? In 1949, almost everyone saw something that wasn't there.

## The trick deck

In 1949, two psychologists at Harvard, Jerome Bruner and Leo Postman, played a sneaky game with 28 students.

They used a machine that could flash a picture for a tiny moment, as short as one hundredth of a second. They flashed playing cards, one at a time. Each card was shown again and again, a little longer each time, until the student named it correctly twice in a row.

Most of the cards were normal. But some were trick cards, painted the wrong color. There was a red six of spades. There was a black four of hearts. Spades are supposed to be black, and hearts are supposed to be red.

## What the students saw

The normal cards were easy. On average, students named them after a flash of about three hundredths of a second.

The trick cards were a different story. They took about four times as long to name correctly. And at first, almost every student saw a trick card as a normal one. A red six of spades got called "six of hearts" (the color won) or "six of spades" (the shape won). Their brains quietly fixed the card to match what they expected.

Some students did something stranger. About half of them, looking at a red spade, reported in-between colors, like purple or brown. Their eyes said red, their expectations said black, and they saw something in the middle.

A few students got upset and confused. They weren't sure anymore what a playing card even looked like. And some never named certain trick cards correctly at all, even when the card stayed up for a full second.

Here's the hopeful part. Once a student had figured out one trick card, the next trick card got much easier. Knowing that trick cards *existed* changed what they could see.

## Why Kuhn cared

Thomas Kuhn loved this experiment, because he thought scientists are just like those students.

A paradigm tells scientists what to expect. Most of the time that's a huge help: it lets them see what matters fast. But it also means that something unexpected can get missed, or quietly fitted into the old categories. Only after scientists start to suspect something's off do they really see it. And once they do, they can't un-see it.

**Weird but true:** The famous drawing that looks like both a duck and a rabbit first appeared in 1892, as a joke in a German humor magazine. People have been puzzling over it ever since.

## Words for your notebook

- **Expectation:** what you think you'll see before you look.
- **Anomaly:** something that doesn't fit what you expected. The trick cards were anomalies.
- **Gestalt switch** (guh-SHTALT): when the same picture suddenly looks completely different to you, like a drawing that flips between a duck and a rabbit.

Side View: Norwood Russell Hanson, an American philosopher, wrote a book in 1958 arguing that seeing isn't just your eyes taking a picture. What you see depends on what you already know. He used drawings that can be seen two ways to make his point. Think of an X-ray: a doctor and someone who has never studied medicine look at the same picture, but they don't notice the same things. Do you think two people looking at the same thing always see the same thing?
