# M07 notes (Rocky only — never open on screen or read out)

## Hook answer
About 50 years. Wegener proposed the idea in 1912, most geologists rejected it for decades, and it was accepted only in the 1960s, after sea-floor evidence and a mechanism (sea-floor spreading). Hear their number first; give no hint.

## Talk-back (one at a time, after the look-back)
1. What was the biggest reason geologists rejected Wegener?
   Answer: he couldn't explain what force could move the continents (no convincing mechanism). Also accepted: his speed estimate was far too fast; he was an outsider.
2. What new evidence finally changed their minds?
   Answer: the ocean floor: the rift along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Marie Tharp, 1952), sea-floor spreading (Harry Hess), and magnetic stripes that match on both sides of ridges (explained by Vine and Matthews in 1963, confirmed by ship surveys by 1966).

## Side View question
Core: Say your whole class thinks your idea won't work, but you still think it might. When should you keep going, and when should you listen? (Feyerabend said scientists sometimes have to stick with an idea most experts reject.)
Extended: Feyerabend said scientists sometimes have to stick with an idea most experts reject. Should they? When?
Opinion question: no right answer. Stay neutral.

## Facts to get right
- Wegener: lecture January 1912, book 1915. He studied weather (a meteorologist). He died in Greenland in 1930.
- Plate tectonics is not exactly Wegener's idea: continents don't plow through the ocean floor; they ride on plates.
- Never call the critics simply stubborn; they had real objections.

## Activity 2 (Core: only if the activity count is two; Extended: runs by default)
Core: Ask the learner about an idea of theirs (or a friend's) that people dismissed at first. What would it have taken to convince them?
Extended: Say: "By 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, a doctor in a Vienna hospital, had noticed that mothers died of childbed fever far more often in the ward run by doctors than in the ward run by midwives. The doctors often came straight from autopsies. When he made them wash their hands in a chlorine solution, deaths dropped sharply. He blamed "cadaverous particles" from the corpses but couldn't show what they were, since germs weren't yet understood, and most doctors rejected his idea. He died in 1865, before it was accepted." Ask: "Semmelweis had strong results but an explanation he couldn't prove. Should doctors have changed what they did anyway? What would it cost to wait?" Stay neutral.
