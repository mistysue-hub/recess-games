# Module 8: Talking Past Each Other

In 1930, an 11-year-old girl in England came up with the name for a brand-new planet. Her name was Venetia Burney, and she suggested "Pluto." Seventy-six years later, a vote took Pluto off the list of planets, and Pluto itself never changed at all.

## The planet nobody counts anymore

On New Year's Day, 1801, the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi spotted a tiny moving dot between Mars and Jupiter. It was named Ceres, and for more than fifty years, astronomy books listed it as a planet.

Then astronomers kept finding more objects in the same zone, dozens of them. Were they all planets? Slowly, during the 1850s and 1860s, astronomers started calling them something else: **asteroids**. Ceres hadn't changed at all. The word "planet" had.

## Pluto's turn

In 1930, Clyde Tombaugh discovered Pluto, and for 76 years it was the ninth planet. Then, in 2005, a team led by Mike Brown found Eris, out past Pluto and almost exactly the same size. If Pluto was a planet, wasn't Eris? And what about the other icy worlds being found out there?

In August 2006, the International Astronomical Union voted on an official definition. A planet must:

1. orbit the Sun,
2. be massive enough to pull itself into a round shape, and
3. have **cleared its neighborhood**: be big enough that its gravity has swept away or controls the other objects near its orbit.

Pluto passes the first two tests. It fails the third, because it shares its region of space with lots of other icy objects. So Pluto became a **dwarf planet**, and Eris and Ceres were called dwarf planets too.

Notice: Pluto wasn't demoted for being "too small." The rules set no minimum size: Mercury is smaller than Jupiter's moon Ganymede and is still a planet. Pluto changed category because of rule 3.

## People still disagree

Not everyone accepts the vote. Alan Stern, the scientist who led NASA's New Horizons mission to Pluto, has argued that rule 3 makes no sense. Some scientists use a definition based on roundness alone, which would make Pluto a planet again, plus many others, including big moons like ours.

Here's Kuhn's point. When two people argue "Pluto is a planet!" "No, it isn't!", they may not disagree about a single fact about Pluto. They agree on its size, its orbit, its icy surface. They're using the word "planet" differently. After a big change in science, the words people use can change meaning, and then people on the two sides talk past each other. Kuhn thought this made revolutions hard to settle with evidence alone.

**Weird but true:** Venetia Burney's grandfather read about the new planet in the newspaper and told her at breakfast. She suggested Pluto, the Roman god of the underworld. He passed the idea to an astronomer friend, who sent a telegram to the observatory in Arizona that had found it.

## Words for your notebook

- **Definition:** the rules for what a word includes and leaves out.
- **Dwarf planet:** a round body that orbits the Sun but hasn't cleared its neighborhood.
- **Talking past each other:** arguing while using the same word to mean different things.

Side View: Scientists and philosophers argue about what science gives us. Some say good science is getting closer and closer to the truth about how the world really is, even the parts we can't see. Others say science only needs to fit what we can observe and make useful predictions, and that's enough. Your friends argue about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. Are they arguing about the hot dog, or about the word? Was Pluto the same kind of argument?
