# Module 11: This Course Is a Paradigm

This reading caught a mistake in itself. While the course was being checked, we noticed that this module's own list of anomalies misdescribed Module 5. It's fixed now. A module about the course's own blind spots had a blind spot of its own, which is roughly what Kuhn would have predicted.

## The textbook problem, from the inside

Module 1 introduced Kuhn's claim that textbooks are where a paradigm lives. They teach through **exemplars**, present the past as a straight road to the present, and quietly drop the losing side. This course is a kind of textbook. So apply the tools.

## How this course came to be

This course was designed in October 2026 for the Recess learning platform by its author, with help from an AI assistant. Every reading was written in new words, because *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* remains under copyright, and every reading was checked against sources before any learner saw it. Two choices shaped it most. Kuhn was chosen as the spine, with other philosophers as side views, a framing decision: Popper, Lakatos and Longino appear as commentators on Kuhn's story rather than as authors of their own. And the examples lean on physics, chemistry and astronomy, as Kuhn's did. Geology entered in Module 7 partly to answer that imbalance.

## Find the planted error

Fact-checking caught real errors in this course's first drafts. Here are three claims about those catches. Two are true. One was changed on purpose and is wrong. Which one? Tell Rocky your pick and your reason, then check.

1. An early Module 2 draft described the first Leyden jars as foil-coated. The first ones were partly filled with water; foil came in 1747.
2. Module 6 was planned under the title "Too Many Circles," drawing on the "epicycles on epicycles" story. Fact-checking found that story to be a modern myth, so the module kept the title with a question mark.
3. An early Module 3 draft said Edmond Halley lived to see his comet return in 1758, which "confirmed Newton's physics within his lifetime." Fact-checking found that Halley died in 1742, sixteen years before the comet came back, as Module 3 now says.

Reveal: Claim 3 is the planted error. It reads like the kind of satisfying ending that textbooks reach for — scientist predicts, scientist is vindicated, applause — and it contradicts what Module 3 plainly says. Claims 1 and 2 are real catches. The fact-checker also flagged traps the course avoided: claiming Copernicus was more accurate or used fewer circles overall (he needed fewer only to explain the backward loops, as Kuhn himself noted), claiming Thomson named the "plum pudding," and claiming Pluto was demoted for being too small. Kuhn would recognize the pattern: errors that fit the expected story are the hardest to see, just like the trick cards in Module 4.

## Is it a paradigm?

In Kuhn's broad sense, the course has a **disciplinary matrix**: a shared vocabulary (paradigm, anomaly, crisis), a set of exemplars (Halley's comet, Neptune, oxygen, the trick cards), and standards for a good answer. Notice how it trained you: by Module 9 you were probably spotting staircases and anomalies automatically. That is the power of a paradigm and its blind spot.

Why it matters now: Textbooks, news sites and AI chatbots all frame what they tell you, and this course was built with help from an AI assistant. The questions you've been asking here work on any of them.

**Weird but true:** None of the errors listed here was caught because it looked wrong. Each read smoothly and fit the story. They were caught only because someone checked them against sources, which is close to Popper's point about criticism, and Longino's.

Side View: Apply each thinker to this course. **Popper** asks: which claims here could be refuted, and did the course try to refute them? **Lakatos** asks: when the course corrected itself, did the corrections lead anywhere new, or only patch? **Longino** asks: were there real venues for criticism, and was criticism taken up? The Anomaly Log is one such venue. **Feyerabend** might ask what a course with no spine at all would look like. **Kuhn** might say: a good textbook, and therefore a hider of its own revolutions. Whose test does the course pass, and whose does it fail?
