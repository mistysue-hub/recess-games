# Module 2: Rules Nobody Wrote Down

In 1746, before King Louis XV, the French experimenter Jean-Antoine Nollet sent the shock of a single Leyden jar through a chain of 180 royal guards holding hands, and they leapt into the air together. He repeated the stunt with a long chain of monks. It was spectacular, and it settled nothing about what electricity actually was. To see why that jar mattered so much, rewind a few years, to when nobody could agree on the question.

## The rules you can't state but would notice broken

Native speakers of English almost all say "a big red ball," never "a red big ball." Very few could state the rule behind that. (Linguists have one: opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material, purpose.) Yet the wrong order sounds instantly off. You learned the rule from thousands of examples, not from a definition.

Kuhn argued that the knowledge that holds a science together is often like this: learned from examples, shared by a community, mostly unstated, and very hard to see from the inside.

## Paradigm

The word **paradigm** comes from the Greek *paradeigma*, "pattern" or "example." Before Kuhn, it was best known as a grammar term: a model verb you conjugate so you can conjugate others like it. After 1962, thanks largely to Kuhn, it became one of the most overused words in English.

Kuhn himself used it loosely. One early critic went through *Structure* and counted more than twenty different senses. In later writing Kuhn separated two main meanings:

1. **The broad sense (he later called it a "disciplinary matrix"):** everything a scientific community shares. Its basic laws and assumptions about what the world contains, its preferred methods and instruments, its standards for what counts as a real problem and a good solution, and even its sense of which results are surprising.
2. **The narrow sense (exemplars):** the concrete, worked-out solutions every student in a field learns. The inclined plane, the pendulum, the balanced chemical equation. Kuhn came to think this sense was the deeper one.

Why would worked examples matter so much? A physics student who has solved enough pendulum and inclined-plane problems starts to *see* a new problem as "basically a pendulum." No textbook states that rule. The skill of seeing resemblances is learned by doing, and it lets a community agree on what a problem is and how to attack it. Members share it so thoroughly that they rarely notice it.

## Before the jar: four camps, no common ground

In the early 1700s, electrical researchers weren't working from one framework. Different researchers treated different effects as the main event. Four common positions:

- **Attraction.** Rubbed amber and glass pull light objects; repulsion was an awkward side puzzle.
- **Conduction.** In 1729 Stephen Gray sent the "electric virtue" along a thread about 800 feet long, and showed that some materials carry it and others don't.
- **Two electricities.** In the 1730s Charles du Fay argued for two kinds, vitreous (from glass) and resinous (from amber).
- **Effluvium.** Others pictured a material stream pouring out of rubbed bodies, and studied sparks and glow.

Each camp explained its favorite effects and fumbled the rest. Because nobody could take any foundation for granted, every author had to argue the basics all over again. Observations piled up, but they didn't add up.

## A jar, a shock, a race

Then, in 1745–46, a glass jar changed the argument. Ewald von Kleist in Germany and, independently, Pieter van Musschenbroek in Leiden found that a jar partly filled with water, with a wire through its cork, could store a startling amount of charge and deliver a savage shock. Musschenbroek wrote that he wouldn't take a second shock for the whole kingdom of France. (Within a couple of years, experimenters swapped the water for metal foil.) Within a year, Nollet was zapping guards at Versailles. The jar was dramatic, and it embarrassed most existing theories.

Benjamin Franklin's **one-fluid theory** (late 1740s) handled it best. Electricity is a single fluid. A body can hold an excess of it (positive, "plus") or a deficit (negative, "minus"). In the jar, the glass keeps the two sides from evening out until a conductor connects them. (In England, William Watson reached a similar idea at about the same time.) Franklin's account didn't explain everything, but it explained the jar and much else, and it gave researchers a common starting point.

What changed afterward is the important part. Researchers stopped relitigating foundations and began working on shared, specific problems: measuring charge, improving the jar, mapping conductors. Papers got narrower and more technical, written for fellow specialists. That shift, from debate about basics to focused puzzle-work, is what Kuhn meant by a field acquiring a paradigm. (Franklin's single fluid was itself replaced later. His plus and minus signs survived.)

## Why this is controversial

Calling science a set of community-held, partly unstated commitments made many readers uneasy. If a paradigm is learned by apprenticeship rather than derived from logic and evidence, isn't science just a culture or a club? Kuhn denied saying science is irrational, but the worry followed him all his career, and you'll meet it again.

Why it matters now: Every field you'll study, from medicine to coding, still trains people on worked examples. When you learn to code by changing programs that already work, you're picking up a paradigm the same way the electricians after Franklin did.

**Weird but true:** Franklin's assignment of "positive" and "negative" was a convention made before anyone knew what carried charge. Electrons turned out to be negative, so in a wire they drift opposite to the "conventional current" still drawn in circuit diagrams. A paradigm's vocabulary can outlive the theory that chose it.

## Terms

- **Paradigm:** a scientific community's shared framework (broad sense) or its model problem solutions (narrow sense, "exemplars").
- **Exemplar:** a standard worked example that teaches students how to see and solve new problems.
- **Pre-paradigm period:** the stage of a field with competing schools and no shared foundations.

Side View: The **Vienna Circle**, a group of philosophers and scientists who met in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s (including Moritz Schlick and Rudolf Carnap), developed **logical positivism**. Its core idea, the verification principle, held that a statement is meaningful only if observation could, in principle, confirm or disconfirm it. On this view, the justification of science lies in logic plus observation, not in how scientists happen to be trained or what their community takes for granted. Those things belong to psychology or sociology, not to the logic of science. Here's a twist: Kuhn's book was first published as part of the *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science*, a series launched by logical positivists, and Carnap was one of its editors. Scientists in a field all learn from the same worked examples. Does that shared training make their results more trustworthy, or just make them think alike? Use the Leyden jar story.
