# Module 6: Too Many Circles?

On the right nights, Mars visibly stops, reverses against the stars for weeks, and resumes. For roughly two thousand years, explaining that loop was the central technical problem of astronomy, and the solutions people accepted say a great deal about how sciences change.

## The problem of the wanderers

Against the background stars, the five planets visible to the naked eye mostly drift eastward. Periodically each one slows, stops, reverses for a while, stops again, and resumes. This **retrograde motion** was the central puzzle of ancient mathematical astronomy.

Around 150 CE, Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria assembled the most complete solution in the work later called the *Almagest*. Earth sits at rest. Each planet rides on an **epicycle**, a small circle whose center travels around a larger **deferent**. Ptolemy refined this with an **eccentric** (the deferent's center offset from Earth) and his own invention, the **equant**: a point from which the epicycle's center appears to move at a steady rate. The equant broke with the old ideal of uniform circular motion about a circle's center, but it worked remarkably well. Ptolemaic astronomy produced usable predictions for well over a millennium, and astronomers in the medieval Islamic world refined it. Ibn al-Shatir in Damascus built models without the equant whose mathematics closely matches what Copernicus later used. Historians still debate whether Copernicus knew of that work or reinvented it.

## Copernicus did not win on accuracy

*De revolutionibus* (1543) placed the Sun near the center and set Earth in motion. Its great payoff was qualitative: retrograde motion stops being a separate mechanism for each planet and becomes a side effect of Earth's own orbit. Earth overtakes the outer planets; Mercury and Venus overtake Earth.

Two popular beliefs about Copernicus are false. His published system was not more accurate than Ptolemy's, and it did not use fewer circles overall. Copernicus kept uniform circular motion, replaced the equant with small extra epicycles, and ended up with a system at least as complicated to calculate with. For decades, the case for a moving Earth could not rest on better predictions.

The accuracy breakthrough came from data. Tycho Brahe's naked-eye observations were far more precise than any before. Johannes Kepler, working on Tycho's Mars data, built a circle-based model that disagreed with the observations by about 8 arcminutes, roughly an eighth of a degree. Earlier astronomers would have shrugged at that. Kepler trusted Tycho's precision more than his own model, discarded it, and in *Astronomia nova* (1609) published his first two laws: planets move on ellipses, and sweep out equal areas in equal times.

## Kuhn's crisis, and the historians' reply

In Kuhn's account, Copernicus's revolution followed a **crisis**: a period when a paradigm's puzzles resist solution for so long that practitioners lose confidence in the rules themselves. Kuhn argued that by the sixteenth century astronomers felt the Ptolemaic tradition had become unmanageable: its adjustments no longer made it more accurate. In his view, that sense of failure opened the field to a radically new approach.

Later historians have challenged parts of this. The image of Ptolemaic astronomers piling "epicycles on epicycles" appears to be a modern myth; circle counts stayed fairly stable, and Copernicus's published book needed about as many as Ptolemy's. Whether working astronomers in 1500 experienced a "crisis," or whether that label was applied afterward, is still argued. Notice the irony: a course about textbooks tidying history is now reporting that Kuhn's own history may have been tidied.

Why it matters now: Scientists still have to choose between models that fit the data about equally well, and they often lean on the same values the Copernicans did, like simplicity and how much a model explains at once.

**A complication:** Kuhn knew this material well. He wrote a full history of the subject, *The Copernican Revolution* (1957), five years before *Structure*. That is worth remembering when you weigh his crisis story against the historians' reply: he had worked in the sources, which makes the disagreement more interesting, not less.

**Weird but true:** Tycho Brahe lost part of his nose in a duel in December 1566, at age 20, after quarreling with his cousin and fellow student Manderup Parsberg, reportedly over who was the better mathematician. He wore a metal replacement for the rest of his life; legend said gold and silver, but a 2012 analysis of traces on his skull pointed to brass.

## Terms

- **Equant:** a point offset from a circle's center, from which a body's motion along the circle appears uniform.
- **Arcminute:** 1/60 of a degree.
- **Crisis (Kuhn's sense):** prolonged failure of normal science that loosens a community's commitment to its paradigm.

Side View: Imre Lakatos, in "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (1970), proposed a middle path between Popper and Kuhn. Every research programme has a **hard core** it protects and a **protective belt** of auxiliary hypotheses it adjusts. A programme is **progressive** if its adjustments lead to novel predictions that are then confirmed, and **degenerating** if adjustments only accommodate facts already known. Rationally, scientists should prefer progressive programmes, though a degenerating one can recover. By Lakatos's test, were Ptolemy's eccentric and equant progressive or degenerating? Was Copernicus's system progressive in 1543, or only later, once Kepler and Galileo built on it?
