# Module 7: Revolution: The Drifting Continents

The Atlantic Ocean is getting wider while you read this, at about the speed your fingernails grow. A hundred years ago, almost every geologist would have told you that was impossible.

## A map that looks like a puzzle

Look at a world map. The bulge of South America looks like it could tuck into the curve of Africa, like two puzzle pieces. Lots of people have noticed that. In 1912, a German scientist named Alfred Wegener decided it wasn't a coincidence.

Wegener studied weather and the atmosphere, not rocks. But in a lecture in January 1912, and in a book in 1915, he made a bold claim: long ago, the continents were joined in one giant landmass, now called Pangaea. Over millions of years, they drifted apart. He called his idea **continental drift**.

## Wegener's evidence

Wegener didn't just point at a map. He collected clues:

- **The fit.** The edges of the continents, out where the shallow sea floor drops off, fit together even better than the coastlines.
- **Fossils.** Fossils of *Mesosaurus*, a small reptile that lived in fresh water, are found in both South America and southern Africa. It couldn't have swum across an ocean. Fossils of an ancient fern-like plant called *Glossopteris* turn up on continents now far apart.
- **Rocks.** Rock types and formations on one side of the Atlantic match the other.
- **Ice and coal.** Scratches left by ancient glaciers appear in places that are hot today, like parts of Africa and India. Antarctica has coal with fossils of plants that need warmth.

## Why almost nobody believed him

Most geologists said no, and some were harsh about it. In 1926, at a big meeting in New York, most speakers argued against him.

They had real reasons. The biggest: Wegener couldn't explain *how*. What force could push a whole continent through the solid rock of the ocean floor? The forces he suggested were far too weak. He also guessed the continents were moving much faster than they really are. And he wasn't a geologist, which didn't help.

Wegener died in 1930 on an expedition across the ice in Greenland. Continental drift looked like a dead idea.

## The ocean floor talks back

After World War II, scientists mapped the ocean floor in detail. In 1952, a geologist and mapmaker named Marie Tharp noticed something in the data: a deep valley running down the middle of a huge underwater mountain range in the Atlantic. Her research partner, Bruce Heezen, brushed it off at first. Months later, he came around.

In the early 1960s, Harry Hess suggested that new sea floor forms at these underwater ridges and spreads outward. Rocks on the sea floor carry stripes of magnetism. In 1963, Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews suggested the stripes were a record of spreading, so they should match on both sides of a ridge, like a mirror. By 1966, ship surveys showed they did.

Now there was a mechanism. By the late 1960s, most geologists had switched to a new idea called **plate tectonics**: Earth's surface is broken into giant plates that move a few centimeters a year.

That's a revolution: not just a new fact, but a new way of seeing the whole Earth.

**Weird but true:** Marie Tharp mapped the bottom of the ocean without being allowed on the research ships. For years women weren't permitted to work on them, so the men collected the depth measurements and Tharp turned the numbers into maps. She finally went to sea in 1968.

## Words for your notebook

- **Continental drift:** Wegener's idea that the continents have moved over time.
- **Mechanism:** an explanation of *how* something happens.
- **Plate tectonics:** the modern theory that Earth's surface is made of moving plates.

Side View: Paul Feyerabend, an Austrian philosopher, argued in 1975 that there's no single scientific method that all good science follows. Sometimes, he said, scientists have to stick with an idea even when the evidence and the rules seem to be against it. Wegener's few supporters might be an example. Say your whole class thinks your idea won't work, but you still think it might. When should you keep going, and when should you listen?
