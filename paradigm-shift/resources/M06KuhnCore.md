# Module 6: Too Many Circles?

Go outside on the right nights and you can watch Mars do something that seems impossible. It slows down, stops, backs up for weeks, and then goes forward again. For 2,000 years, explaining that loop was one of the biggest puzzles in science.

## The wanderers

Watch the night sky for a few months and most stars march across it together, like one giant spinning bowl. But five bright lights don't keep up. The Greeks called them *planētēs*, "wanderers." Stranger still, a wanderer like Mars usually drifts east against the stars, then slows, loops backward for about two months, and then turns east again. Astronomers call that loop **retrograde motion**.

## Ptolemy's toolkit

Around the year 150, an astronomer in Alexandria, Egypt, named Claudius Ptolemy wrote a great book of astronomy. In it, Earth sits still and everything circles it. To match the wanderers, he used two main tools:

- **Big circle:** the main path the planet follows around Earth.
- **Epicycle:** a smaller circle that rides on the big one, like a seat on a spinning carnival ride. The epicycle makes the backward loops.

It worked. For more than 1,400 years, astronomers used Ptolemy's tools (plus a few clever extras) to predict where planets would be. Astronomers in the Islamic world improved his models for centuries.

## Copernicus's surprise

In 1543, Nicolaus Copernicus published a book that put the Sun near the center and set Earth moving around it. Now the backward loops had a simple story: when Earth, on its faster inside track, passes Mars, Mars seems to slide backward, like a slower car you pass on the highway.

But here's the surprise. Copernicus still used circles, and still needed small epicycles. His model **was not more accurate** than Ptolemy's. And it didn't use fewer circles overall either.

The real jump in accuracy came later. Tycho Brahe made the most careful sky measurements anyone had made without a telescope. Johannes Kepler spent years trying to fit Tycho's measurements of Mars with circles. One of his circle models missed by a tiny amount. Kepler trusted Tycho's data more than his model, so he threw the model out. In 1609 he showed that Mars moves on an **ellipse**, a stretched circle, and his predictions fit.

## Kuhn's crisis

Thomas Kuhn told this story as a **crisis**. A crisis, he said, is when a paradigm's puzzles keep failing, and scientists start to feel the old rules might be the problem. He argued that by Copernicus's time, astronomers sensed that Ptolemy's system, fixed and refixed for centuries, had grown into a tangle.

Historians since then have pushed back on part of that story. You may have heard that Ptolemy's followers kept piling "epicycles on epicycles" until the system collapsed. Historians say that's a myth: the number of circles stayed roughly the same, and Copernicus's book used about as many.

So was there really a crisis? You'll judge for yourself.

**Weird but true:** Tycho Brahe, whose measurements helped Kepler break the circles, wore a fake nose. At age 20 he lost part of his real one in a sword fight with his cousin, reportedly over which of them was the better mathematician.

## Words for your notebook

- **Retrograde motion:** when a planet seems to move backward against the stars for a while.
- **Epicycle:** a small circle carried around on a bigger one.
- **Crisis (Kuhn's sense):** a time when a paradigm's puzzles keep failing and scientists start to doubt the rules.

Side View: A philosopher named Imre Lakatos said don't judge a theory by whether it has problems, because every theory does. Ask instead: is it still predicting new things nobody expected? Or is it only making patches to fit things already known? Think of two ways to fix a bad video-game strategy: a patch that only fixes yesterday's loss, or a new idea that also wins games you haven't played yet. Which was Kepler's ellipse?
