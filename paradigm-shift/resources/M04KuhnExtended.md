# Module 4: Seeing What You Expect

What color is the six of spades? You answered without looking, and that is the point of this module. In 1949, two psychologists showed that people shown a red six of spades for a split second almost never see a red six of spades.

## An experiment Kuhn couldn't stop thinking about

In 1949 the Harvard psychologists Jerome Bruner and Leo Postman published a short paper with a large afterlife. Their subjects were 28 Harvard and Radcliffe students. Using a tachistoscope, a device that displays an image for a precisely timed instant, they flashed playing cards one at a time. Each card started at a hundredth of a second and was shown for longer and longer, up to a full second, until the subject named it correctly twice in a row.

Most cards were ordinary. Six were "trick" cards with suit and color swapped: a black three of hearts and a black four of hearts, a red two of spades and a red six of spades, a black ace of diamonds, a red six of clubs.

## Four ways of seeing a card that shouldn't exist

Ordinary cards were recognized at about 28 thousandths of a second on average. Trick cards needed about 114, roughly four times longer. The way subjects got there is the interesting part. Bruner and Postman described four kinds of reaction:

- **Dominance.** Nearly every subject (27 of 28) at first reported a trick card as a normal one. Either the shape won (a red six of spades seen as a six of spades) or the color won (seen as a six of hearts). The anomaly was absorbed into an existing category without being noticed.
- **Compromise.** About half the subjects, shown a red spade, reported something in between: purplish, brownish, blackish-brown. Expectation and input were averaged into a color neither one predicted.
- **Disruption.** Sixteen subjects at some point lost confidence entirely, unsure of the color, the suit, or even whether it was a playing card.
- **Recognition.** Correct identification often came suddenly, after a growing sense that something was wrong. Some said the symbols looked wrong, or "reversed," before they could say why.

Some trick cards were never correctly identified, even at a full second; about one in ten trick-card presentations stayed unrecognized at the longest exposure. And experience mattered: after recognizing one trick card, subjects recognized the next much faster. Practice with normal cards gave no such boost.

## The scientist as card-reader

Kuhn used this experiment as a model of scientific discovery. A paradigm supplies expectations, and expectations make perception fast and efficient. The cost is that genuine novelty first appears as nothing at all, or as a normal case slightly off. It takes time, a sense of wrongness, and often some failure before an anomaly is seen for what it is. Then, as with the subjects who caught one trick card, the whole category of the unexpected becomes visible.

Kuhn went further. After a revolution, he said, scientists don't simply interpret the same data differently. In some sense they see differently, the way a drawing that looked like a duck abruptly becomes a rabbit. He called this a **gestalt switch**, while admitting the comparison was only partly apt: unlike someone staring at the drawing, a scientist can't simply choose to see it the old way again.

Why it matters now: Experts still miss what they aren't looking for. In a 2013 study, 20 of 24 radiologists searching lung scans for small nodules failed to notice a picture of a gorilla, about 48 times the size of a typical nodule, placed in one of the scans.

**A complication:** The 1949 result has also been questioned. Later critics suggested that slow answers might reflect hesitation about reporting something odd, not different seeing. The wider debate, whether expectations change perception itself or only judgment, is still alive: in 2016 Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl argued that none of the many reported top-down effects on perception is yet compelling evidence.

**Weird but true:** The duck-rabbit first appeared, unsigned, in the German humor magazine *Fliegende Blätter* on October 23, 1892. The psychologist Joseph Jastrow used it, and the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein made it famous as an example of "seeing as." Kuhn borrowed it for scientific revolutions.

## Terms

- **Tachistoscope:** an instrument that displays an image for a precisely controlled, very short time.
- **Theory-laden observation:** the idea that what we observe is shaped by the concepts and theories we already hold.
- **Gestalt switch:** a sudden reorganization of how the same input is perceived.

Side View: Norwood Russell Hanson, in *Patterns of Discovery* (1958), argued that seeing is not a neutral recording of light. He used ambiguous figures, like the drawing that can be seen as a young woman or an old one, to argue that what we perceive is organized by what we already know. A trained physicist and a beginner looking at the same apparatus don't see the same thing. Hanson's view sharpens a problem for Popper's method from Module 3: if observations are shaped by theories, can an observation ever be a neutral judge between two theories? A doctor and a first-year student look at the same X-ray. Whose observation should count as evidence, and could the student ever see something the doctor's training hides?
