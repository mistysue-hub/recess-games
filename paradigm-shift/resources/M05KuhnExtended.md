# Module 5: The Puzzle That Won't Solve

On December 28, 1612, and again on January 27, 1613, Galileo plotted a faint object near Jupiter in his notebook. It was Neptune. He took it for a fixed star; it was barely moving at the time. Neptune would wait 234 years to be found on purpose, and the search began with a planet that refused to follow the rules.

## Uranus misbehaves

William Herschel discovered Uranus on March 13, 1781, the first planet found since antiquity. Astronomers soon computed its orbit from Newton's laws, and in 1821 Alexis Bouvard published detailed tables of its expected positions. Within a couple of decades, Uranus had drifted measurably off those tables.

Faced with an anomaly like this, a scientist can question the paradigm, or question something else. Questioning Newton, whose mechanics had already predicted Halley's comet, seemed far less reasonable than supposing something unseen was at work.

## Rescue, version one: Neptune

In 1845–46 Urbain Le Verrier in Paris and John Couch Adams in Cambridge independently calculated the position of an undiscovered planet whose gravity could account for Uranus's wandering. Le Verrier published his result and sent it to the Berlin Observatory. On the night of September 23–24, 1846, Johann Galle, assisted by Heinrich d'Arrest, found the planet within an hour of starting the search, less than a degree from Le Verrier's predicted position.

Neptune turned an anomaly into a triumph. It became the textbook example of a scientific prediction. (How much credit Adams deserves is still debated; his work was mostly unpublished at the time.)

## Rescue, version two: Vulcan

Mercury's orbit is an ellipse whose closest point to the Sun, its perihelion, slowly rotates. Newtonian gravity, counting the pulls of the other planets, accounts for nearly all of that rotation. Le Verrier found a stubborn remainder, later fixed at about 43 arcseconds per century. (One arcsecond is 1/3,600 of a degree.)

In 1859 Le Verrier proposed the same cure that had worked for Uranus: unseen matter inside Mercury's orbit. That year, an amateur astronomer, Edmond Lescarbault, reported seeing a small body cross the Sun, and in January 1860 Le Verrier announced the planet, named Vulcan. Sightings came and went for decades. None held up.

In 1915 Einstein's general theory of relativity predicted Mercury's extra 43 arcseconds with no new planet at all. The anomaly had been pointing at a limit of Newton's theory itself.

## What the two stories teach together

The two rescues used exactly the same reasoning. One succeeded spectacularly and one failed completely. That is Kuhn's central point about anomalies. Scientists do not, and should not, abandon a successful paradigm at the first mismatch; the mismatch is usually a puzzle for normal science, and often it gets solved. But there is no rule that tells you in advance which anomalies will yield to a fix and which ones mark the edge of the paradigm. Mercury's anomaly was known for more than half a century before a new theory replaced Newton's.

Rescues of this kind keep happening. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli proposed an undetectable particle to explain why energy seemed to go missing in a type of radioactive decay, rather than give up conservation of energy. The neutrino was finally detected in 1956. In 2011 the OPERA experiment reported neutrinos apparently arriving faster than light; in 2012 the cause turned out to be a fiber-optic cable that wasn't fully plugged in. Sometimes the fix is a new particle. Sometimes it's the equipment. Sometimes, as with Mercury, it's the theory.

Why it matters now: Astronomers face their own "Neptune or Vulcan?" choice today. Galaxies spin as if they hold far more mass than we can see. Most physicists blame unseen "dark matter," a Neptune-style rescue; a minority think gravity itself needs changing, as it did for Mercury. The question is still open.

**A complication:** Mercury's drift was not only a choice between Vulcan and a new theory. In 1894 the astronomer Asaph Hall suggested adjusting Newton's inverse-square law by a tiny amount instead. Each rescue attempt cost something different, which is why "save the theory" is not one move but a menu.

**Weird but true:** When Galileo first recorded Neptune, the planet had just begun its retrograde loop and was nearly stationary, too slow to stand out in his small telescope. One physicist argued in 2009 that he may have noticed it shift, though others doubt it. If so, he saw an anomaly and filed it as noise.

## Terms

- **Anomaly:** an observation that violates the expectations of the current paradigm.
- **Auxiliary hypothesis:** an extra assumption used alongside a theory to make a prediction (for example, "there is no planet beyond Uranus").
- **Perihelion precession:** the slow rotation of a planet's orbit, measured at its point closest to the Sun.

Side View: Pierre Duhem (*The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory*, 1906) and W. V. O. Quine ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism," 1951) argued that hypotheses are never tested in isolation. A prediction always depends on a bundle of assumptions: the theory, the number of planets, how the instruments work, and more. When the prediction fails, logic alone doesn't say which assumption to blame. This **Duhem–Quine thesis** is why the Neptune rescue was legitimate, and also why a determined defender can save almost any theory. It is a problem for Popper's falsification from Module 3: if one failed prediction never forces you to drop a particular theory, what does? Imagine you're an astronomer in 1880. Mercury's orbit is still off, and after twenty years of searching nobody has found Vulcan. How much longer do you keep searching before you suspect Newton?
