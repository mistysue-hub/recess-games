# Module 5: The Puzzle That Won't Solve

In December 1612, Galileo pointed his telescope near Jupiter and drew a faint dot in his notebook. He thought it was a star. It was actually the planet Neptune. Nobody would find Neptune on purpose for another 234 years, and it took a planet that wouldn't behave to do it.

## A planet off course

In 1781, the astronomer William Herschel discovered a new planet, Uranus. Astronomers used Newton's laws to work out exactly where it should be, year after year. But Uranus wouldn't cooperate. By the 1840s it was clearly drifting away from where the math said it should be.

That's an **anomaly**: a result that doesn't fit what your paradigm expects. Astronomers had two choices:

1. Newton's laws are wrong.
2. Newton's laws are fine, and something we can't see is pulling on Uranus.

## The rescue

Two mathematicians, Urbain Le Verrier in France and John Couch Adams in England, chose option 2. Each worked out where an unseen planet would have to be to pull Uranus off course. Le Verrier sent his prediction to the Berlin Observatory.

On the night of September 23, 1846, the astronomer Johann Galle pointed his telescope where Le Verrier said to look. In less than an hour he found a planet that wasn't on any star map, less than one degree from the predicted spot. It was Neptune.

The anomaly became one of Newton's greatest victories.

## The same trick, again

Then Le Verrier noticed another problem. Mercury, the planet closest to the Sun, doesn't trace exactly the same oval every time. Its whole orbit slowly turns. Newton's laws explained most of that turning, but not all. A tiny bit was left over (today we know it's 43 arcseconds every hundred years). (An arcsecond is a very small piece of a degree.)

Le Verrier tried the trick that had worked so well. In 1859 he suggested an unseen planet even closer to the Sun. It was named Vulcan. That same year, an amateur astronomer said he'd seen it crossing the Sun. For decades, astronomers searched. Vulcan was never found.

In 1915, Albert Einstein published a new theory of gravity called general relativity. It explained Mercury's leftover 43 arcseconds exactly, with no extra planet at all. Mercury's anomaly hadn't been a missing planet. It was a crack in Newton's laws.

## Why scientists don't give up right away

Kuhn's point: an anomaly almost never makes scientists drop their paradigm right away. First they try to fix it, and often they should. The Neptune rescue worked brilliantly. The Vulcan rescue failed. At the start, nobody could tell which kind of anomaly they were looking at.

Only some anomalies refuse every fix. Those are the ones that can push a science into crisis, which is what Module 6 is about.

**Weird but true:** Galileo looked straight at Neptune in 1612 and wrote "a star" in his notebook. The actual eighth planet was in his telescope. His paradigm said planets move; Neptune happened to be nearly still that night (just starting its own backward loop); so his brain filed it as a star. The right thing was right there and he missed it.

## Words for your notebook

- **Anomaly:** a result that doesn't fit what the paradigm expects.
- **Rescue:** explaining an anomaly by adding something new (like an unseen planet) while keeping the paradigm.
- **General relativity:** Einstein's 1915 theory of gravity, which replaced Newton's for some problems.

Side View: Pierre Duhem (and later W. V. O. Quine) argued that when a prediction fails, you can always blame one of its parts (your telescope, the number of planets) instead of the main theory. When would you stop looking for the lost shoe and admit someone moved it?
