5Don't Crash

He designed a flying machine, then spent his notes worrying about how it would fail.

About 3 min read

Most people who dream up a flying machine think about takeoff. In the same group of flying notes, Leonardo is worried about something else: the crash. What happens when the machine breaks, or tips over in the air?

In his own words

TO ESCAPE THE PERIL OF DESTRUCTION.

Destruction to such a machine may occur in two ways; of which the first is the breaking of the machine. The second would be when the machine should turn on its edge or nearly on its edge, because it ought always to descend in a highly oblique direction, and almost exactly balanced on its centre.

He sees exactly two ways for it to be destroyed: the machine snaps, or it flips onto its edge. His fixes are simple. Make it as strong as possible, and keep it coming down on a long slant, balanced on its middle, like a glider.

A backpack of bags

Then comes a note that sounds like a joke, but is not:

In his own words

Bags by which a man falling from a height of 6 braccia may avoid hurting himself, by a fall whether into water or on the ground; and these bags, strung together like a rosary, are to be fixed on one's back.

A braccio was about an arm's length, so 6 of them is roughly a 12-foot fall. Leonardo's idea: a string of cushion bags, like beads on a necklace, strapped to your back. It is a crash-protection backpack from the 1400s.

Why he believed it could work

In his own words

An object offers as much resistance to the air as the air does to the object. You may see that the beating of its wings against the air supports a heavy eagle in the highest and rarest atmosphere ... Again you may see the air in motion over the sea, fill the swelling sails and drive heavily laden ships.

Look at his proof. Air is a real thing you can push on, and it pushes back. He points at what he can see: a heavy eagle held up by its wings, and sails that drag giant ships across the sea. If wind can do that, why not wings for a person?

The sketch next to it

The man who edited the English edition, Jean Paul Richter, adds a footnote to this page of notes:

Richter's footnote

A parachute is here sketched, with an explanatory remark.

Leonardo drew a parachute in his notebooks, shaped like a pyramid. Here is a simple diagram of the idea. It is a modern drawing to show how it works, not Leonardo's own sketch.

500 years later

In 2000, a skydiver named Adrian Nicholas rode a hot-air balloon up to about 10,000 feet and jumped, wearing a pyramid-shaped parachute built from a Leonardo-inspired design made of wood and canvas. It worked. He cut away from it at about 2,000 feet and landed with a modern parachute.

Words to know

braccia
plural of braccio, an old Italian measure about the length of an arm
oblique
slanted, not straight up and down
resistance
the push-back you feel when you move something through air or water
