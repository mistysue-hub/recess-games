14Seashells on a Mountain

Seashells, hundreds of miles from the sea. How did they get there?

About 4 min read

Here is a puzzle Leonardo could not stop thinking about. Climb high into the mountains near his home, dig into the rock, and you can find seashells. Real seashells, layer after layer of them, far from any sea.

In Leonardo's day, almost everyone had a ready answer: the great Flood from the Bible must have carried the shells up there. Leonardo did not accept an answer just because everyone repeated it. He tested it with logic. First, he pointed out what the Flood story would predict:

In his own words

If you were to say that the shells which are to be seen within the confines of Italy now, in our days, far from the sea and at such heights, had been brought there by the deluge which left them there, I should answer that if you believe that this deluge rose 7 cubits above the highest mountains--as he who measured it has written--these shells, which always live near the sea-shore, should have been left on the mountains; and not such a little way from the foot of the mountains; nor all at one level, nor in layers upon layers.

In other words: a flood would leave shells scattered all over, not sorted into neat layers. Then came his best argument, and it is a funny one. What if the shellfish simply crawled up the mountain, following the rising water?

In his own words

...that the cockle is an animal of not more rapid movement than the snail is out of water, or even somewhat slower; because it does not swim, on the contrary it makes a furrow in the sand by means of its sides, and in this furrow it will travel each day from 3 to 4 braccia; therefore this creature, with so slow a motion, could not have travelled from the Adriatic sea as far as Monferrato in Lombardy, which is 250 miles distance, in 40 days ...

Do the math

A braccio was about the length of a man's arm, roughly 2 feet, so a cockle moves about 6 to 8 feet a day. 250 miles is about 1,320,000 feet. At 6 to 8 feet a day, that is somewhere around 450 to 600 years, not 40 days.

He had one more clue, and this one came from actually looking at the rock:

In his own words

...for in these mountains living ones are found, which are recognisable by the shells being in pairs; and they are in a layer where there are no dead ones

Shells still joined together in pairs, in their own quiet layer, not scattered and broken the way a flood would leave them. Leonardo's conclusion was that this ground had once been the bottom of the sea, and that the sea floor had slowly been lifted up into mountains. That is very close to what geologists explain today. He got there 500 years ago using careful looking and a little arithmetic.

The cave

Leonardo did not only think at a desk. In another note, he describes going out to find the strange shapes nature makes, and standing at the mouth of a cave:

In his own words

having wandered some distance among gloomy rocks, I came to the entrance of a great cavern, in front of which I stood some time, astonished and unaware of such a thing ...

two contrary emotions arose in me, fear and desire--fear of the threatening dark cavern, desire to see whether there were any marvellous thing within it

Fear and desire at the same time. That might be the best description of curiosity ever written.

Words to know

deluge
a great flood
braccia
plural of braccio, an old Italian measure about the length of an arm
cockle
a small shellfish with a ribbed shell
