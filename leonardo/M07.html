Engineer for Hire

He was applying for a job. Painting came almost last.

About 3 min read

In his notebook, Leonardo kept a rough draft of a letter to Lodovico Sforza, the ruler of Milan. It is a job application. He lists what he can do for the duke. Count what he mentions first.

Item 1 of his letter

1) I have a sort of extremely light and strong bridges, adapted to be most easily carried, and with them you may pursue, and at any time flee from the enemy; and others, secure and indestructible by fire and battle, easy and convenient to lift and place. Also methods of burning and destroying those of the enemy.

Item 6 of his letter

6) Item. I will make covered chariots, safe and unattackable which, entering among the enemy with their artillery, there is no body of men so great but they would break them. And behind these, infantry could follow quite unhurt and without any hindrance.

Bridges. War machines. Covered chariots that crash through armies. Leonardo is not pitching himself as an artist. He is pitching himself as an engineer who can win battles. Now look at where the art shows up, near the very end:

In his own words

10) In time of peace I believe I can give perfect satisfaction and to the equal of any other in architecture and the composition of buildings public and private; and in guiding water from one place to another.

Item: I can carry out sculpture in marble, bronze or clay, and also in painting whatever may be done, and as well as any other, be he whom he may.

Painting is tucked in at the end, almost as an afterthought. And his closing line is the bold one:

In his own words

And if any one of the above-named things seem to any one to be impossible or not feasible, I am most ready to make the experiment in your park, or in whatever place may please your Excellency

Translation: you do not believe me? Fine. Pick a spot, and I will show you.

A bridge as big as a stadium

Leonardo really did dream big about bridges. In another note, he sketched a giant bridge for the city now called Istanbul:

In his own words

The bridge of Pera at Constantinople, 40 braccia wide, 70 braccia high above the water, 600 braccia long; that is 400 over the sea and 200 on the land, thus making its own abutments.

~1,200 ftlong (600 braccia)
~140 ftabove the water (70 braccia)
~80 ftwide (40 braccia)

A braccio was roughly two feet, so those are rough conversions. Either way: huge for the year 1502, when he made the plan.

500 years later

In 2001, a footbridge in Ås, Norway opened that was built from Leonardo's design. It is about 109 meters long and made of laminated wood. The original was never built in Leonardo's day, but his drawing finally got its bridge.

Words to know

braccia
plural of braccio, an old Italian measure about the length of an arm
abutment
the solid support at each end of a bridge
artillery
large weapons, such as cannons, used in battle
