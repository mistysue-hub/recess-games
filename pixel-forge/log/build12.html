Eleven builds. Five finished games. You've written state machines, lists, timers, collision, tilemaps, a class system, physics, a save file, a settings screen. There's no new engine feature in this build, because you don't need one. What you need is the thing every junior dev learns the hard way: the game you can finish is better than the game you can imagine. Make a new folder, build12-your-game. Unzip the starter kit into it; it holds an empty state machine and a design sheet, and nothing else.

Step 1Design, on one page

Open DESIGN.md from the kit and fill it in before you type a line of Lua. One page. These headings, no more:

  • Title and your studio name (yes, you have one now; pick it).
  • The pitch, one sentence. "You are a lighthouse keeper who throws fish at seagulls." If it takes two sentences, the game is too big.
  • The three states, named. Every game you built had at least title, playing and done; yours will too.
  • The list or grid. What's the collection of things? Rocks, tiles, crates, wisps. Name it and say what each one holds.
  • What's saved or loaded. A high score file, or levels in text files. Pick one.
  • Art and sound plan. Kenney pack, your own pixels, shapes only. Which sounds, and where they come from.
  • A sketch of the playing screen. Pencil, photo it, or draw boxes in any app. Ugly is fine. Missing is not.

Show the sheet to the lead dev before Step 3. Not after. A design doc you write after the game is a lie.

Step 2Scope: the rule of three

Jamie's capstone, years ago, was an open-world fishing RPG with crafting. Jamie shipped a title screen. Here is the rule that would have saved it:

Three sittings, three features, three states. If your game can't be playable, start to finish, in three sittings of work, cut a feature. Then cut another. The features you cut go in a LATER.md file so they don't feel dead; they're just not in version 1.

A good capstone is one mechanic you already built, used in a way you haven't. Meteor Drift's lists, but the rocks are words and you type them. Tower Hop's gravity, but the platforms are your own drawings. Circuit Breaker's grid, but it's a garden. Small and finished beats big and broken every single time, in this studio and in every other one.

Step 3Build, in the order that keeps it running

The kit's main.lua is a state machine with empty rooms. Fill them in this order, running after every step, exactly as every Build Log did:

  1. Title state: your studio name, your game's title, "press space." Run it.
  2. Playing state with one thing on screen that you control. Run it.
  3. The list or grid, drawn. Run it.
  4. The rule that makes it a game: a goal, a fail, a score. Run it.
  5. The done state, with restart. Run it.
  6. Saving or loading. Run it.
  7. Sound. Run it.
  8. Art, last. A game that works with rectangles is a game. Art is paint.

Here's the shape of the file you're starting from, so you know the rooms:

function love.load()
  state = "title"
  -- your tables go here
end

function love.update(dt)
  if state == "playing" then
    -- your rules go here
  end
end

function love.draw()
  if state == "title" then
    -- studio name, title, press space
  elseif state == "playing" then
    -- the world
  elseif state == "done" then
    -- score, restart
  end
end

Every bug you hit, you've hit before. The cheat sheet is open in a tab. Build Logs 01 to 11 are a click away. The lead dev points at lines and never writes them; that hasn't changed.

Step 4Playtest with one human

When it runs start to finish, hand it to one real person: a parent, a sibling, a friend who has LÖVE installed (package it as a .love file, Build 11, Step 5). Then do the hardest thing in game development: say nothing. No "press space." No "you have to go left." Watch. Write down three things you saw:

  1. The first thing they did that you didn't expect.
  2. The moment they looked confused, and what was on screen.
  3. The thing they said when they stopped.

Those three lines go at the bottom of DESIGN.md under Playtest. Then fix one thing, just one, that the playtest showed you. Not the thing you wanted to fix. The thing they tripped on.

Step 5The pitch

Two minutes, on screen share, to the lead dev. In this order: the title screen, one sentence on what it is, play it for sixty seconds, show the thing you fixed after the playtest, and say what's in LATER.md. That's a pitch. People get jobs with less.
