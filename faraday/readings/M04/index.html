Ten Years, Then a Ring (1831)

After the spinning wire, Faraday wrote a line in the notebook where he kept ideas to chase: convert magnetism into electricity. If a current could make a magnet move, surely a magnet could make a current.

That line sat there for ten years.

Ten years of nothing

Every so often he would try. He wound coils of wire, set the strongest magnets he could find next to them, connected the most delicate current detector in London, and watched. Nothing. He put the magnet inside the coil. Nothing. He tried bigger magnets, more wire, better detectors. Nothing, nothing, nothing.

Other scientists across Europe were trying the same thing and getting the same result, so Faraday was not alone in failing. He was just more stubborn about it. (An American schoolteacher named Joseph Henry saw the same jump in Albany at about the same time and published later; the unit of induction is named after him, as a consolation.)

The problem was that everyone, Faraday included, was looking for a steady current from a steady magnet. They were watching the detector after they set things up. The interesting moment had already passed.

29 August 1831

Faraday had an iron ring made, about six inches across. On one side he wound a coil of wire connected to a battery. On the other side, a second coil, connected to a detector. The two coils never touched; iron was the only thing between them.

He connected the battery. The detector needle jumped, and then settled back to zero.

He disconnected the battery. The needle jumped the other way, and then settled back to zero.

For ten years the answer had been there, flashing by in the instant a current was switched on or off, and everyone had been looking at the wrong moment. A magnet that stays the same does nothing to a coil. A magnet that is changing makes a current, for exactly as long as the change lasts.

Weird but true

Faraday's diary entry for that day is numbered 1. For eleven years he had dated his entries without numbering them; on the morning of the ring he started counting. He kept counting, with one restart the next year, for the rest of his working life, and the numbers eventually passed 16,000.

Testing the idea

Faraday did what he always did: he made it simpler to be sure. Within weeks he had thrown away the battery and the ring. He pushed a plain bar magnet into a coil of wire by hand. The needle jumped. He held the magnet still inside the coil. The needle sat at zero. He pulled the magnet out. The needle jumped the other way.

That is the whole law, and it is the one you will test today.

  1. Push the magnet in: a current, one way.
  2. Hold it still: nothing.
  3. Pull it out: a current, the other way.
  4. Move it faster, use more loops of wire or a stronger magnet: a bigger current.

In Faraday's picture, the coil is being cut by lines of force. A line sweeping across a wire pushes the charges in it along. No sweeping, no push.

He called it electromagnetic induction. The ring he used is still at the Royal Institution, in a glass case, looking like something a blacksmith made in an afternoon, because it is.

Verdict: the current is in the change.

Why this mattered more than the motor

The motor turned electricity into motion. Induction did the reverse: motion into electricity. Together they meant that electricity could be made anywhere there was something to spin, sent down a wire, and turned back into motion at the other end.

Nobody in 1831 could see all of that. Faraday could see some of it. Next module is where the rest shows up.

Words for your diary

  • Induction: making a current in a wire by changing the magnetic field around it.
  • Coil: wire wound round and round, so one magnet cuts many turns at once.
  • Detector: in Faraday's day, a galvanometer: a compass needle near a coil, which twitched when current flowed. Today, a light bulb does the same job in the simulation.
