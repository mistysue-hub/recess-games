Faraday the Chemist

People remember Faraday for electricity, but the Royal Institution hired him as a chemist, and he was a very good one. Before the spinning wire and long after it, he spent his days with glass tubes, foul smells and small explosions.

A liquid that shouldn't exist

In 1823 Davy suggested an experiment: seal a chlorine compound in a glass tube and heat it. Faraday did, and a visiting doctor named Paris, walking past, teased him for using a dirty tube with oil in it. Faraday looked closer. The "oil" was chlorine. A yellow-green gas that everyone knew only as a gas had turned into a liquid. In the next weeks he did it to half a dozen more. Tubes burst, glass flew, and Faraday got splinters in his eyes more than once. He kept going.

Until then nobody was sure a gas like chlorine could be a liquid. Faraday showed that a gas is just a liquid that hasn't been squeezed or cooled enough. That idea runs every refrigerator in the world.

The stuff in the lamp gas

In 1825 he was handed a barrel of oily gunk left over from making lamp gas and asked what was in it. He pulled out a new, clear liquid with a sweet smell and a ring of six carbon atoms, though nobody would know about the ring for forty years. He called it bicarburet of hydrogen. We call it benzene. It's in plastics, dyes, medicines and gasoline. It's also poisonous, which Faraday, sniffing it in a basement, did not know.

Electricity takes chemicals apart

Davy's great trick had been to run a current through melted chemicals and pull out brand-new metals. Faraday wanted to know the rules. In 1833 and 1834 he measured, carefully, how much chemical came apart for a given amount of electricity.

He found two rules so clean they are still called Faraday's laws of electrolysis.

  1. The amount of stuff that comes apart is exactly in proportion to how much electricity goes through. Twice the electricity, twice the stuff.
  2. For the same electricity, different chemicals give amounts that match their chemical combining weights.

Hidden inside those rules was a thought Faraday couldn't quite finish: electricity seemed to come in fixed amounts tied to atoms. Sixty years later that idea got a name, the electron.

The simplest electrolysis is pulling water apart. Two metal rods in water with a pinch of something dissolved in it (pure water barely conducts; that's what the word electrolyte is for, a few paragraphs down), a current through them, bubbles on both. Water is H₂O: two hydrogens for every oxygen. Before you run today's experiment, decide which rod you'd expect to bubble faster, and by how much.

The negative rod, where hydrogen appears, bubbles about twice as fast as the positive rod, where oxygen appears. Two hydrogens for every oxygen, two volumes of gas for every one. Faraday's second law, in a glass of water.

Letters to a word-maker

Faraday needed names for the parts of his experiment, and he hated the ones in use, which were tangled up with old theories he didn't believe. So in 1834 he wrote to his friend William Whewell, a scholar at Cambridge who was famous for inventing words. (Whewell also invented the word scientist. Before him, there wasn't one.)

Faraday already had electrode (the metal rod the current enters or leaves by), electrolyte (the liquid the current travels through) and electrolysis (the taking-apart itself), worked out with another friend, a doctor named Nicholl. Whewell added, from Greek, anode (the positive rod, from "way up," the way the sun rises), cathode (the negative one, "way down") and ion ("goer," the charged bit that travels through the liquid).

Faraday worried the new words were clumsy and that people would misread them. Whewell told him they were short and simple and would catch on. Every chemistry book on Earth now uses all six.

Verdict: the laws are exact. The words will do.

Weird but true

Whewell built the words from Greek on purpose, so they'd belong to no modern language in particular. It worked. A Japanese chemist, a Brazilian chemist and a Scottish chemist all say "anode."

Name That Word

Decide what each word means before you show the card.

Which word means the charged bit that travels through the liquid?

Ion, from the Greek for "going." Faraday imagined it walking from one rod to the other.

Which word is the rod where current enters, named for the way the sun comes up?

Anode, the positive electrode. "Ana" means up; "hodos" means way.

Which word is the liquid that lets current through by coming apart?

Electrolyte. "Lysis" means loosening or breaking apart, the same root as in "analysis."

Words for your diary

  • Electrolysis: using an electric current to split a liquid into its ingredients.
  • Liquefy: to turn a gas into a liquid, by squeezing it, cooling it, or both.
  • Benzene: the sweet-smelling ring of six carbons that Faraday pulled out of lamp-gas sludge in 1825.
