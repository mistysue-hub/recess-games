Twelve Feet of Tinfoil (1836)

In January 1836, Faraday had a wooden frame built in the Royal Institution's lecture theater. It was a cube, twelve feet on a side, and he covered it in copper wire, tinfoil and wire mesh until it was a metal box big enough to walk around in. Then he had an electrical machine pump charge into it until sparks jumped off the outside.

And he climbed in, carrying his most sensitive instruments.

Inside the box

Faraday wrote afterward that he could find no trace of electricity inside, "though all the time the outside of the cube was powerfully charged." He walked around in there with his detectors while the surface crackled, and the detectors read nothing at all.

This was not a stunt. It was the end of a careful chain of experiments on how charge spreads through metal, and the answer was always the same: charge on a conductor runs to the outside surface and stays there. Never inside.

So the inside of any closed metal box is a quiet place, however violent it is outside. We call such a box a Faraday cage.

Why it works, in lines of force

Picture lines of force running through the room, left to right, from a charged plate on one side to a plate on the other. (This is an electric field: the same idea as the magnet's field from the lines-of-force page, but made by electric charge instead of a magnet, and it pushes on charges instead of on compasses.) Now drop a hollow metal box into the middle.

Metal is full of charges that can move. The lines of force push on them. Negative charges slide to the side of the box the lines are coming from; positive charges pile up on the side the lines are going to. Those piled-up charges make lines of force of their own, pointing the opposite way, right through the inside of the box.

The charges keep sliding until the two sets of lines cancel exactly. They have to: as long as any line is left inside the metal it is still pushing on the charges, so they are still sliding, and the only place they can come to rest is where nothing is left pushing. Zero. One more rule finishes the job: a line of force never starts or ends in empty space; it runs from a charge to a charge. So any line left in the hollow would have to run from the wall to the wall, and that is a push on the wall's charges too, so they keep moving until those lines are gone as well. Inside the box: nothing. Outside, the lines bend and end on the box's surface, as if the box had swallowed them.

The box doesn't have to be solid metal. A net of wire works, as long as the holes are small compared with the wavelength of whatever is trying to get in: to a wave, a hole much smaller than itself is no gap at all. A microwave oven's waves are about twelve centimeters long and the holes in the door are a millimeter, so the waves stay in; visible light is thousands of times shorter than the holes, so you can see the popcorn. Faraday's cube was a wooden frame, copper wire, paper and strips of tinfoil.

Now turn it inside out. Put a charged ball inside a metal box instead. Its lines have to end somewhere, and the nearest charges are in the wall. Does anything show up outside the box? Today's simulation will tell you; decide first.

Weird but true

Seven years later Faraday did the inside-out version on a tabletop with a pewter ice pail (a bucket), a charged brass ball on a silk thread and an electroscope, a charge meter made of two thin metal leaves that spread apart. Lowering the ball into the bucket made the leaves spread; touching it to the inside wall changed nothing on the meter, but the ball came out with no charge left. The bucket had taken it all. Physics classes still do the Faraday ice pail.

Cages you have been inside

A car in a lightning storm is a cage. The lightning runs over the metal skin and down to the ground; the people inside are in the quiet place. (It's the metal, not the rubber tires, that protects you. The bolt has just crossed a mile of air; an inch of tire is not going to slow it down. It jumps from the body to the ground.) An airplane is a cage; they get struck about once a year each and keep flying. The microwave oven in your kitchen is a cage turned inside out: the mesh in the door keeps the microwaves in. And an elevator is a cage by accident, which is why your phone loses its signal in one. The signal is a wave of electric and magnetic lines of force. Its electric part drives the metal's charges back and forth, and the currents they make throw the wave back off the surface, so almost nothing gets through. (A plain static magnet is the one thing a copper cage does nothing about: a compass inside still points north, because a magnet that isn't changing drives no current in the copper. Make the magnet wiggle, though, and the copper fights back; that's the induction rule from the ring, read as a shield.)

Verdict: no trace inside. Build a bigger cube.

Words for your diary

  • Faraday cage: any closed box of conducting material. The inside is shielded from outside electric fields.
  • Conductor: a material whose charges can move freely. Metals are conductors; glass, rubber and dry air are not.
  • Shielding: blocking a field from reaching something. The cage shields its inside; the microwave door shields the kitchen.
