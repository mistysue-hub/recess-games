The Cage and the Lightning (1836)

In January 1836, Faraday had a wooden frame built in the Royal Institution's lecture theater. It was a cube, twelve feet on a side, and he covered it in copper wire, tinfoil and wire mesh until it was a metal box big enough to walk around in. Then he had an electrical machine pump charge into it until sparks jumped off the outside.

And he climbed in, carrying his most sensitive instruments.

Inside the box

Faraday wrote afterward that he could find no trace of electricity inside, "though all the time the outside of the cube was powerfully charged." He walked around in there with his detectors while the surface crackled, and the detectors read nothing at all.

This was not a stunt. It was the end of a careful chain of experiments on how charge spreads through metal, and the answer was always the same: charge on a conductor runs to the outside surface and stays there. Never inside.

So the inside of any closed metal box is a quiet place, however violent it is outside. We call such a box a Faraday cage.

Why it works, in lines of force

Picture lines of force running through the room, left to right, from a charged plate on one side to a plate on the other. Now drop a hollow metal box into the middle.

Metal is full of charges that can move. The lines of force push on them. Negative charges slide to the side of the box the lines are coming from; positive charges pile up on the side the lines are going to. Those piled-up charges make lines of force of their own, pointing the opposite way, right through the inside of the box.

The charges keep sliding until the two sets of lines cancel exactly. Inside the box: nothing. Outside, the lines bend and end on the box's surface, as if the box had swallowed them.

The box doesn't have to be solid metal. A net of wire works, as long as the holes are small compared with the wavelength of whatever is trying to get in. A microwave oven's waves are about twelve centimeters long and the holes in the door are a millimeter, so the waves stay in; visible light is a thousand times shorter than the holes, so you can see the popcorn. Faraday's cube was a wooden frame, copper wire, paper and strips of tinfoil.

Before you run the simulation, decide: if the outside field got stronger, would the inside stay at zero, or would some leak through?

It stays at zero. Stronger field, more charge slides to the walls, bigger cancellation. The charges always move until the inside is quiet. A perfect conductor shields perfectly, however strong the field.

Weird but true

Seven years later, in 1843, Faraday did a tabletop version with a pewter ice pail (a bucket), an electroscope and a charged brass ball on a silk thread. As he lowered the ball in, the electroscope's reading climbed, then stopped changing once the ball was a few inches below the rim, however much deeper he went. When he let the ball touch the inside wall, the reading didn't change at all, but the ball came out with no charge left. That little bucket experiment is still done in physics classes, and it's still called the Faraday ice pail.

Cages you have been inside

A car in a lightning storm is a cage. The lightning runs over the metal skin and down to the ground; the people inside are in the quiet place. (It's the metal, not the rubber tires, that protects you.) An airplane is a cage; they get struck about once a year each and keep flying. The microwave oven in your kitchen is a cage turned inside out: the mesh in the door keeps the microwaves in. And an elevator is a cage by accident, which is why your phone loses its signal in one. The signal is a wave of electric and magnetic lines of force, and the metal box swallows the electric part, which is enough to kill it. (A plain static magnet is the one thing a cage does nothing about: a fridge magnet sticks just as well inside a steel box.)

Verdict: no trace inside. Build a bigger cube.

Words for your diary

  • Faraday cage: any closed box of conducting material. The inside is shielded from outside electric fields.
  • Conductor: a material whose charges can move freely. Metals are conductors; glass, rubber and dry air are not.
  • Shielding: blocking a field from reaching something. The cage shields its inside; the microwave door shields the kitchen.
