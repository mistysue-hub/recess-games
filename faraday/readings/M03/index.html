The Spinning Wire (1821)

In the summer of 1821 a magazine editor asked Faraday for a favor. Every lab in Europe was fiddling with Ørsted's discovery, and nobody could agree on what it meant. Would Faraday write a clear history of the whole mess?

Faraday said yes, which meant repeating every experiment himself. He didn't trust a result he hadn't seen with his own eyes.

Something nobody had noticed

As he worked through the experiments, Faraday fixed on something the others had seen but not followed up. A compass needle near a current-carrying wire didn't point at the wire and didn't point away from it. It pointed sideways, as if the force were running in circles around the wire.

That was strange. Every push or pull anyone knew about, from gravity to a magnet grabbing a nail, acted in a straight line between two things. This force went round and round.

Faraday reasoned like this. If the force around a wire runs in a circle, then a magnet's pole, placed near the wire, should be pushed around that circle. And if the pole is held still, the wire should go round the pole instead. Not a swing, not a twitch: a circle, for as long as the current flows.

3 September 1821

In the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution, with his brother-in-law George watching, Faraday set it up. A bar magnet stood upright in a bowl of mercury, which is a liquid metal that conducts electricity. A stiff wire hung from above so that its tip dipped into the mercury and could swing freely. He connected a battery.

The wire began to circle the magnet, and kept circling, round and round, like the hand of a clock that had lost its mind. (If you've ever built a motor from a battery, a screw and a magnet, that's this. It even has a name: a homopolar motor.)

Faraday shouted "There they go! There they go!" and that evening took George, who was fourteen, to the circus to celebrate. (That's George's memory, told years later, not the diary.) In his diary he wrote: "Very satisfactory, but make more sensible apparatus."

It was the first time in history that electricity had been turned into continuous motion. Every electric motor since, in every fan, drill, train, toy and car, is a descendant of that wire in a bowl.

Weird but true

Faraday made pocket-sized versions of the apparatus, sealed in little glass tubes, and mailed them to scientists across Europe, so they could watch the wire spin for themselves instead of taking his word for it.

The quarrel

Faraday published fast, within a month. He did not mention that his boss, Humphry Davy, and Davy's friend William Wollaston had tried to make a wire rotate earlier that year and failed. Wollaston's idea had been different, and wrong, but the gossip spread that the bookbinder's boy had stolen it.

Davy, who had hired him, did not defend him. Faraday wrote to Wollaston offering to apologize, kept all his notes, and set out in print exactly what Wollaston had tried and why it was not the same thing. Wollaston, to his credit, told him not to worry about it. Two years later, when the Royal Society voted to make Faraday a Fellow, Davy had argued openly against it, and there was exactly one vote against. The ballot was secret. Everyone assumed whose it was.

Faraday never said a bad word about Davy in public for the rest of his life.

Verdict: very satisfactory. Make more sensible apparatus.

Why the wire circles

Today's experiment lets you switch the current on and off and turn on the field lines, so you can see the circles Faraday reasoned about. Here is what to look for.

  1. Around the wire, the lines of force are circles, like rings on a target. Not straight lines, not spokes.
  2. The magnet has its own lines, looping from one end to the other.
  3. The magnet's lines push on the current in the wire, sideways: at right angles to both the current and the magnet's field. Sideways everywhere means round and round.

Try this before you run it. If you reversed the battery, so the current ran the other way, which way would the wire go?

The other way round. Reverse the current and the sideways push reverses too, so the wire circles in the opposite direction. Flip the magnet instead and the same thing happens.

Words for your diary

  • Electromagnetic rotation: Faraday's name for continuous motion made by a current and a magnet. The first electric motor.
  • Mercury: a metal that is liquid at room temperature and conducts electricity. Very poisonous, which is why nobody does this experiment in a bowl anymore.
  • Credit: in science, saying who had the idea first. Faraday learned the hard way that leaving it out costs you.
