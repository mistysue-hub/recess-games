Magnetising a Ray of Light (1845)

On 13 September 1845 Faraday put a lump of glass between two filters that blocked all light, switched on the biggest electromagnet in the building, and light came through. It should not have. Nobody had ever made a magnet do anything to light. Here is how he got there, and why it took him twenty years.

By 1845 he was 53, famous, and tired. His memory had failed him for a while in his late forties; nobody is sure why, though overwork and thirty years of chemical fumes are the usual guesses, and he had spent about four years doing almost no experiments. When he came back to the lab, he picked up the question he had been chewing on for two decades. Electricity and magnetism were one thing. Was light part of it too?

A hard thing to test

The trouble was finding anything a magnet could do to light. Light goes straight through a magnetic field as if it isn't there. Faraday tried for years: light through magnetized air, light past electrified wires, light through water between magnet poles. Nothing.

What he needed was a property of light delicate enough to show a tiny change. He found it in polarization.

Light is a wave, and it wobbles sideways as it travels, the way a jump rope wobbles. Ordinary light wobbles in every sideways direction at once. A polarizing filter is like a picket fence: it only lets through the wobble that lines up with its slats. Two filters with their slats crossed let nothing through at all. Line them up, and the light comes back.

That gave Faraday a test. Cross two filters so the light is blocked. Put something between them. If anything twists the light's wobble, even a little, some light will leak through the second filter. Dark means no twist. Any brightness means a twist.

Before the experiment, make a guess: with the two filters crossed, if you turn the second one slowly, does the light come back gradually, or all at once?

Gradually. The light gets brighter and brighter as the slats turn toward lining up, and brightest when they match. Turning to 45 degrees gives about half the light. That smooth change is what makes polarization such a sensitive detector.

13 September 1845

Faraday set a glass rod between crossed filters, inside a powerful electromagnet. He used a special heavy glass he had made himself twenty years earlier for a different project, so dense that it bent light unusually hard.

He switched on the magnet. Light came through.

The magnet had twisted the light's wobble. Turn the magnet off and the twist vanished. Reverse the magnet and the twist went the other way. A stronger magnet, a bigger twist. It is now called the Faraday effect, and it was the first time anyone had shown that light and magnetism had anything to do with each other.

Faraday wrote in his diary that day that he had at last succeeded "in magnetising a ray of light." For a man who graded his experiments "very satisfactory," that counts as shouting.

Weird but true

Sugar water also twists polarized light, but bounce the beam off a mirror and send it back through the way it came and the twist undoes itself. Faraday's glass doesn't play fair: send the light back through and the twist doubles. Nobody could explain that for decades. Today it's used to build one-way doors for laser light.

Weird but true

The heavy glass only existed because of a failed project. In the 1820s the government, through a Royal Society committee, set Faraday to making better glass for telescopes. He spent about five miserable years at it, the glass was never good enough for telescopes, and he kept a few lumps on a shelf. One of them turned out to be exactly the thing that would twist light. Faraday's rule: never throw out a failed experiment.

What it meant

Faraday could not explain the effect. He suspected that light was some kind of vibration in the lines of force themselves, so that squeezing the lines with a magnet would twist the vibration. He said so in a lecture, carefully, and most of the physicists of the day thought he had gone a bit far.

Twenty years later James Clerk Maxwell did the math and showed that light is exactly that: a wave of electric and magnetic fields, traveling through space at a speed his equations predicted. Maxwell wrote that he was only putting Faraday's pictures into symbols. Radio, Wi-Fi, X-rays and every color you can see are the same wave at different sizes, and the first clue was a lump of failed glass in a magnet.

Verdict: I have magnetized a ray of light.

The man who said no

Faraday let it be known that if anyone offered him a knighthood, he would say no; plain Mr. Faraday was fine. He was offered the presidency of the Royal Society, twice. No, and no. When the government asked his opinion on a plan to use poison gas in the Crimean War, he gave a cool, careful technical answer, pointed out the defenders could simply wear masks, and took no part in it. He kept giving the Christmas Lectures for children until he was too old to stand up for a whole evening. He died in 1867 in a cottage the Queen had lent him, and asked for a plain gravestone, which he got.

Words for your diary

  • Polarization: the sideways direction a light wave wobbles in. A filter picks one direction and blocks the rest.
  • Faraday effect: a magnetic field twisting the polarization of light passing through a material along the field.
  • Electromagnetic wave: Maxwell's name for what light turned out to be: electric and magnetic fields vibrating together, moving through space.
