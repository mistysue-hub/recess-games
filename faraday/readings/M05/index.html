Make Your Own Electricity

Two months after the induction ring, Faraday built a machine. It was a copper disk, about a foot across, mounted on an axle so it could spin between the poles of the biggest magnet in London, borrowed from the Royal Society. One wire touched the axle; another touched the rim.

When he turned the crank, a steady current flowed out of the disk. Not a jump and a flop back to zero: a steady current, for as long as he kept cranking.

It was the first generator. He had turned his own muscle into electricity.

How a generator works

Think back to the lines of force. The magnet's lines run across the gap between its poles. The copper disk spins through that gap, so every part of the copper is constantly cutting lines. Cutting lines pushes charges along. As long as the disk keeps spinning, the lines keep being cut, and the current keeps flowing.

That's it. That's a generator. Spin a conductor through lines of force, or spin a magnet past a coil (same thing, from the coil's point of view), and you get current.

Here's a test of whether you believe it. Which would give you more current: a bigger magnet, a faster spin, or more turns of wire in the coil?

All three. A stronger magnet means more lines to cut. A faster spin means the lines get cut more often each second. More turns means each line cuts more wire on the way past. The simulation lets you change each one and watch the bulb.

What use is it?

There is a famous story, and it is almost certainly a legend. A politician visits Faraday's lab, watches the little generator, and asks what possible use it could be. Faraday replies that he doesn't know, but one day the government will tax it.

Nobody has ever found that conversation written down anywhere, so treat it as a joke scientists like to tell. Here is the true version, which is better: every power station on Earth is Faraday's copper disk, grown up. Coal, gas and nuclear fuel do one thing in a power station: they boil water. (Solar panels are the odd ones out: no steam, no spinning, no Faraday at all; that's a diary page for another day.) The steam spins a turbine. The turbine spins a magnet. The magnet spins past coils. Lines of force get cut. The current goes down the wire to your house. Hydroelectric dams skip the boiling and let falling water spin the turbine directly, which is what today's simulation shows.

Weird but true

The electricity in your wall outlet goes back and forth about 60 times every second (50 in Europe). That isn't a flaw. It's the magnet in the power station spinning past the coil: current one way as the north pole goes by, the other way as the south pole follows. Faraday's "jump one way, jump the other" from the induction ring never went away. It's just happening miles from you, sixty times a second, forever.

So why did Faraday's copper disk give a steady one-way current instead? Because every spoke of the disk is always cutting the lines the same way, from axle to rim, all the way round. A coil sees north go past, then south; a disk sees the same thing forever. Engineers call Faraday's disk a homopolar generator, the twin of the homopolar motor from the 1821 module, and almost nobody builds one, because the coil kind is so much easier to make.

The transformer

Faraday's iron ring from last module turned into something too. Remember: a coil with current on one side, a second coil on the other, iron between them. Feed the first coil a current that goes back and forth (which, as the card above says, is what the power station makes anyway) and the second coil gives a back-and-forth current of its own, with no wires connecting them.

Make the second coil with more turns than the first and you get a higher voltage out than you put in. Fewer turns, lower. That is a transformer, and it is why the power station can send electricity across the country at voltages that would vaporize a toaster, then step it down to something safe before it reaches your kitchen. Those gray cans on power poles are iron rings with two coils.

Faraday built the first one while hunting for something else, and noticed what it was really doing. That was his gift.

Verdict: the whole grid is one disk and one ring.

Words for your diary

  • Generator: a machine that turns motion into electric current by moving a conductor through lines of force (or moving the lines past it).
  • Turbine: a wheel of blades, spun by steam, water or wind, that turns the generator.
  • Transformer: two coils on an iron core that change the voltage of a current, with no wires between them.
