# Module 1: The Textbook Lie

In 1775, an English minister breathed a brand-new gas on purpose, just to see what it would do to him. He was right that the gas was special. He was wrong about what it was. And the way most books tell this story is a little bit wrong too.

## Who discovered oxygen?

Open almost any science book and you might read: "Joseph Priestley discovered oxygen in 1774." One person, one year, done.

It's a neat sentence, and it leaves out most of what really happened. Here's the messier story.

**Around 1771 or 1772,** a Swedish chemist named Carl Wilhelm Scheele heated some chemicals and collected a gas that made flames burn brighter. He called it "fire air." His book didn't come out until 1777.

**In August 1774,** an English minister and chemist named Joseph Priestley heated a red powder and collected a gas. A candle burned in it with an amazingly strong flame.

**In October 1774,** Priestley visited Paris and told a French chemist, Antoine Lavoisier, about his gas.

**Early in 1775,** back in England, Priestley tested the gas some more. A mouse in a jar of it stayed lively much longer than in ordinary air. Priestley breathed some himself.

But Priestley didn't think he had found a new element. Chemists back then believed that anything burning gave off an invisible stuff called *phlogiston* (say it: flo-JISS-tun). Priestley decided his gas was air with the phlogiston taken out. He called it "dephlogisticated air."

Lavoisier repeated the experiments and came to a completely different conclusion. There is no phlogiston, he argued. This gas is a basic ingredient of the air, an element. When things burn, they grab it instead of giving something off. He named it *oxygen*. Priestley never agreed. For the rest of his life, he kept saying phlogiston was real.

So who discovered oxygen? Scheele made it first. Priestley made it and told the world first. Lavoisier was the first to understand it as we do today. Three people, three different pieces.

## Why do science books tidy things up?

Science books teach today's ideas fast, with no room for every wrong turn. So they line up the past like a staircase, each scientist adding one more step.

A historian of science named Thomas Kuhn thought that staircase picture was a big problem. In 1962, he published *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*. Its big idea: science doesn't just pile up facts, one on top of another. Every so often, scientists change the whole set of rules they use to see the world. Kuhn called those changes *revolutions*. Oxygen replacing phlogiston was one of them.

And here's the sneaky part. After a revolution, the new books make the revolution almost invisible. Priestley becomes "the man who discovered oxygen," as if he agreed with us all along.

**Weird but true:** In 1767, Joseph Priestley lived next to a brewery, used the gas bubbling up from the beer vats, and worked out how to make fizzy water.

## Words for your notebook

- **Phlogiston:** an invisible stuff that chemists once believed burning things gave off. It turned out not to exist.
- **Revolution (in science):** a big change in the rules scientists use to see the world.
- **Textbook history:** the tidy, staircase version of the past that science books often tell.

Side View: In 1620, an English thinker named Francis Bacon said science should gather careful observations and build them up into laws, so knowledge grows like a pile of facts. Kuhn's picture is different: sometimes the pile gets knocked over and rebuilt. Think of LEGO bricks: keep adding forever, or sometimes knock it down and build it a new way? Which fits the oxygen story better?
