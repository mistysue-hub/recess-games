# M02 notes (Rocky only — never open on screen or read out)

## Hook answer
No single right answer. The reading says scientists learn much of their paradigm by example, the way people learn a game by watching and trying.

## Talk-back (one at a time, after the look-back)
1. Before Franklin, why did every electricity book have to start from scratch?
   Answer: researchers didn't share a paradigm. Each had their own theory, so nobody could take the basics for granted.
2. What's one "unwritten rule" in a game, sport or class you know that you learned by example?
   Any honest example counts.

## Side View question
Core: Think of a card game your friends play with house rules nobody wrote down. Does a rule book explain how it's really played, or do you have to learn it from the group? (The Vienna Circle wanted only logic and checkable observations; Kuhn said how the group is trained matters too.)
Extended: Scientists in a field all learn from the same worked examples. Does that shared training make their results more trustworthy, or just make them think alike? Use the Leyden jar story.
Opinion question: no right answer. Stay neutral.

## Game: Guess the Hidden Rule
The learner sees cards that fit a secret rule and cards that don't, taps "Fits" or "Doesn't fit" for six new cards, then picks the rule from three choices. Then the rule secretly changes for four more cards; the game asks what they thought when their predictions missed, explains anomalies, and asks them to pick the new rule. The last screen is "Notebook closed" with scores and a Copy button.
Stuck nudge: "Compare a card that fits with one that doesn't. What's different?" Never say the rule.

## Activity 2 (only if the activity count is two)
Ask the learner to explain how they figured out the hidden rule, without saying the rule. Then ask: was that more like reading a rule book or like learning a game by watching?

## Anomaly Log
Core: "Is there a kind of person, or a kind of science, that this story about electricity never mentions? Who might be missing? Write it in your log, or just tell me."
Extended: "Whose voice, or which science, is missing from this module's story? Add it to your log, then tell me one thing you wrote."
The page shows the course's own Anomaly line only after the learner taps the reveal button (it asks for their own guess first). Any honest answer counts, including "nothing this time."
