# Module 10: Kuhn vs. Popper, 1965

On October 25, 1946, at a Cambridge philosophy club, Karl Popper and Ludwig Wittgenstein argued so heatedly that, in Popper's telling, Wittgenstein brandished a fireplace poker before storming out. Witnesses' accounts conflict, and the episode may have been dramatized in the retelling. Popper liked an argument. In 1965 he had another one, with Kuhn.

## Bedford College, July 1965

The International Colloquium in the Philosophy of Science met at Bedford College, London, from July 11 to 17, 1965. Its central symposium put Kuhn's *Structure*, then three years old, before Karl Popper and the circle around him at the London School of Economics. Paul Feyerabend could not attend, and John Watkins stepped in for him and for Lakatos as a main speaker. The papers, much expanded, appeared in 1970 as *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*, edited by Imre Lakatos and Alan Musgrave.

## What they agreed on, and didn't

The disagreement is easy to exaggerate. Kuhn and Popper both rejected the view that science grows by piling up verified facts; both gave revolutions a central place; both treated testing against experience as essential.

Their real conflict was over **normal science**. Popper's contribution, "Normal Science and its Dangers," granted that normal science exists but called it a danger to science and even to civilization. In his view, the normal scientist has been taught badly, trained to apply rules rather than to criticize them. For Popper, the scientific attitude is permanent criticism, and a scientist should always be trying to refute their best theory.

Kuhn's answer was that permanent criticism would be fatal. A community that abandoned its framework at every anomaly would never develop any framework far enough to discover which anomalies matter. Commitment, even dogmatism up to a point, is what makes deep testing possible: the Neptune rescue required trusting Newton. In Kuhn's account, the criticism Popper praised mostly happens during crises, not every day.

Critics, Lakatos among them, charged that Kuhn had made theory change a matter of "mob psychology." That charge followed Kuhn for years. In the 1970s, David Bloor and Barry Barnes at Edinburgh went further than Kuhn. Their **Strong Programme** in the sociology of science proposed that the same kinds of causes, social ones included, should explain both true and false scientific beliefs. Kuhn distanced himself sharply from this, most clearly in a lecture published in 1992 as "The Trouble with the Historical Philosophy of Science."

## Kuhn's answer: shared values

In a 1973 lecture, published in 1977 as "Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice," Kuhn gave his most direct reply. Scientists choosing between theories share five standard criteria: **accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness.** They are real and they matter. But they work as values, not algorithms. Applied to the same case they can conflict, and individual scientists can weigh them differently.

Take the case Kuhn himself used, Copernicus. His Sun-centered model made no better predictions than the old one until Kepler reworked it. A moving Earth also clashed with the physics everyone accepted at the time. Its pull was elegance: the planets' backward loops fell out of Earth's own motion, instead of needing extra machinery for each planet. Kepler and Galileo found that elegance persuasive. Many careful astronomers did not, and they weren't being foolish. For Kuhn, that variability is not a flaw in scientific rationality. It is how a community spreads its bets while a new paradigm is still unproven.

Why it matters now: When expert advice changes, as it has about dietary fat or about resting after a concussion, people have the same argument Kuhn and Popper did: should experts hold steady until the evidence is overwhelming, or keep revising as they go?

**A complication:** Kuhn was not the only one under pressure in that room. John Watkins, Popper's colleague, wrote a paper at the same meeting arguing against Kuhn's normal science. The debate was more than a duel: several people pressed different objections.

**Weird but true:** The poker incident is a small lesson in doing history. Smart, attentive eyewitnesses in the same room gave accounts that can't all be true. And the most widely told version is the one Popper himself wrote down, nearly three decades later.

## Terms

- **Normal science (Popper's critique):** routine work that, for Popper, risks replacing criticism with training.
- **Theory choice:** how a community comes to accept one theory over a rival.
- **Strong Programme:** an Edinburgh-based approach in sociology of science that seeks the same kinds of causal explanations for true and false beliefs.

Side View: Helen Longino, in *Science as Social Knowledge* (1990), relocates objectivity from individuals to communities. No scientist is free of background assumptions. A community's methods are objective to the degree that they permit **transformative criticism**: there must be public venues for criticism, responsiveness to it, shared standards, and tempered equality of intellectual authority among participants. Her view takes Kuhn's point that science is a social enterprise, and Popper's point that criticism is essential, and makes the quality of a community's criticism the test.
