# Module 3: Puzzle-Solving

Edmond Halley made a prediction he knew he would never get to check. A comet, he said, would come back in 1758. Halley would have been 102 years old by then.

## A puzzle with a promise

When you open a jigsaw puzzle, you know three things before you start. There is a picture. All the pieces fit. And if you can't finish it, the problem is you, not the puzzle.

Thomas Kuhn thought most of the science that scientists do every day works the same way. He called it **normal science**. Inside a paradigm, scientists work on puzzles the paradigm promises can be solved. Their job isn't to question the paradigm. Their job is to finish the picture.

## The comet that came back

In the late 1600s, the English astronomer Edmond Halley studied old records of comets. Three of them, seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682, had very similar paths across the sky. Halley guessed they weren't three comets at all. They were one comet, coming back about every 76 years.

Halley used Isaac Newton's new laws of gravity to work out its path. He predicted the comet would return in 1758. Halley died in 1742, so he never got to find out.

As 1758 got closer, three French mathematicians took on the puzzle, including Nicole-Reine Lepaute. They knew the giant planets Jupiter and Saturn would tug on the comet and slow it down. For months they worked through huge calculations by hand. They predicted the comet would make its closest pass by the Sun in mid-April 1759.

On Christmas night, 1758, a German farmer who loved astronomy spotted the comet. It made its closest pass by the Sun on March 13, 1759, about a month off from the prediction. Today we call it Halley's Comet.

## What nobody was doing

Here's the interesting part. Nobody in this story was trying to find out whether Newton's laws were right. Everyone already trusted them. The question was only: can we do the math well enough to get the date right?

If the comet had been very late, the mathematicians would have checked their math first. That's what Kuhn meant by a puzzle. The rules are trusted, so a failure points back at the puzzle-solver.

Kuhn didn't think this made scientists dull. Normal science makes measurements sharper and sharper. And because scientists expect the pieces to fit, they notice when one really doesn't. You'll see why that matters in Module 5.

**Weird but true:** Mark Twain was born in 1835, two weeks after Halley's Comet came closest to the Sun. In 1909 he said he expected to "go out with it" when it came back. He died on April 21, 1910, one day after its next closest pass.

## Words for your notebook

- **Normal science:** everyday science done inside a paradigm, solving puzzles the paradigm says have answers.
- **Puzzle (Kuhn's sense):** a problem that is sure to have a solution, where failing means the solver needs to try again.
- **Perihelion** (PAIR-ih-HEE-lee-un): a comet's or planet's closest point to the Sun.

Side View: Karl Popper, an Austrian-British philosopher, had a very different idea. In a 1934 book he said what makes an idea scientific is that it could be proven wrong. "All swans are white" is scientific, because one black swan would prove it false. Popper thought good scientists should try hard to prove their own ideas wrong, not just protect them. He worried that normal science teaches scientists to stop questioning. Say you think your friend is the fastest runner in class. Would you go looking for a race she might lose, or just keep timing her wins? Who sounds more like good science, Popper or Kuhn?
