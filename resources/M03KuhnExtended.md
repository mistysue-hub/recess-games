# Module 3: Puzzle-Solving

Edmond Halley made a prediction he could not live to see tested: a comet would return in 1758, when he would have been 102. Everyone who checked it would be relying on rules Halley had helped to spread. He had even paid to print Isaac Newton's great book of physics in 1687.

## Science without questioning the rules

Picture a scientist. You may imagine someone daring to overturn everything we know. Kuhn argued that this picture gets most science backward. Almost all the time, scientists are not testing their field's basic ideas. They are using those ideas to solve problems.

Kuhn called this **normal science**, and compared it to puzzle-solving. A real puzzle comes with a guarantee: a solution exists, the rules for reaching it are known, and if you fail, the failure is yours. A crossword clue with no answer isn't a hard puzzle. It's a broken one.

Inside a paradigm, Kuhn said, the important problems come with that guarantee. He sorted normal science into roughly three kinds of work:

- measuring more precisely the facts the paradigm says matter;
- making the paradigm's predictions match nature more closely;
- filling in the paradigm's open details and extending it to new cases.

None of these asks whether the paradigm is true. All of them assume it.

## The return of the comet

The cleanest example comes from the century after Isaac Newton. In 1705 Edmond Halley argued that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 were one object on a long, stretched orbit, returning about every 76 years. Using Newton's mechanics, he predicted its return in 1758. He died in 1742.

The return became a test of skill, not of Newton. In the late 1750s the French mathematician Alexis Clairaut, working with Joseph Lalande and Nicole-Reine Lepaute, calculated how the gravity of Jupiter and Saturn would delay the comet. The work took months of hand computation. They predicted perihelion, its closest approach to the Sun, for mid-April 1759.

Johann Palitzsch, a German farmer and amateur astronomer, saw it first, on Christmas night 1758. Perihelion came on March 13, 1759, about a month earlier than the forecast. That was a triumph for the calculation, and it made Newton's mechanics look stronger than ever. But notice what was really at stake. If the comet had arrived a year late, the first suspect would have been the arithmetic, or some unknown pull, never Newton's laws.

## Why normal science isn't boring

It's easy to hear "normal science" as an insult. Kuhn meant the opposite. Because a paradigm tells scientists which problems matter and promises they're solvable, it lets them dig deeply into small, hard questions they would never attempt if they were still arguing about basics. Precision instruments, careful measurements and detailed calculations all come from normal science.

It also sets up revolutions. A community that expects every piece to fit is the community best placed to notice when one doesn't. Without sharp expectations there are no surprises. That is the subject of Module 5.

## How people actually test ideas

In 1960 the psychologist Peter Wason gave volunteers three numbers, 2, 4, 6, and told them the numbers fit a secret rule. Volunteers could propose other sets of three and be told whether each fit. Most quickly formed a guess, like "even numbers going up by two," and then tested only sets that fit their guess: 8, 10, 12; 20, 22, 24. Every test said yes, so they felt sure. Many announced rules far more specific than the real one.

You'll try Wason's task yourself in this module's game.

Why it matters now: Most science today is still puzzle-solving inside a framework. Weather forecasts, drug trials and particle colliders all take their basic theories for granted and work on the details. And Wason's trap is still easy to fall into: it's tempting to search only for evidence that agrees with you.

**A complication:** Wason's result has been read in two ways. Later psychologists, notably Joshua Klayman and Young-Won Ha in 1987, argued that testing cases that fit your hypothesis is often a sensible shortcut, because the things we look for are usually rare. The trap is sharpest when the true rule is broader than your guess. Is that a bias, or a good habit with a known failure?

**Weird but true:** Mark Twain was born on November 30, 1835, two weeks after that year's perihelion of Halley's Comet. In 1909 he said he expected to go out with it. He died on April 21, 1910, the day after its next perihelion.

## Terms

- **Normal science:** research inside a paradigm, solving problems the paradigm guarantees are solvable.
- **Puzzle (Kuhn's sense):** a problem with an assured solution and known rules, where failure reflects on the solver.
- **Falsifiable:** able to be shown false by some possible observation.

Side View: Karl Popper (1902–1994), in *Logik der Forschung* (1934; in English as *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959), argued that what separates science from non-science is **falsifiability**. A scientific theory must forbid some possible observations. No number of white swans proves "all swans are white," but one black swan refutes it. Popper thought good scientists should state bold theories and then try hard to refute them. From that angle, Kuhn's normal scientist, who takes the paradigm for granted, looked worrying: Popper later wrote that normal science was a danger to science. Wason studied individuals, not scientific communities, but his volunteers behaved more like Kuhn's picture than Popper's ideal: they looked for confirmation, not refutation. Wason's volunteers tested only guesses that could say yes. Is that a flaw people can fix, or just how everyone works until something surprises them?
