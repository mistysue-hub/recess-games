# Module 9: Progress Toward What?

Ernest Rutherford later said that one result from his laboratory was about as believable as a heavy artillery shell bouncing back off tissue paper. That result reshaped the atom. It was one step in a century of models, and the question this module asks is what all those steps were heading toward.

## A century of atoms

| Model | Date | What it explained |
|---|---|---|
| Dalton | 1803–1808 | Elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios, because they are made of indivisible atoms of characteristic weight. |
| Thomson | 1904 | Atoms contain electrons (discovered 1897), embedded in a diffuse positive charge. The "plum pudding" name probably came from popular writers, not Thomson. |
| Rutherford | 1911 | The Geiger–Marsden experiments (key result 1909) showed a few alpha particles bouncing back from gold foil: positive charge and most of the mass sit in a tiny nucleus. |
| Bohr | 1913 | Electrons occupy fixed energy levels; jumps between them produce hydrogen's exact spectral lines. It failed for atoms with more electrons. |
| Schrödinger | 1926 | Wave mechanics replaces orbits with orbitals, probability distributions with no definite path. |

Read as a staircase, this is the textbook picture of progress. Each step explains what the last could not. Rutherford's nucleus even created a new problem, since classical physics says an orbiting electron should radiate energy and spiral inward, and Bohr's fixed levels were partly an answer to it.

## Evolution without a goal

*Structure* ends with an analogy to Darwin. What made Darwin's theory hard to accept, Kuhn argued, was not evolution itself but evolution without a goal. Organisms don't evolve toward a predetermined perfect form; they evolve away from less successful ones. Kuhn suggested science works the same way. Later paradigms are better puzzle-solvers than earlier ones, so there is genuine progress. But we may not need, and may not be able to justify, the idea that science is closing in on a final, finished description of nature. Progress from, not progress toward.

## Kuhn loss

If progress were simple accumulation, nothing would ever be lost. The philosopher Heinz Post coined the term **Kuhn loss** for problems or explanations that a new paradigm abandons. An example often given: René Descartes's vortex theory explained why all the planets orbit in the same direction and roughly the same plane, because they are carried by one swirling vortex. Newton's gravity, which replaced it, could account for the orbits once they existed but offered no reason for that shared direction; Newton attributed the system's arrangement to an intelligent designer. A question the old theory answered became, for a long time, a question the new one didn't try to answer.

The atom has smaller losses of its own. Bohr's model gave an intuitive, drawable picture; quantum mechanics traded it for far better predictions. Teachers still draw Bohr atoms, and chemists still reason with Dalton's ratios, because a model can be wrong in detail and right for the job.

Why it matters now: Your phone's GPS has to correct for Einstein's relativity to stay accurate, yet engineers still design bridges with Newton's laws. An old model that's good enough for the job doesn't disappear.

**A complication:** The staircase hides a long fight over whether atoms were real at all. Into the early 1900s, respected chemists such as Wilhelm Ostwald held that science could do without atoms. Jean Perrin's measurements of jittering particles in liquid around 1908 helped change minds, and Ostwald conceded in 1909. Models of the atom were competing with a view that said there might be nothing to model.

**Weird but true:** In the gold-foil experiments, almost every alpha particle passed through with little deflection. The rare large-angle bounces were what mattered. Most of the data agreed with the old model; the anomaly was in the few.

## Terms

- **Model:** a simplified representation used to explain and predict.
- **Kuhn loss:** an explanation or problem-solving ability lost when one paradigm replaces another.
- **Verisimilitude:** Popper's term for a theory's degree of closeness to the truth.

Side View: Karl Popper held that even false theories can differ in **verisimilitude**, closeness to the truth, so a sequence of refuted theories can still approach truth. Larry Laudan, in *Progress and Its Problems* (1977), rejected truth as the measure. We can never tell how close we are to a truth we don't have, he argued. So progress should be judged by problem-solving: how many important problems a theory solves, minus the ones a change leaves unsolved. Laudan's view takes Kuhn loss seriously; Popper's has trouble with it. Bohr's atom is wrong in detail, yet teachers still draw it. Does a model that's wrong but useful count as progress?
