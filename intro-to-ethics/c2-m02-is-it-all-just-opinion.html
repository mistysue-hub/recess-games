    Thirty-second recap

    Last case asked where right and wrong come from: is something good because it's commanded, or commanded because it's good? This case asks a sharper question. Is there a right answer at all, or is morality just opinion: yours, mine, or your culture's?

    Two tools from Challenge 1 come along. An argument is valid when the conclusion has to be true if all the premises are. A valid argument with a false premise is called unsound: it proves nothing. And if you reject a valid argument's conclusion, you owe us the premise you reject.

    Spoiler-free promise: this file won't tell you which side wins. It will make sure that whichever side you pick, you pick it for a reason that survives a push.

    Three kinds of claim

    A claim can be totally false and still be a fact claim.

    Before we can ask whether morality is "just opinion", we need to know what the other options are. Here are three kinds of claim people make all day:

    Fact claimAbout how the world is. It's true or false whether anyone likes it, and in principle evidence could settle it. "Sharks are fish" (true). "Bats are blind" (false, and still a fact claim).
    Value claimAbout what's morally good or bad, right or wrong, what people should or shouldn't do. "Cheating is wrong." "Everyone deserves a fair hearing."
    Taste claimAbout what pleases you. "This song slaps." "Olives are disgusting." Even with a big word like "best", it's really a report on your likes.

    Quick tap: "This song slaps." Could you be wrong about that?

    Many people say no, and that's the mark of a taste claim. Test 3 below turns it into a tool.

    Here's the live question. A popular picture, which Appiah calls positivism (after a group of 20th-century philosophers), says there are really only two piles: facts, which can be true or false, and everything else, which is feeling or desire. On that picture, value claims are secretly taste claims. "Bullying is wrong" would just be a fancy "I really don't like bullying."

    The philosopher Kwame Anthony Appiah (born 1954, raised in Ghana; his 2006 book Cosmopolitanism is the reading for MIT's version of this lecture) argues the two piles don't hold up. His best evidence is hiding in ordinary words.

    Exhibit A: words that do two jobs

    Call a remark cruel, a person brave, a tip stingy. Each word describes (it tells you what happened) and judges (it says whether that was good or bad) at the same time. And you can get it wrong: if someone calls a gentle joke "cruel", you can show them they've misdescribed it. Philosophers call these thick terms (Bernard Williams coined the label in 1985). Words that only judge, like good, wrong and should, are thin.

    So a sentence with a thick moral word is a fact claim and a value claim at once. Where's the positivist's line now?

    Three quick tests when you're sorting a claim
    1. Could evidence, in principle, settle it? (Points to fact.)
    2. Is it about moral right or wrong, good or bad, or should? (Points to value. Not "good" as in tasty or stylish: that's taste.)
    3. If two people disagree, does someone have to be making a mistake? Not for taste: if you love olives and I hate them, neither of us has messed up.

    The trap to watch for: "people argue about it, so it must be opinion." People argue about whether the Moon landing happened. That doesn't turn it into a taste.

    Two relativisms (don't mix them up)

    About 2,500 years ago, a Greek historian told the story of a king who ran a culture-clash experiment.

    Herodotus tells it in his Histories. King Darius of Persia asked some Greeks what it would take for them to honor their dead fathers the way a people called the Callatians did: by eating the bodies, as a funeral rite of respect. No amount of money, they said. Then he asked some Callatians what it would take for them to honor their dead the Greek way, by burning them. They were horrified. Herodotus's moral, borrowing a line from the poet Pindar: custom is king.

    Quick tap: Greeks or Callatians: whose funeral custom is right?

    Many people pick "neither". Hold that thought: it's the best case for relativism. The rest of this screen asks how far it stretches.

    Stories like this lead to two very different claims, both called relativism:

    Exhibit B: the two relativisms

    Descriptive relativism: different cultures in fact hold different moral views. That's a fact claim about what people believe. Anthropologists can check it, and lots of it is true.

    Normative relativism (often just called cultural relativism): what is actually right or wrong for people is fixed by their own culture's moral code. "Wrong" just means "wrong according to our code". There's no standard beyond the codes.

    The first is about what people believe. The second is about what's true. Going from one to the other is a jump, a cousin of the is/ought gap from Challenge 1: a fact about what people think doesn't, on its own, tell you what's so. The rival view, moral objectivism, says at least some moral claims are true or false no matter what any person or culture thinks.

    Why disagreement doesn't prove it

    Here's the argument people usually have in mind, laid out the Challenge 1 way:

    Exhibit C: the argument from disagreement 1. Cultures disagree about right and wrong. So, there's no objective truth about right and wrong.

    As it stands, it isn't valid: something is missing between the premise and the conclusion. You'll find the missing piece in Lead 2. Two more things to know first:

    • Some disagreements are about customs, not values. One culture greets with a handshake, another with a bow. Underneath, both are showing respect.
    • Some are about facts, not values. If two towns both want to keep children healthy, but one wrongly believes a medicine is poison, their "moral" disagreement is really a factual one.

    Disagreements left over after you take out customs and facts are called fundamental. They're real, and they're rarer than they first look.

    If relativism were true...

    On cultural relativism, every reformer in history was wrong right up until the moment they won.

    The argument from disagreement fails. But a bad argument can still have a true conclusion. So let's test the view another way: assume it's true, and follow where it leads.

    Exhibit D: what you'd have to accept
    1. Reformers can never be right when they speak up. A reformer says "my own society's code gets this wrong." But if "wrong" means "wrong by our code", that sentence contradicts itself. People who campaigned against slavery, or for women's right to vote, went against the accepted code of their own societies. On relativism, they were mistaken until enough people agreed with them.
    2. Moral progress becomes mere change. If a society's code changes, it doesn't get better; it just gets different.
    3. You can't criticize other cultures by any standard outside their own code, even for practices that seem to crush people, as long as that code approves.
    4. Want to know what's right? Take a poll. Moral questions turn into questions about what most of your society approves.
    5. Which culture? You belong to a family, a school, a country, maybe a religion. They have different codes. Relativism has to say which one counts.

    Quick tap: which of these five is hardest for you to accept?

    That's the one a relativist would have to persuade you about. Rocky will ask.

    A tough relativist can bite every bullet: "Yes, the reformers were wrong at first. Progress is just change." But most people find at least one of these impossible to believe. Turn that into an argument and it's valid:

    Exhibit E: the reformer argument 1. If cultural relativism is true, a reformer who opposes her society's code is wrong when she first speaks up. 2. Some reformers were right when they first spoke up. So, cultural relativism is false.

    The relativist has to reject premise 2. Lead 3 lets you watch exactly what that costs.

    The tolerance trap

    The most popular reason for being a relativist may be the one that sinks it.

    Lots of people like relativism because it seems humble and tolerant. Who are we to judge other cultures? Live and let live. Those are good instincts. The question is whether relativism can actually back them up.

    Try holding both of these at once:

    RrelativismWhat's right for a culture is whatever that culture's code approves.
    TtoleranceEvery culture ought to tolerate other cultures' ways.

    Notice that T is a moral claim meant for every culture, including cultures whose codes say nothing of the kind.

    Quick tap: can a relativist consistently say "everyone ought to be tolerant"?

    Hang on to your answer; Lead 4 tests it.

    Can you see a crash coming? Lead 4 hands you a third card and lets you find it. If there is a crash, the relativist has three ways out:

    • Make tolerance optional.
    • Make tolerance universal.
    • Call tolerance "just our value".

    The philosopher Bernard Williams called the mix of "right means right for a society" with "so everyone must tolerate everyone" vulgar relativism. In his 1972 book Morality he described it as "possibly the most absurd view to have been advanced even in moral philosophy."

    Me, not us: subjectivism

    If this theory is true, you and your worst enemy have never once disagreed about morality.

    Cultural relativism ties right and wrong to a group. Subjectivism shrinks it to a single person: "X is wrong" just means "I disapprove of X." Relativism says us; subjectivism says me.

    That sounds like the ultimate in open-mindedness. Now watch. Sam says "Lying to get out of chores is wrong." Jo says "No, it isn't."

    Quick tap: are Sam and Jo disagreeing?

    Many people say yes. Now watch what subjectivism has to say.

    On subjectivism, Sam means "I disapprove of it" and Jo means "I don't disapprove of it." Both statements are true! They're each reporting a different person's attitude, like "I'm cold" and "I'm not cold." So they aren't disagreeing at all. But it sure looks like they are. That's the disappearing-disagreement problem.

    Second problem: on subjectivism, you can never be wrong about morality, as long as you report your feelings honestly. Nobody has ever held a mistaken moral view. That's a strange thing to believe about humans.

    A. J. Ayer, a leading logical positivist, tried a cleverer version in Language, Truth and Logic (1936): "stealing is wrong" doesn't report a feeling; it expresses one, more like "Stealing, boo!" Critics nicknamed it the boo-hurrah theory. Ayer bit the bullet on the first problem: he said that when people really argue about morals, they're always arguing about facts. Later emotivists tried to rescue disagreement by saying a "boo" and a "hurrah" clash, as attitudes rather than beliefs. Either way, moral claims aren't true or false the way fact claims are, which is exactly the positivist pile Appiah was attacking.

    "It's just my opinion"

    Everyone says it, professors included on a bad day. Here's the problem: it doesn't do anything in an argument. Of course it's your opinion. Every sincere claim anyone makes is something they believe. Saying so doesn't add a reason for anyone else to agree, and it doesn't answer the reasons on the other side. It's a way of leaving the conversation while looking like you're still in it. Even people who think morality is ultimately subjective still argue about it with premises.

    Whether moral claims can be objectively true is a real question; it's the question this whole case file is about. You don't need to settle it to give reasons.

    What relativism gets right

    You can agree on what to do while disagreeing completely about why.

    Now the fair part: relativism starts from real insights.

    • Many differences really are just custom. Bowing or shaking hands, clearing your plate or leaving a bite: there's no "correct" one, and treating your own habits as morality is a classic mistake.
    • Humility is a virtue. Your culture has surely got some things wrong, and you probably can't see which.
    • Judging well takes understanding. A practice can look strange from outside until you learn what it means to the people who do it.

    And the relativist has comebacks. Tap each one open:

    Comeback 1: "Who decides what the objective truth is?"

    Objectivists owe an answer: if moral truths exist, how do we find them out? There's no microscope for wrongness.

    The other side says: nobody decides, just as nobody decides math. We test moral claims with reasons, consistency and cases. And not being sure of an answer isn't the same as there being no answer.

    Comeback 2: "'We know the truth' has been used to crush other cultures."

    History is full of conquerors who were certain they knew what was right for everyone else.

    The other side says: that's a reason for humility, and only an objectivist can say the crushing was really wrong.

    Neither reply ends the argument. If one of these comebacks still convinces you, take it to Rocky.

    Appiah's way forward is neither "anything goes" nor "my culture knows best". He calls it cosmopolitanism: caring about people everywhere while respecting that they will live differently. His key observation, in our words: people often agree about what to do in a particular case even when they disagree about the deep reasons, and thick words like cruel, kind and unfair give us a shared vocabulary for talking across cultures. Conversation, he suggests, can help us get used to one another even when it doesn't produce agreement.

    You now know three judges you could put on any moral case:

    The objectivistSome moral claims are true or false whatever anyone thinks. People and cultures can be mistaken.
    The cultural relativistRight and wrong for people are fixed by their own culture's code.
    The subjectivist"Wrong" means "I disapprove." Each person's attitude settles it for them.
