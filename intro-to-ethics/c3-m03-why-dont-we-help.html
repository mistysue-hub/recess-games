    Why Don't We Help?

    One child in a pond gets everyone's shoes wet. Millions of children far away barely get a glance. This file puts your own gut on trial: is it a moral compass, or a very old machine with some well-documented glitches?

    Gut call before anything else. When you feel a strong pull to help someone, and no pull at all for someone else, is that feeling a good guide to how much you ought to help? There's no right answer yet. You'll answer again at the end.

    Filed under Intro to Ethics · Challenge 3: Doing Right by OthersRead, then open each leadAbout 30 minutes

    Thirty-second recap

    Last file, Peter Singer's 1972 argument: if you could save a child drowning in a shallow pond at the cost of ruining your clothes, you obviously should, and distance makes no moral difference, so the same goes for people dying of preventable causes far away. His principle came in two strengths: if you can prevent something bad without giving up anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it; the milder version says the same about preventing something very bad without giving up anything morally significant.

    Most people find the pond case obvious and the far-away case... not. This file asks why. Psychologists have spent fifty years measuring exactly when our urge to help switches on and off, and the results are strange. Then we ask the philosopher's question: when our feelings switch off, are they telling us something true, or glitching? And we give Singer's critics their best shot.

    One promise: this file will not tell you what to do with your money. It's about how to think about the argument. What you believe at the end is yours.

    Your gut on the witness stand

    Your bathroom scale can be wrong in two very different ways, and only one of them is a bias.

    A moral intuition is a judgment that shows up before any argument: "you have to pull that kid out," "you don't have to answer every charity letter." Philosophers use intuitions as evidence all the time. Singer's whole case starts with one (the pond).

    But evidence can be faulty. A scale that's off by a different random amount each time is just noisy. A scale that always reads 2 pounds heavy is biased: it's wrong in the same direction, every time, for a reason built into the machine. A bias in thinking is the same: a systematic tilt that pushes our judgments the same way whenever a certain feature shows up.

    Exhibit A: the one-feature test

    Take two cases that are identical in everything that seems to matter morally: how bad the need is, how much your help would do, and what it would cost you. Change one other feature. If your reaction moves, ask: on reflection, should that feature matter? If the answer is no, you've probably caught a bias.

    Next lead runs a version of this test on you. No tricks: there's no hidden purpose, nothing is real money, and at the end the page shows you exactly how the cards pair up. Answer each card by gut, fast. Because you know a test is coming, you may beat it. That's fine; most people don't.

    One face beats a million

    People gave more to help a stranger they knew nothing about, as long as that stranger had already been chosen.

    In 1968 the economist Thomas Schelling (later a Nobel prize winner) pointed out a strange asymmetry. Let one particular person be in danger, and people will spend almost anything to save them. Let a policy save the same number of lives as anonymous statistics, and the money dries up. His distinction between identified lives and statistical lives launched decades of research. The pattern is now called the identifiable victim effect: we help more when there's a specific someone to help.

    Is it just that a face and a name are vivid? Partly, but not only. In a 2003 study, Deborah Small and George Loewenstein had people donate to help another participant identified only by a number, drawn from a bag. People gave more when the number had already been drawn than when it would be drawn afterwards. No face, no name, no story: just "a determined person" versus "someone, to be decided." That alone moved the money.

    Exhibit B: the Rokia studies

    In experiments published in 2007, Small, Loewenstein and Paul Slovic gave people $5 for taking part and a chance to give some of it to a children's charity. One letter described Rokia, a 7-year-old girl in Mali, with her photo. Another gave statistics about millions of people facing food shortages in Africa. Rokia raised roughly twice as much.

    Then the twist: adding the statistics to Rokia's story lowered giving compared with Rokia alone. And one more finding, about what happened when people were first taught about the identifiable victim effect...

    Your guess (not graded): after a short lesson about this bias, what happened to giving?

    ?Short on time? Skip this one: one face versus eight

    In 2005 Tehila Kogut and Ilana Ritov found that one identified sick child drew more support than a group of eight identified children needing the same total help. Eight faces didn't do eight times the work. They did less than one.

    The feeling system can't count

    In one series of studies, compassion began to fade as soon as there were two people to feel for.

    The psychologist Paul Slovic, borrowing a term from the psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton, calls this psychic numbing. Our feelings respond well to one person, then flatten out as numbers grow. Numbers deliver the information, but the feeling doesn't arrive with it. Large numbers can make us care less, not more.

    1 person: strong feeling → 2 people: already fading → 1,000,000 people: "a statistic"

    That last word comes from a line often pinned on Joseph Stalin: "one death is a tragedy, a million deaths is a statistic." Nobody has found good evidence he said it. Psychology agrees with the line anyway. Two findings, tap each to open:

    Compassion fade: when does feeling start to drop?

    In a 2014 set of studies, Daniel Västfjäll, Slovic and colleagues measured feelings (including body signals) and donations for one child versus more than one. Both began to drop as soon as a second child was added. Describing the children as one family or group helped undo the drop.

    Proportion dominance: which refugee camp would you fund?

    In a 1997 study from Slovic's lab, led by David Fetherstonhaugh, people were less willing to send aid that would save a fixed number of lives in a Rwandan refugee camp when the camp was described as much bigger. Same lives saved. Your camp cards in Lead 1 used this design: 4,500 lives in a camp of 11,000 versus the same 4,500 in a camp of 250,000. The bigger camp made it feel like "a drop in the bucket." We react to the fraction saved, not the number.

    Why might minds work like this? One common story: our feelings evolved for small groups, where helping meant the person in front of you. They weren't built for a world where you can read about millions. That's a hypothesis about origins, and we'll come back to what it would prove.

    The intercom experiment

    Psychologists staged an emergency that nobody could see. What people did depended on something that shouldn't matter.

    In 1968 the psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané ran a now-famous study in New York. Each student sat alone in a small room and joined a discussion over an intercom, taking turns to talk. Some believed the only other person on the line was one fellow student. Others believed there were two, or five, other people talking.

    Partway through, one voice (actually a tape recording) began to sound as if he was having a seizure and asked for help. Nobody could see anybody else. Each participant had to decide alone whether to leave the room and get help. The researchers timed them.

    Notice the design: the emergency was the same recording for everyone. The only thing that changed between conditions was how many other listeners each student believed were there. That's the one-feature test, run as an experiment.

    The study relied on deception, which is now tightly limited by research ethics rules. This file doesn't deceive you: the next lead asks for your predictions, then shows the real numbers and what the researchers made of them.

    The story everyone tells (and the real one)

    The most famous example of the bystander effect mostly didn't happen the way it's told.

    Textbooks for decades opened this topic with the 1964 killing of a young woman, Kitty Genovese, in Queens, New York (this file leaves out the details of the attack). Two weeks later a New York Times story said 38 neighbors had watched and none had called the police during the attack. It became a symbol of city coldness, and it inspired Darley and Latané's research.

    Your guess (not graded): of those 38, how many do you think really watched the whole attack?

    The 1964 story
    38 witnesses watched the whole attack and none called the police during it.
    What later checking found
    Nobody saw the whole thing. Far fewer people saw or heard part of it, many thought it was a quarrel, some people say they did call the police, and a neighbor, Sophia Farrar, went out and held her until help arrived.

    In 2007 three psychologists, Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins, called the 38-witness version a "parable" rather than a fact. In 2016 the Times itself said its original reporting had overstated the number of witnesses and what they saw. Lesson one: check the famous story.

    Lesson two is even better. In 2020 a team led by Richard Philpot studied CCTV footage of 219 real arguments and fights in public, in Amsterdam, Lancaster (UK) and Cape Town. In about 9 out of 10 cases, at least one bystander stepped in, and the more bystanders there were, the more likely the victim got help.

    How can both be true? Diffusion of responsibility is about each individual's chance of acting. With more people around, each one may be less likely to act, but there are more people who might, so the group may still come through. The danger is the case where everyone waits for someone else, and nobody comes. A related lab finding: when Latané and Darley filled a room with smoke, most people sitting alone reported it, but people sitting with two calm strangers (secretly part of the study) mostly didn't.

    Intuition or glitch? The debunking argument

    A philosopher noticed that most people's verdicts flip depending on whether the person in need is bleeding by your car or asking by letter.

    Peter Unger, in his 1996 book Living High and Letting Die, compared two thought experiments, retold here. In one, you drive past an injured stranger on a lonely road; getting him to the hospital will ruin your car's expensive leather seats. Almost everyone says you must help. In the other, a charity letter tells you a $100 check would save more than thirty children's lives; you throw it away. Most people say that's not great, but not monstrous either. Unger argued that what drives the difference is mostly how conspicuous the need is, and that conspicuousness isn't morally important.

    That's a debunking argument: showing that a belief is caused by something that has nothing to do with whether it's true.

    Quick one (not graded): you learn you believe it'll rain today only because you're wearing your lucky socks. Do you still trust that forecast?

    Exhibit C: the debunking argument 1. Our sense that we must help the person in front of us, but needn't help distant strangers, is largely caused by features like nearness, vividness, being identifiable, and being the only one who could help. 2. Those features don't change how bad the suffering is or how easily we could prevent it, so they aren't morally relevant. So that sense is not reliable evidence that distant strangers matter less.

    Singer's own scorecard, from his 1972 paper and later work:

    Not relevant: how far away they are; whether you know who they are; whether other people could help but aren't; whether you caused the problem (you must help even if you didn't).
    Relevant: how bad the outcome is; how much good your help would do (more lives beat fewer); what it would cost you.

    Three ways to push back. Tap each to open.

    It may prove too much

    Evolution also shaped our sense that pain is bad and that helping the drowning child is required. If origins in evolution debunk one intuition, why not those? Singer's answer is that some judgments survive careful reflection better than others.

    The other side says: whether that line can be drawn cleanly is exactly what's in dispute.

    Some features might really matter

    Maybe being the only person who can help really does make it more your job. Maybe relationships really do create extra duties. A debunker has to show the feature is irrelevant, not just that it moves us.

    The other side says: then name the reason it matters. "It just feels like it does" is the very thing under suspicion.

    Fixing a bias can backfire

    Remember Rokia: learning about the bias cut giving to the identified child without raising it for anyone else. Even if the feeling is a bad guide, the "fix" matters.

    The other side says: that's a point about how to correct a bias, not about whether it is one.

    Singer's critics take the stand

    The strongest objection to Singer isn't about the pond. It's that, if he's right, morality would take over your life.

    Five objections. Each card holds the objection, a reply from Singer's side, and the critics' comeback. Open all five; Lead 5 needs them.

    Objection 1: demandingness. It asks too much.

    Follow the strong principle all the way and you'd have to keep giving until giving more would cost you something as important as what it prevents. Hobbies, savings, nice things: all hard to justify. A morality that asks that much, critics say, can't be right. Susan Wolf's 1982 essay "Moral Saints" argued that a life devoted entirely to maximizing moral good wouldn't be an especially good or attractive ideal for a person to aim at.

    Reply: Singer also offered a milder version: prevent something very bad when you can do so without giving up anything morally significant. That still asks a lot, but far less. And "that's a lot to ask" doesn't by itself show a claim is false; our sense of "too much" might be shaped by self-interest, the very kind of bias this file is about.

    The other side says: if almost nobody could live by a rule, that may be a sign the rule is wrong, not that almost everybody is.

    Objection 2: special obligations. Family comes first.

    You have duties to your family and friends that you don't have to strangers. A parent who spends the family's food money on strangers isn't a saint; they're failing their kids.

    Reply: Grant it. Special obligations may limit how much you must do for strangers, but they don't show strangers don't count. Once your family's real needs are met, the argument still applies to what's left.

    The other side says: close relationships are part of a good life, not just a list of needs to meet before the "real" duties start.

    Objection 3: that's the government's job.

    Global poverty is a problem for institutions: governments, international bodies, fair trade rules. Asking individuals to fix it lets the real culprits off the hook.

    Reply: Maybe institutions should fix it. But at the pond, "the lifeguard should have done this" is no excuse when the lifeguard isn't there. While institutions fall short, the need is still there. (And individuals can act on institutions too, for example by voting and campaigning. Singer agrees with this part.)

    The other side says: the lifeguard reply assumes the pond and the world are alike, when real help passes through governments, organizations and long supply chains.

    Objection 4: I only owe my fair share.

    If everyone well off did a modest amount, the problem would shrink dramatically. So surely I only owe my share; I don't have to make up for everyone who doesn't. The philosopher Liam Murphy defended a view along these lines.

    Reply: Imagine two children drowning, and another adult on the bank who could help but won't. You save one: your "share." Do you walk away from the second? Most people say no, which suggests your duty doesn't stop at your share when others fail.

    The other side says: that shows something about an emergency right in front of you; ongoing, worldwide needs may work differently.

    Objection 5: it's a drop in the ocean.

    The problem is so huge that anything one person does makes no real difference.

    Reply: That's proportion dominance and psychic numbing talking. Helping one person isn't helping 0.0001% of a problem; it's helping a whole person. The 4,500 lives in the big camp are just as many lives as in the small one.

    The other side says: the real worry may be about facts, not feelings: whether a particular effort actually works is a fair question.

    Philosophers still argue about every one of these.
