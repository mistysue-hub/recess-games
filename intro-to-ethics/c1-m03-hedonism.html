    Thirty-second recap

    Case 1 split ethics into two questions: what makes a life go well for the person living it (well-being), and what we owe each other (morality). This file is about the first: not "is it wrong?" but "is it actually good for you?"

    Philosophers have three big families of answers. This file tests the oldest and simplest: it's all about how your life feels.

    Case 2's toolkit comes along: when you meet an argument you don't like, ask which premise you'd reject.

    The theory: feel good, live well

    The most famous ancient hedonist thought the best pleasure was... peace and quiet.

    Hedonism (from hēdonē, the ancient Greek word for pleasure) is the theory that the only thing good for you in itself is pleasure, and the only thing bad for you in itself is pain. How well your life goes is the balance between the two.

    well-being = all your pleasure − all your pain

    "Pleasure" here means any experience that feels good: laughing so hard you can't breathe, the calm of a quiet morning, finally landing a trick. "Pain" means any experience that feels bad, including boredom, dread and embarrassment, not just stubbed toes.

    Exhibit A: two ways to be good for you

    Good in itself (philosophers say intrinsically good): good even if it led to nothing else. Good for what it brings (instrumentally good): good because of what it leads to. Money is the classic instrumental good. Nobody dreams about a pile of paper.

    Here's the bold part. The hedonist says almost everything you care about is only good for what it brings. How far does "almost everything" go? Lead 1 will test you.

    And that "peace and quiet" thing? The Greek philosopher Epicurus (341 to 270 BCE) was a hedonist, but not a party animal. He thought the best life came from simple pleasures, friends, and above all freedom from pain in the body and worry in the mind.

    Bentham's pleasure math

    In the 1780s, an English reformer tried to turn happiness into arithmetic.

    Jeremy Bentham, an English philosopher and legal reformer, opened his 1789 book An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation with a famous line: "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure."

    For Bentham, the only thing that matters about a pleasure is how much of it there is. Not what kind. Push-pin was a simple children's game, and Bentham wrote: "Prejudice apart, the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry."

    So how do you measure "how much"? Bentham listed seven things to check. The method is now usually called the hedonic calculus (or felicific calculus):

    Intensity: how strong does it feel?
    Duration: how long does it last?
    Certainty: how sure are you that it'll actually happen?
    Nearness: how soon? (Bentham's word: propinquity.)
    Fecundity: will it lead to more pleasures afterward? (Fecund means fertile.)
    Purity: is it free of pain that tags along after?
    Extent: how many people does it reach?

    Extent counts other people, because Bentham's bigger project was which laws make a whole society happiest. (That's morality: a later case file's job.)

    Bentham never gave a units system. Nobody can say a hug is worth exactly 7 pleasure points. The calculus is a checklist for comparing, not a calculator. Lead 2 shows you why that matters.

    Mill: not all pleasures are equal

    John Stuart Mill started learning ancient Greek at age three.

    Mill grew up inside Bentham's circle: his father, James Mill, was one of Bentham's closest allies. In his twenties Mill went through a long low patch, and he later wrote that poetry, especially Wordsworth's, helped him out of it.

    In Utilitarianism (1863), Mill stayed a hedonist, with a twist. Pleasures differ in quality, not just quantity. Pleasures of the mind, like understanding, imagination and deep friendship, are higher pleasures. Pleasures we share with other animals, like eating and lounging, are lower pleasures.

    Exhibit B: the most quoted line in the debate

    "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." And if the fool disagrees, Mill adds, it's only because the fool knows just one side of the comparison.

    How do you tell which pleasure is higher? Mill's test, often called the competent judges test: ask people who have properly experienced both. If nearly all of them prefer one, even with more hassle attached, and wouldn't swap it for any amount of the other, it's higher in quality.

    Two worries critics have pressed ever since:

    • The circle. Who counts as a competent judge? Suppose someone has tried both poetry and video games and prefers the games. If the Millian replies "then they didn't really appreciate poetry," a competent judge just means "someone who agrees with Mill." (Fair to Mill: he thought people can lose the capacity for higher pleasures. Whether that escapes the circle is the fight.)
    • Is it still hedonism? If a higher pleasure can win while feeling less pleasant, something other than pleasantness is doing the work. Quality starts to look like a second ingredient.

      ?Pleasure, straight to the source?

      In 1954, James Olds and Peter Milner at McGill University in Montreal found that rats would press a lever again and again when it sent a small current to certain spots in the brain. In later experiments, some pressed thousands of times an hour. The phrase "pleasure centers" caught on; Olds himself used it.

      Your turn: a button that makes you feel good for a second, every time. Would you keep pressing it?

      Plot twist: the rats may not have been enjoying it. Later neuroscientists, notably Kent Berridge, argued that the brain's systems for wanting something are partly separate from its systems for liking it. The rats may have been driven to want the zap more than they enjoyed it.

      Researchers who study addiction have used the same split to explain how someone can keep wanting a drug long after it stops being enjoyable: lots of wanting, not much liking.

      Now the big question for hedonism, bonus file or not. Hedonism says what matters is how things feel. If science could hand you the feelings directly, with no need for the real things that usually cause them, would you take the deal?

      The Experience Machine

      The most famous thought experiment about happiness is about three pages long, inside a book about government.

      In 1974 the Harvard philosopher Robert Nozick published Anarchy, State, and Utopia, a book mostly about government. Tucked inside is the idea from the cover. Here's our simplified version of it:

      Exhibit C: the deal

      Imagine brain scientists who can wire you up so you experience anything you like: writing a hit song, making a best friend, scoring the winning goal. Your body rests safely in a lab the whole time. You pick the experiences in advance. Inside, it feels exactly like real life, and you don't know you're plugged in. Would you plug in for the rest of your life?

      (In Nozick's original you come out every couple of years to pick the next batch, then forget again. We've simplified.)

      Nozick's bet: most of us wouldn't. And if that's right, it's a problem for hedonism. Lay it out the Case 2 way:

      Exhibit D: the argument 1. If hedonism is true, a life in the machine with more pleasure would be better for you than your real life. 2. A life in the machine would not be better for you than your real life. So, hedonism is false.

      It's valid. So if you don't buy the conclusion, which premise would you reject? (Not graded.)

      Nozick's three reasons for refusing, in our words:

      • We want to do things, not just have the experience of doing them. Feeling like you wrote a hit song isn't writing one.
      • We want to be a certain kind of person. In the machine, there's no real answer to whether you're brave, kind or funny.
      • We want contact with reality. The machine limits you to a world someone programmed.

      Experience vs reality

      Here's the distinction everything hangs on. Your experience is how things seem from the inside: what you see, feel and believe. Reality is how things actually are, whether or not you ever find out.

      Twin A
      Twin B
      Has real friends, really wins the championship, really writes the song.
      Lies in a lab, wired up. The machine feeds her exactly the same experiences, second by second.
      From the inside: identical. Neither can tell which she is.
      Hedonism's verdict: equally well off. Pleasure is an experience, so if the experiences match, so does the well-being.

      A theory that says only your experiences can make your life go better or worse is called a mental-state theory. Hedonism is the classic one. Its rivals say a life can go badly in ways you never feel.

      Thomas Nagel's case (1970): a man is betrayed by his friends and ridiculed behind his back. He never finds out and never feels a thing. Is his life going worse?

      Pop culture ran this test too. In The Truman Show (1998), Truman's whole world is a TV set and everyone in it is acting. For most of his life he has no idea. Is his life going well?

      The hedonist strikes back

      Flip the question around, and lots of people's answers flip too.

      Back to Exhibit D: the hedonist rejects premise 2, so they owe us an explanation of why plugging in feels so wrong. Tap each reply open; Lead 5 opens once you've read all four.

      Reply 1: you'd refuse to unplug, too

      People tend to prefer whatever they already have. Researchers call this status quo bias. In 2010 the philosopher Felipe De Brigard turned the machine around: he asked students to imagine they had been in a machine all along and could now unplug into a real life they knew nothing about. Almost half chose to stay plugged in. If we refuse to plug in and refuse to unplug, maybe we're not valuing reality. We're valuing where we already are.

      The other side says: about half did unplug, into a life they knew nothing about. Bias may explain some refusals, not all.

      Reply 2: your imagination is cheating

      The story says the machine never breaks, but it's hard to truly believe that. Your "no" may sneak in worries the story already ruled out: glitches, power cuts, someone messing with you.

      The other side says: even people who picture a perfect, unbreakable machine often still say no.

      Reply 3: that's not a well-being reason

      "I can't abandon my family" is a great reason. But use your Case 1 tool: it's about what you owe others. It shows plugging in might be wrong, not that it would be worse for you.

      The other side says: imagine you're alone and owe nobody anything. Many people still wouldn't plug in.

      Reply 4: reality is usually the best route to pleasure

      In normal life, real friends and achievements are the most reliable sources of good feelings, so we learn to care about the real thing. That habit just misfires in a sci-fi case where reality and pleasure come apart.

      The other side says: explaining where a feeling comes from doesn't show the feeling is mistaken.

      The debate is very much alive.
