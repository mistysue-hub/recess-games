    What Makes a Life Meaningful?

    A philosopher's shopping list for a good life, a man who counts grass, a king pushing a boulder up a hill forever, and one question that splits the whole debate in two.

    Thirty-second recap

    We're still on the well-being question: what makes a life go well for the person living it. Two suspects so far:

    • Hedonism (Case 3): only pleasure is good for you in itself. Your life goes as well as it feels.
    • Desire-satisfaction theory (Case 4): your life goes well when the things you want actually happen, whether or not you find out.

    Both make what's good for you depend on something in your mind: your feelings, or your wants. Philosophers call theories like that subjective (from "subject", the person doing the feeling and wanting). Change the person's mind, and you change what's good for them. Today's suspect says both missed something.

    The theory: some things are just good for you

    The name of today's theory comes from a few pages at the back of a 1984 book.

    An objective list theory says there's a list of things that are good for you whether or not you want them or enjoy them. "Objective" is the opposite of subjective: the item's value for you doesn't depend on your feelings or wants about it.

    A typical list (theorists argue about the details)
    • real friendship and love
    • knowledge and understanding
    • achievement: doing something hard and worthwhile
    • developing your abilities
    • appreciating real beauty

    The name comes from the British philosopher Derek Parfit, in an appendix to Reasons and Persons (1984). He sorted theories of well-being into three families. His sample list included knowledge, using your mind well, developing your abilities and being aware of true beauty. He also listed things that are bad for you even if you never notice them, like being betrayed or deceived.

    HedonismHow does it feel?
    Desire theoryDid you get what you wanted?
    Objective listDoes your life have the good stuff?
    Exhibit A: the grass counter returns

    Case 4's strange desire comes from John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971): a clever person whose only pleasure is counting blades of grass in park squares and lawns. Rawls admitted that, on his own definition of a person's good, counting grass really is this man's good. An objective list theorist shakes their head: he's content, he gets what he wants, and his life is still missing goods it could have had.

    Your call: is the grass counter's life going well? (Not graded.)

    The best evidence for the list theory is cases where hedonism and desire theory seem to give the wrong answer. If your gut said, back in Case 3, that a fake best friend in the Experience Machine isn't as good for you as a real one, the list theorist can explain why: real friendship is on the list, and the machine can't give it to you.

    The Three Judges

    Here's the trick that makes this topic click. Picture the three theories as judges on a panel. Show them the same life, and each asks just one question:

    The hedonist"How does this life feel, moment by moment?" Nothing else goes on the scorecard.
    The desire theorist"Did the things this person wants actually happen?" Even if they never find out.
    The list theorist"Does this life have the items on the list?" Feelings and wants don't count unless they're on it.
    Exhibit B: the panel meets the Experience Machine

    Hedonist: if the machine life feels better, it's better. Desire theorist: depends what you want. If you want real friends and real wins, the machine leaves those wants unmet, so it's worse. List theorist: worse, no question: no real friendship, no real achievement, and a head full of false beliefs instead of knowledge.

    Run every new case past all three judges before deciding what you think. The interesting cases are the ones where the judges split.

    Who gets to write the list?

    The best-known answer to this question is about 2,400 years old.

    The first big objection hits the list theory where it lives: the list. Why these items? Why friendship and not stamp collecting? If a theorist just writes down things they happen to like, the "theory" is a shopping list. Critics call this the problem of an unprincipled list: a list with no reason behind it that explains why each item is on it.

    Quick: stamp collecting, on the list or off? (Not graded.)

    Reply 1: test every item

    Use the missing-item test. Imagine a life that has everything else on the list but is missing this one item. Does that life seem worse for the person, even if they never notice the gap? Then the item earns its spot. And check it's good in itself, not just useful: money fails, because it only matters for what it gets you.

    Reply 2: find the principle underneath

    Look for one idea that explains the whole list. The classic is perfectionism, which goes back to Aristotle: what's good for a human is developing and using well the abilities that make us human, especially our ability to reason. Knowledge exercises your mind; friendship, your capacity to care; achievement, your skills. One principle, whole list.

    Short on time? Skip this one; nothing later depends on it.

    ?Two real lists, and a worry about bossiness

    Lists made by testing items differ, but overlap a lot. The legal philosopher John Finnis listed basic human goods including knowledge, friendship and play (1980). The philosopher Martha Nussbaum's list of central human capabilities also includes play, alongside health, imagination, thought and close relationships.

    A second worry is paternalism: if philosophers know what's good for you better than you do, might someone use that to force "goods" on you? List theorists usually answer that knowing what's good for someone doesn't give anyone the right to impose it, and many put freedom to run your own life on the list itself.

      "Good for me? I couldn't care less."

      The strongest objection to the list theory fits in five words: "But I don't care about it."

      Picture Mira. Her parents made her take piano for ten years. She's now technically brilliant: real skill, real achievement. She never once enjoyed it, never wanted it, and hasn't touched a piano since. The list theorist has to say piano made Mira's life go better for her.

      Did piano make Mira's life go better for her? (Not graded.)

      This is the alienation worry. To be alienated from something is to feel cut off from it, as if it isn't really yours. The philosopher Peter Railton put it influentially in 1986: a person's good has to connect somehow with what that person cares about or would find appealing. A theory that says your good could leave you entirely cold has lost the "for you" in "good for you".

      Exhibit C: the list theorist's dilemma If the list ignores what you care about, it faces the alienation worry. If it lets your caring decide what counts, it slides back into a subjective theory. Is there a way between?

      The popular way out is a hybrid theory: something is good for you only when both halves are there. The objective half: it's genuinely good stuff (on the list). The subjective half: you care about it, want it or enjoy it. Parfit floated this himself in the same appendix and called it a composite view: what's good for someone is having things like knowledge and love while also wanting them; either half alone is worth little or nothing.

      On a hybrid view, Mira's piano does little for her: objective half there, subjective half missing. The grass counter is the mirror image.

      Susan Wolf: when caring meets worth

      One philosopher argues meaning is neither happiness nor morality, but a third thing a good life needs.

      First, two questions people mix up. The meaning of life asks whether the universe, or human existence as a whole, has a purpose. Meaning in a life asks what makes one person's life full and worth living. We're on the second.

      The American philosopher Susan Wolf (born 1952) argues in Meaning in Life and Why It Matters (2010, from lectures she gave in 2007) that meaning is its own kind of value: not boiled-down happiness, not boiled-down morality. A life can be cheerful and meaningless, or meaningful and hard. "Find your passion" gets half the truth, she says; "be part of something bigger than yourself" gets the other half. Her one-line answer: meaning arises when "subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness", and you actually do something with it.

      Exhibit D: Wolf's two-part test Subjective half: you care about it, get gripped by it and actively engage with it. Going through the motions doesn't count. Objective half: it's genuinely worth caring about. Your love fits its object. Both halves together: meaning. Either half alone: not meaning.

      That word fit is the heart of it. Being afraid of a charging bull fits the situation; being terrified of a butterfly doesn't. Wolf thinks love can fit or misfit in a similar way.

      A life whose whole world revolves around love for a pet goldfish: does that love fit? (Not graded.)

      Frustration doesn't cancel meaning, by the way. A scientist grinding through years of failed experiments toward a cure she cares about passes both halves, even on the bad days. Meaningful isn't the same as fun.

      Wolf is talking about meaning, not well-being as a whole. But her test has the same shape as a hybrid theory: a subjective half plus an objective half.

      Sisyphus and the boulder

      Philosophy's favorite picture of a pointless life is already in Homer.

      In Greek myth, Sisyphus was a king punished by the gods: in the underworld, he must roll a huge stone up a hill. Every time it nears the top, it rolls back down, and he starts again, forever. The scene already appears in Homer's Odyssey.

      The American philosopher Richard Taylor built an essay called "The Meaning of Life" (in his book Good and Evil) around it, and changed the myth in two ways:

      Exhibit E: Taylor's two variations (our summary)

      The injection. The gods, in a strange sort of mercy, put a substance in Sisyphus's veins that makes him want, more than anything, to roll stones. Nothing about the task changes, but now he's doing exactly what he wants. Taylor's verdict: his life now has meaning for him, though from outside nothing has changed. Meaning, Taylor concluded, comes "from within us", not from outside.

      The temple. Suppose the stones stayed put and built a beautiful, lasting temple. Then, Taylor said, the meaninglessness would disappear: the work would have a point.

      Your gut: is the injected Sisyphus's life meaningful? (Not graded. Lead 5 reuses this answer.)

      Wolf discusses the injected Sisyphus and disagrees. He's happier, sure. But the task is as pointless as ever, so on her view his life gains no meaning. Worse, she suggests, he must either be fooling himself about the task or have had his mind dulled to love it.

      The French writer Albert Camus, in The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), ended on the most famous line in the debate: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." His Sisyphus knows the task is pointless and throws himself into the struggle anyway.

      Exhibit F: "Who's to say what's worth loving?"

      The obvious comeback to Wolf. Her answer: "No one in particular." No person or group has special authority on it, not even philosophers. Anyone can reason about it, and our judgments can be wrong and can be revised. She isn't trying to rank people's lives on a scale, and plenty of different things pass: for example art, science, care for people, craft, sport and play.

      Short on time? Skip this one; nothing later depends on it.

      ?Glowing worms and an eternity of boredom

      Taylor compared Sisyphus to the glowworms in caves in New Zealand: they hatch, glow, catch insects, turn into short-lived adults with no mouths, lay eggs, get eaten, and the cycle repeats for millions of years, going nowhere.

      And he didn't stop at the temple. Once it's finished, what does Sisyphus do? Sit and look at it for eternity? Taylor called that a picture of infinite boredom. For him, the point was never the result. It was the doing, and the inner drive to keep doing.
