    Getting What You Want

    A championship you never hear about, a party for a win that never happened, a man who counts grass, and a future bishop who caught the cynics getting the order of events backwards.

    Your team finally wins the championship. But the day before the final, you left for six months at a research station with no signal. By the time you're home, everyone has moved on, nobody mentions it, and you never look it up. You never find out. Did your life go any better because they won?     Thirty-second recap

    We're still on the well-being question from Case 1: what makes a life go well for the person living it? (Not what we owe each other. That's morality, and it waits for Challenge 2.)

    Case 3 tested hedonism: only how your life feels counts. Because it looks only at what goes on inside your head, it's called a mental-state theory. Then the Experience Machine hit it hard. Lots of people refuse a lifetime of perfect fake experiences, because they want the real thing.

    So here's the second big family of theories, and it starts right there: what if what matters is getting what you actually want, in the real world?

    Case 2's toolkit still applies: when an argument's conclusion seems wrong, find the premise you'd reject.

    The theory: the world matches your wishes

    On this theory, your life can go better on a day when you feel nothing at all.

    Start with two words. A desire is any want, wish, hope, goal or preference: wanting something to be the case. ("I want my sister's surgery to go well." "I want to finish this game." "I want a quiet weekend.") A desire is satisfied when the thing you wanted actually happens.

    Careful: philosophers don't mean feeling satisfied. A desire is satisfied when the world matches it, whether you find out or not. So your desire can be satisfied while you feel nothing (the team wins while you're off the grid), and you can feel thrilled while your desire is not satisfied (someone tells you a win happened that didn't).

    Now the theory. The desire-satisfaction theory (or just desire theory) says your life goes well for you to the extent that your desires are satisfied, and badly to the extent they're frustrated. Stronger desires count for more.

    well-being = wants that come true − wants that don't(weighted by how strongly you want each one)

    Why people like it:

    • You're the authority. What's good for you depends on what you care about, not on a list written by a philosopher. That explains why a life that suits your cousin perfectly could be miserable for you.
    • It survives the Experience Machine. The machine gives you the feeling of your wishes coming true. If what you actually want is real friends and real wins, the machine frustrates your desires while making you feel great. Desire theory says: don't plug in.
    • Economists use a version of it. When an economist measures welfare by whether people get what they prefer, that's a cousin of this theory.
    Exhibit A: two scorekeepers

    Picture two scorekeepers following your life. The hedonist keeps a Feel-o-meter: it moves only when you feel something. The desire theorist keeps a World-ledger: it scores only whether the things you wanted really happened.

    Feel-o-meter (hedonist)
    World-ledger (desire theorist)
    The secret win: your team wins, you never find out.
    No change. You felt nothing.
    Better. What you wanted really happened.
    The false report: you're told your team won and party all night. The report was wrong; they lost.
    Better. Best night of the year.
    Worse. What you wanted didn't happen. You just don't know it.

    The two theories pull apart in both directions. That's what makes them testable against each other.

    Why do you want that? Chains go UP

    Ask "why?" enough times and every want runs out of reasons. That's not a bug. It's the whole point.

    Not all wants are the same kind. Some things you want only as a way to get something else. That's an instrumental desire. Other things you want for their own sake. That's a final desire (philosophers also say intrinsic desire). You can find out which is which by climbing a why-chain: keep asking "why do you want that?" until there's no further answer.

    Exhibit B: one why-chain, bottom to top
    1. I want a phone charger.
    2. So my phone works tonight.
    3. So I can see my cousin's text when her plane lands.
    4. So I can be at the airport gate to meet her.
    5. Because I love her and haven't seen her in a year. Why do I want that? ...I just do. The chain stops: final desire.

    Everything below the top is instrumental. If you found out the plane was canceled, you'd stop caring about the charger instantly. That's the test: an instrumental desire disappears when its purpose disappears.

    Going UP (a why-answer): what's it for? "I want the charger so my phone works."
    Going DOWN (a how-answer): how would you get it? "I'll borrow my friend's charger." True, but that's a plan, not a reason.

    Most people slip into how-answers at least once. Watch for it.

    Your turn: think of something you want this week and climb its chain in your head. Where does it stop? (Not graded.)

    ?Bonus: Aristotle got here first (short on time? skip this one)

    Over 2,300 years ago, Aristotle made a version of this point in his Nicomachean Ethics: if everything were wanted only for the sake of something else, the chain would go on forever and wanting would be empty. Something has to be wanted for itself.

    Why this matters for the theory: desire theorists usually count only final desires. If the charger and the reunion both scored points, you'd be counting the same thing twice. And if you get the charger but the reunion falls through, your life hasn't gone better at all.

    Read the reason, not the thing

    The same activity can be final for you and purely instrumental for your best friend.

    Here's a trap. It's tempting to think some things are just "naturally" final (fun stuff, love) and others "naturally" instrumental (money, chores). But whether a desire is final or instrumental depends on why that person wants it.

    • Ines runs every morning because she loves the feeling of running. For her, running is final.
    • Ben runs every morning only because his coach requires it for the team. For him, running is instrumental.
    • Mo runs because he loves it and because it keeps him fit for soccer. His desire is both: final and instrumental at once. That's common, and it's allowed. If soccer disappeared tomorrow, Mo would keep running, just with one reason fewer.
    Exhibit C: the quick test

    Imagine the purpose is removed. Would they still want it? No: instrumental. Yes, and they gave no other reason: final. Yes, but they also gave a further purpose: both.

    Wants aimed at other people follow the same rule. Wanting your friend to recover because you care about her is final. Wanting her to recover so she can drive you to the concert is instrumental. Hold on to that; a future bishop will need it later.

    Trouble 1: wrong wants, odd wants, shrunken wants

    A theory that says "getting what you want is good for you" has to deal with people who want the wrong things.

    Problem 1: ill-informed desires

    You're thirsty and want to drink whatever is in the juice bottle in the fridge (you think it's juice). You don't know your little cousin refilled it with salt water as a prank. You drink it. Your desire was satisfied: you drank what was in that bottle. Did your life just go better? Obviously not. The desire was based on a false belief.

    Problem 2: odd desires

    The philosopher John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), imagined a clever person whose one great aim in life is counting the blades of grass in parks and lawns. He isn't confused about anything; it's simply what he wants most. If he gets to count grass all day, is his life going well? Rawls accepted that his own account of a person's good had to say yes, though he said we'd naturally be surprised that such a person exists. Many readers think the case shows that what you want isn't the whole story.

    Quick vote: is the grass counter's life going well? (Not graded.)

    Problem 3: adaptive desires ("sour grapes")

    In Aesop's fable, a fox can't reach some grapes and decides they were probably sour anyway. People do this too. If you're told for years that someone like you will never get to go to college, you might stop wanting it, just to stop the ache. Philosophers call these adaptive preferences: wants that shrink to fit what seems possible. Satisfy the shrunken wants and the World-ledger looks great, even though something has clearly gone wrong.

    ?Bonus: who worried about sour grapes (short on time? skip this one)

    The philosopher Jon Elster wrote a whole book on this, Sour Grapes (1983). The economist-philosopher Amartya Sen and the philosopher Martha Nussbaum raised it as a problem for measuring how well people's lives go: if people learn not to want what they can't have, asking "are you getting what you want?" can hide real deprivation. (Some defenders of desire theories think the objection can be answered.)

    The fix: informed desires. Many desire theorists switch to counting what you would want if you knew all the relevant facts and were thinking clearly. The philosopher Richard Brandt developed a version of this idea in 1979. Fully informed, you wouldn't want to drink the salt water, so drinking it doesn't count. And a well-informed version of the student talked out of college might still want to go, so the frustrated desire counts after all.

    But the fix has a price.

    • Who is this informed you? A version of you who knew everything might be such a different person that their wants don't feel like yours at all. Why should their wishes run your life?
    • Does it drift? If we keep correcting desires until they point at things that are actually good for you, the desires seem to stop doing the work. The "good things" do it. (That's where Case 5 picks up.)
    • The grass counter survives. Inform him of every fact and he might still want to count grass.

    Trouble 2: wants that wander far from you

    If the simplest version of this theory is right, a stranger's recovery can make your life go better.

    The simplest version counts every desire you've ever had, about anything at all. Call it the unrestricted theory. It runs into four kinds of strange cases.

    Strange case 1: the stranger

    Derek Parfit, in Reasons and Persons (1984), described meeting a stranger who is believed to be seriously ill. You're moved, and you sincerely want him to recover. You never meet again. Later, without your knowing, he recovers. On the unrestricted theory, your life went better. Parfit himself found that implausible, and used the case against the unrestricted theory. Do you agree with him?

    Strange case 2: after you're gone

    You spend years building a community garden and want it to keep growing long after you die. Some time after your death, it's paved over for parking. You're not around to know. The unrestricted theory says your life went worse, after it was over. Some philosophers accept this; they think it helps explain why breaking a promise to someone who has died seems to wrong them. Others find it spooky.

    Strange case 3: about the past

    Your family says your great-great-grandmother invented the soup recipe you all love, and you really hope it's true. Suppose it isn't: she copied it from a newspaper in 1920. Your desire about the past is frustrated, so on the unrestricted theory your life is going worse, because of something that happened a century before you were born.

    Strange case 4: desires you've outgrown

    At seven you desperately wanted to be a train driver. At seventeen you couldn't care less. If you end up driving trains anyway, does the seven-year-old's wish make your life go better now?

    Three ways to patch it. Tap each one open; Lead 5 opens once you've read all three.

    Patch 1: keep it about your own life

    Count only desires about your own life. Parfit discussed a view along these lines. That rules out the stranger and, probably, the soup.

    The other side says: is a parent's desire for their child to be happy "about their own life"? It feels like it should count.

    Patch 2: only what you want now

    Count a desire only while you still have it. That handles the train driver, and the garden too, since nobody has desires after they die.

    The other side says: what about plans you'll care about later, or a life's work you want to last?

    Patch 3: bite the bullet

    Accept the strange verdicts. Maybe your life really is a bit better when the stranger recovers, and a bit worse when the garden goes.

    The other side says: then your well-being depends on events you'll never touch or know about, which is a strange kind of "good for you".

    Is everyone secretly selfish? Butler's timing trick

    In 1726, an English clergyman (later a bishop) noticed that the cynic's story gets the order of events backwards.

    You've probably heard this one: "Nobody ever really does anything for anyone else. You helped your friend move because it made you feel good."

    Convincing? (Not graded.)

    That claim has a name. Psychological egoism says every desire you have is ultimately a desire for your own benefit. One famous version, psychological hedonism, says everything you want, you really want for your own pleasure (or to avoid pain).

    Notice these are claims about motivation (why people act), not about well-being. But they matter here. If psychological hedonism were true, every final desire would be a desire for your own pleasure, and the desire theory would collapse into hedonism. So the desire theorist needs it to be false.

    Enter Joseph Butler (1692 to 1752), an English clergyman and philosopher who later became a bishop. In his Fifteen Sermons (1726), he made an argument that philosophers still use. In our words:

    Exhibit D: Butler's argument 1. Most of our desires are aimed at things outside ourselves: food, a friend's safety, the end of a good book, even revenge. 2. Pleasure is what we get when one of those desires is satisfied. 3. So the pleasure depends on the desire coming first. If you didn't want food at all, eating wouldn't delight you. So, those desires can't just be desires for pleasure. The pleasure presupposes them.
    1. WANTyour friend's move to go well
    2. GETit goes well
    3. GLOWyou feel great

    The cynic points at step 3 and says it explains step 1. Butler says step 3 only happens because of step 1. Your good feeling after helping isn't proof you were selfish all along. It's evidence that you really wanted your friend to be OK.

    Not everyone thinks Butler wins. An egoist can reply that we want the food, or the friend's success, only because we expect it to bring us pleasure. Rocky may push you on this.

    Two warnings. First, Butler doesn't prove people are nice. Spite and revenge are also aimed outside ourselves, and Butler pointed out that people sometimes wreck their own interests to get even. His point is that our desires reach out into the world, for better or worse. Second, he never denied that we sometimes act for our own pleasure. He denied that we always do.

    The payoff for this case: final desires can be aimed at other people and at the world. So the desire theory really is a different theory from hedonism, not hedonism in disguise.
