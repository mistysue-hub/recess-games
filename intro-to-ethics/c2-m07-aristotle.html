    Who Are You Becoming?

    A 2,300-year-old theory that says ethics isn't mostly about rules or results. It's about the person your small choices are quietly turning you into. Also: why Aristotle thought people your age shouldn't take this class.

    Two people find the same lost wallet with $200 inside, and both hand it in. Maya does it without a second thought; keeping it never even crossed her mind. Sam badly wants the money, argues with himself for an hour, and hands it in anyway. Who is the better person? Lock in your gut call. You'll make the call again at the end.

    A different question

    So far in this challenge, the big theories have judged actions. Bentham asked which act produces the most happiness overall (judge by consequences). Kant asked whether you act on a principle you could will as a law for everyone, or treat people merely as tools (judge by the principle behind the act). Both answer "What should I do?"

    Today's thinker is about 2,000 years older than both, and he starts somewhere else: "What kind of person should I be?" Get that right, he thinks, and good actions follow. That approach is called virtue ethics (virtue, from the Greek aretē, "excellence").

    Exhibit A: who was Aristotle?

    Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE) was born in Stagira, in northern Greece, studied at Plato's Academy in Athens for about twenty years, and later tutored the teenage prince who became Alexander the Great. His main ethics book, the Nicomachean Ethics, was probably built from his lecture notes and named after Nicomachus, his son (or his father; both had the name).

    Aristotle thought you were too young for this class.

    Really. He says a young person isn't a good student of ethics, having seen little of life and tending to follow their feelings. Then he softens it: the problem isn't your age, it's how you live. Consider that a challenge.

    The goal: eudaimonia

    Ask "why?" enough times and every answer ends at the same word.

    Everything we do aims at some good, but most goods are wanted for the sake of something else:

    Why study?→to pass the test→to get into a good program→to get a good job→to live well

    Ask "why live well?" and there's no further answer. The highest good is like that: wanted for its own sake and never for anything else, and self-sufficient, meaning that on its own it makes a life worth choosing. Everyone agrees on its name, eudaimonia (you-die-MOH-nee-ah), but not on what it is.

    Quick gut check (not graded): is feeling great all day the same as living well?

    Eu means well; a daimōn is a guardian spirit. "Happiness" is the usual translation, but flourishing is better: living well and doing well as a human being. Four ways it differs from a good mood:

    Exhibit B: flourishing is not a mood

    1. It's a whole life. In W. D. Ross's translation: "For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day."

    2. It's an activity. At the Olympics, he says, the crown goes to those who compete, not to the strongest people watching. A good character slept through a whole life doesn't count.

    3. It needs some luck. It's hard to do fine things with no friends, no money and no influence. A good person bears ordinary setbacks well; only disaster on the scale of Priam, the king of Troy who lost his sons, his city and his life in old age, spoils a life.

    4. You can be wrong about it. Aristotle names three popular lives: pleasure, honor (public life) and study. He dismisses pure pleasure as a life fit for grazing animals, and money as only a means.

    The function argument

    Aristotle's most famous argument starts with a flute player and a leatherworker.

    So what is flourishing? In Book I, chapter 7, Aristotle answers with an argument about function (Greek ergon: a thing's characteristic work). In our words, checked against the text:

    Exhibit C: the argument 1. For anything with a function, being good means doing that function well. A good flute player plays well; a good eye sees well. 2. Carpenters and leatherworkers have functions, and so do eyes, hands and feet. It would be strange if a human being had none. 3. A function must be special to the thing. Growing and feeding we share with plants; sensing, with horses, oxen and every animal. What's left is an active life of the part of us that has reason. 4. That part has two sides: one that thinks, and one that can listen to reason, the way you follow good advice. The second side is our feelings and desires. So the human good is the soul's activity in line with virtue (excellence), the best and most complete one if there are several, over a complete life.

    Fine print. Book I doesn't say "think all day": step 4 brings in trained feelings (though Book X praises a life of study as best, a tension scholars still argue about). No designer is named. And he calls it all a rough outline: ethics allows only as much precision as its subject does.

    Three objections. Tap each to see the other side.

    Special isn't the same as good.

    Humans may be the only animals that file taxes or write cruel jokes. Being unique to us doesn't make something our good.

    The other side says: Aristotle means doing the special thing well, in line with excellence, and cruelty isn't an excellence.

    Good at isn't good for.

    A good thief steals well. That doesn't make stealing well good for the thief. Aristotle needs "excellent human" and "flourishing human" to line up.

    The other side says: a thief's skill isn't a human excellence; it's cleverness aimed at a bad goal.

    Do humans even have a function?

    A knife was made for cutting. Who says a person is for anything?

    The other side says: think of a healthy oak tree. It thrives in its own way, and no designer is needed.

    Virtue is a trained habit

    Throw a stone upward 10,000 times. It will never learn to fly.

    Virtues of thought, like wisdom, grow mostly through teaching and need time and experience. Virtues of character, like courage, honesty and self-control, grow through habit. Greek ēthos, character (where "ethics" comes from), is close to ethos, habit.

    They aren't natural (no amount of throwing trains a stone to rise), but we're born able to take them on, and habit finishes the job.

    Exhibit D: learning by doing

    People become builders by building and musicians by playing. In the same way, we become just by doing just acts, self-controlled by doing self-controlled acts, and brave by doing brave acts. So the habits you form from childhood make, in Ross's words, "a very great difference, or rather all the difference."

    The result is a state of character (Greek hexis): a settled tendency to feel and act a certain way. Not a feeling, and not a raw capacity: it's what training does to your feelings.

    The pleasure test. Someone who skips a third slice of cake and is glad about it has self-control; someone who skips it miserably isn't there yet. A real virtue is chosen knowingly, for its own sake, from a firm character. Before that, you do acts that only look just, like a learner playing the right note with a teacher's help, until they become yours.

    Book VII ranks four kinds of people, which brings back the cover question:

    Bad characterWants the wrong thing, does it, and sees nothing wrong.
    Weak-willedKnows better, but gives in.
    Self-controlledWants the wrong thing, but wins the fight.
    VirtuousWants the right thing. No fight needed.

    The doctrine of the mean

    Sometimes the right amount of anger is a lot.

    With feelings and actions you can go wrong in two directions. Take facing danger. Too much fear (too little confidence) is cowardice; too little fear (too much confidence) is recklessness. Courage sits between them, at the mean. In the table and the games, read the columns as too little or too much of the virtue: cowardice is too little courage, even though it's too much fear.

    The mean isn't the mathematical middle. It's the mean relative to us. If 10 pounds of food is too much for an athlete and 2 too little, a trainer won't just order 6: too little for Milo, a champion wrestler, too much for a beginner. So the mean is feeling and acting at the right time, about the right things, toward the right people, for the right reason, in the right way: sometimes gentle, sometimes fierce.

    Practical wisdom: the skill that finds the mean

    Aristotle: a teenager can be a math genius, but not (yet) practically wise.

    Who decides where the mean is? Aristotle's definition of virtue ends: as the person of practical wisdom would set it. Practical wisdom (phronēsis, FRO-nee-sis) is the ability to think well about what's good for living well as a whole, not just in one area like fitness or money. His example: Pericles, the Athenian statesman.

    It deals with particulars: this friend, this argument, this Tuesday. So it needs experience. Young people get excellent at geometry and math, which run on general rules, but practical wisdom is built up from lots of real situations.

    Exhibit E: map vs local

    "Be honest" and "be kind" are a map. When they collide (your friend asks if you liked their rough song demo), the map can't say which way to turn. Practical wisdom is the local who knows these streets: it sees what this situation calls for, like noticing that a friend's "I'm fine" isn't fine.

    Not just cleverness. Cleverness is skill at reaching whatever goal you've set; a brilliant scammer is clever. Practical wisdom needs a good goal, and good goals come from good character. So the two need each other: virtue makes the goal right, practical wisdom the route.

    Three theories, one screen

    Virtue ethics came back in a big way after 1958.

    That year the British philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe published "Modern Moral Philosophy," arguing that talk of moral "oughts" and duties had lost its foundations, and that philosophers should look again at human psychology, virtue and flourishing. The revival of Aristotle's approach followed. Side by side:

    Bentham: consequences

    First question
    Which act produces the most happiness overall?
    Right act
    The best total outcome, with everyone counted equally.
    The wallet
    Same act, same result: equally right. (Bentham himself said motives still matter when we judge the person, because they predict what someone will do next.)

    Kant: principles

    First question
    Could my principle be a law for everyone? Am I treating people as ends?
    Right act
    Acting on a principle you could will for everyone, out of respect for duty.
    The wallet
    Kant praises a helper who acts from duty after losing all sympathy. So if Sam hands it in because it's right, his act clearly has moral worth. (Kantians argue about how Maya scores.)

    Aristotle: character

    First question
    What would a person of good character do here, and what will doing it make me?
    Right act
    As modern virtue ethicists put it: what a virtuous person, with practical wisdom, would do.
    The wallet
    Maya is virtuous. Sam is self-controlled: admirable, but not there yet.

    Your turn (not graded): which first question is closest to how you actually decide?

    Objections: can it tell you what to do?

    Objection 1: no action guidance

    "Do what a virtuous person would do." But if I'm not virtuous yet, how would I know what that is? Bentham hands you a sum and Kant hands you a test. Aristotle seems to hand you... a role model.

    Reply: the virtue words are full of rules.

    Rosalind Hursthouse (On Virtue Ethics, 1999): an act is right if it's what a virtuous person would characteristically do in that situation. And the virtue words carry rules of their own: do what's honest, don't do what's cruel (she calls them v-rules). The rivals need judgment too: whose happiness counts how much? Which principle describes my act?

    The other side says: in the hardest cases, where good people disagree, "do what the wise person would do" sounds a lot like "good luck."

    Objection 2: whose virtues?

    Aristotle's list includes virtues only the rich could have, like magnificence (big public spending), and his courage is mainly about battle. He calls "undue humility" a vice, while the Christian tradition later put humility near the top (Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue, 1981, traces such differences from Homer to Jane Austen and Benjamin Franklin). And he held that women's reasoning lacks authority and that "natural slaves" can't deliberate, so neither could fully share in the virtuous life, a view almost everyone now rejects. A virtue list can carry a society's prejudices.

    Reply: every culture faces the same spheres.

    Martha Nussbaum (1988): every human life meets the same spheres: facing danger and death, bodily appetites, handling resources, dealing with others' good and bad fortune. A virtue is whatever it takes to do well in that sphere. Cultures argue about details, but about the same thing, so the method can correct Aristotle's own list.

    The other side says: if the details vary that much, has virtue ethics really escaped relativism?
