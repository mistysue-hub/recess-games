    Can You Harm One to Help Five?

    A runaway trolley, a surgeon with a terrible idea, and a rule about side effects that's over 700 years old. The question for today: do people have rights that stop you doing the most good?

    Here's the whole case in its plainest form. Is it ever OK to harm one person who has done nothing wrong, if that's the only way to stop five other people being harmed just as badly? Lock in your gut call now. At the end of the file you'll make the call again, knowing what philosophers know.

    Callback: two giants and a surgeon

    Challenge 2 gave you two giants. Bentham (Case 4): the right act is whatever produces the best overall outcome, everyone counted equally. You met it there as act utilitarianism. Philosophers call the wider family, judging each act by its overall outcome, act consequentialism; utilitarianism is the version where "best" means "most happiness." Kant (Case 6): never treat a person merely as a means, and test it with consent: could they agree to how you're treating them, knowing everything?

    You also met the act consequentialist's scariest result: the surgeon (philosophers call the case Transplant), who could secretly sacrifice one healthy visitor to save five patients. And you met its comeback: secrets leak, trust collapses, so count all the consequences. The question this file presses is different. Suppose the secret really is safe. Is the theory right in principle?

    One new exhibit: the Scapegoat

    A mob will riot, and several people will die, unless someone is punished for a crime. The judge can't find the culprit. She could frame an innocent person and stop the riot. (Philippa Foot used a case like this in 1967. Remember her: she's back in two screens.)

    Many people feel the wrong done to the visitor or the framed person has nothing to do with leaks. Each has a right not to be used like that.

    House rule for this file: when a case compares "one" with "five," each person faces the same kind of harm, unless the case says otherwise.

    Rights: the walls of the maze

    A right is the one thing in ethics that can say "no" to a bigger number.

    A moral right is a claim a person has against everyone else. It holds whether or not respecting it brings the best results. Think: your right not to be killed, attacked, robbed or lied to. Negative rights are rights not to be interfered with. Positive rights are rights to be given help. This file is mostly about negative rights.

    A goal
    A side-constraint
    Something to promote. More of it is better, and you should do whatever brings the most.
    A limit on what you may do while chasing any goal. It isn't something to maximize.
    "Make the world as happy as possible."
    "Whatever you're chasing, don't kill an innocent person to get it."

    The philosopher Robert Nozick drew this line in Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974). Think of a maze: you can chase your goal anywhere, just not through the walls. His reason for the walls is that each of us is a separate person: "There are only individual people, different individual people, with their own individual lives."

    Quick tap (not graded): a mob will frame five innocent people unless you frame one innocent person yourself. Is framing the one OK?

    Exhibit C: the twist that matters most in this file

    Imagine a consequentialist who loves rights and says, "Let's make the goal as few rights violations as possible." Sounds like respecting rights. It isn't. In the mob case, that goal says: frame the one, since one violation beats five. A side-constraint says: you may not frame anyone, not even to prevent more framings. Nozick's point: putting rights in the goal still lets you sacrifice people. Only a constraint protects each person.

    Ronald Dworkin had a similar image: rights are trumps that individuals hold against appeals to the general good (Taking Rights Seriously, 1977). How strong are the walls? Some say absolute: never cross them. Others say they have a threshold: they hold against "five instead of one" but give way when the stakes are huge. Critics ask where that line is.

    The deep puzzle: why should it matter more that I don't violate one person's rights than that five people's rights get violated by someone else? Defenders say a constraint is about what you do, not how many violations the world contains. Reasons like this are called agent-relative: reasons for you in particular.

    Meet the trolley family

    You met Switch and Footbridge in Challenge 2, Case 3. Here's the whole family, and this time the question is what makes the difference.

    Quick reminder: Philippa Foot (1920 to 2010) started it all in 1967 with a runaway trolley's driver, and the MIT philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson (1929 to 2020) named it the trolley problem in 1976 and added new versions in 1985 (in the Yale Law Journal). What's new here: Foot's driver came up as a contrast with the Scapegoat judge. Why may the driver steer, she asked, when the judge may not frame? Each version below changes one thing, like a scientist changing one variable. Nobody thinks they're about trains.

    Driver Foot, 1967

    The brakes fail. You're driving. Stay on course toward five, or steer onto the track with one?

    Switch Thomson

    Now you're a bystander next to a lever. Pull it and the trolley goes onto the side track, where one person is standing.

    Footbridge Thomson

    No side track. You're on a bridge next to a stranger wearing a huge, heavy backpack, strapped on tight, with no time to get it off. Push him off the bridge and he'll stop the trolley before the five. Jumping yourself wouldn't stop it.

    Loop Thomson, 1985

    Like Switch, but the side track loops back and rejoins the main track before the five. The one person on the loop is carrying a heavy pack that would stop the trolley.

    Quick tap (not graded): which case feels most different from the other three?

    Three ideas make sense of the family:

    Permissible: morally allowed. Not required, just "not wrong."
    Doing vs allowing harm: you do harm when your action causes it. You allow harm when you fail to prevent it.
    Using someone merely as a means: Case 6's test was consent: could they agree, knowing everything? Trolley philosophers add a sharper test for one kind of using: does your plan need this person to be harmed? Ask: if they weren't there, would your plan still work? Worked example, Switch: if nobody stood on the side track, the five would be saved just the same. So the one person isn't being used; his harm is a side effect. (The other cases are yours, in Lead 2.)

    The trolley problem is the puzzle of explaining why most people think pulling in Switch is fine but pushing in Footbridge is wrong, when both trade one life for five.

    Killing vs letting die

    Two people want the same result, and they get it. One does something; the other just doesn't. Is one of them worse?

    The life-and-death version of doing vs allowing is killing vs letting die. Foot's 1967 answer to her own puzzle leaned on it: negative duties (duties not to harm) are stricter than positive duties (duties to help). That explains two cases at once:

    • Transplant: killing one vs letting five die. The strict duty not to kill wins, so don't operate.
    • Driver: killing one vs killing five. Both options are doings, so harm fewer people. Steer.

    In 1975, James Rachels argued the opposite: the bare difference between killing and letting die makes no moral difference. His method was to build twin cases that are identical except for that one difference. His original example was grim. Here's our cartoon version:

    Exhibit D: the Bake-Off twins

    Smith wants to win the bake-off, so he knocks his rival's prize cake off the table. Jones wants to win the same way. He sees his rival's cake sliding off the table on its own, could catch it with one hand, and lets it fall. Same motive, same result. One did; one allowed.

    If Smith and Jones seem equally bad, Rachels says, doing vs allowing isn't doing the moral work. Defenders reply that both twins are awful because of their motive, which hides the difference. Try everyday life instead: you may not push someone over to save yourself $50, but you are allowed to keep $50 instead of giving it away. (Is that really OK? That's the next case file.)

    Exhibit E: Thomson's warning

    Suppose Foot is right that the duty not to kill is always stricter than the duty to save, so you may never kill one person to save others whom you would otherwise only let die. That handles Driver and Transplant neatly. Thomson noticed it runs into trouble with Switch, the case most people find easiest. Lead 3 asks you to work out why.

    Side effect or secret plan? The doctrine of double effect

    A rule written down in the 1200s still turns up in modern arguments about medicine and war.

    The medieval philosopher Thomas Aquinas, discussing self-defense in his Summa Theologiae, noticed that one act can have two effects: one you intend and one you don't. Later thinkers turned this into the doctrine of double effect (DDE). In our words, an act with a good effect and a bad effect can be permissible if:

    1. the act itself isn't wrong anyway;
    2. you don't intend the bad effect, either as your goal or as your means to the good effect;
    3. the good effect is serious enough to outweigh the bad one. Philosophers call this proportionality.

    The key distinction is intending vs merely foreseeing. A foreseen side effect is something you know will happen but that isn't part of your plan. If by some miracle it didn't happen, your plan would work just as well, and you'd be glad.

    Worked example: Switch

    Goal? Saving the five, not harming the one. Means? No: if he escaped, the five would still be saved, so his harm is foreseen, not intended. Worth it? Five saved against one lost. So DDE says pulling can be permissible. The other trolley cases are waiting for you in Lead 4.

    ?The textbook example from war ethics (short on time? skip this one)

    One pilot bombs a factory, foreseeing that people nearby will die. Another bombs people nearby on purpose, to frighten the enemy into giving up. The toll might be the same, but DDE says only the second one intends the deaths, as a means.

    Two big objections:

    • The closeness problem. Someone who uses a person as a blocker could say, "I only intend that he's in the way. I don't intend him to be hurt; that's a side effect." If that excuse works, DDE allows almost anything. If it doesn't, where exactly is the line?
    • Intentions judge the person, not the act. Two bakers run the same loud mixer at 7 a.m. to finish a wedding cake. One is secretly glad it wakes her annoying neighbor. Critics such as Thomson and T. M. Scanlon say intentions like hers show what kind of person she is, but don't change whether the act is permitted.

    Quick tap (not graded): does "I only intended him to be in the way, not to be hurt" work as an excuse?

    Run six cases through three gates, the way a DDE defender would. Gate 1: is the harm aimed at as a goal? Gate 2: is the harm the means, so the plan needs it? Gate 3: is the good serious enough to be worth the harm? The act passes only with no, no, yes. As soon as a case fails a gate, the decoder stamps it. This is about what DDE says, not what you think.

    Field notes: what scientists found

    Most people make the same trolley calls, and most of them can't say why.

    Quick tap (not graded): could you explain, in one sentence that actually works, why pulling in Switch and pushing in Footbridge are different?

    In a 2007 online study of thousands of people, Marc Hauser and colleagues found that about 9 in 10 said pulling in a Switch-style case was permissible, but only about 1 in 10 said so for a Footbridge-style case. Asked to explain, most who judged the two differently couldn't give a reason that worked. Intuition, the researchers concluded, drives these judgments, and the principles behind them stay hidden even from the people using them.

    ?Brain scans and self-driving cars (short on time? skip this one)

    In 2001, Joshua Greene and colleagues scanned people's brains while they judged cases like these. Up-close, hands-on harm seemed to trigger stronger emotional responses than impersonal harm like pulling a lever. Greene's view: our "don't push" alarm may be a gut reaction to personal force, not a deep principle. Others reply that the gut reaction may be tracking something real, like the difference between using someone and not.

    Trolley problems have also left the classroom. Self-driving cars must be programmed for rare crash choices, and an online project called the Moral Machine (published in Nature in 2018) collected millions of people's choices about them.

    The scoreboard so far

    Act consequentialism: pull, push, pull. Simple, but it also says operate in Transplant.
    Rights as side-constraints: no pushing, no operating. But then why is pulling OK? It also harms someone with a right not to be harmed.
    Doing vs allowing: explains Transplant and Driver, but struggles with Switch.
    Double effect: splits Switch from Footbridge, but struggles with Loop and the closeness problem.

    No theory gets every case "right." That's normal: either we find a better principle, or we decide some gut reactions are mistaken.
