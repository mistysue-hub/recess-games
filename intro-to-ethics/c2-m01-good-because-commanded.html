    Good Because Commanded?

    A referee, a king with a quill, and a 2,400-year-old question from a conversation outside an Athenian courthouse. Underneath it all: does a rule make something right, or does it just tell you what was already right?

    Your school bans running in the hallways. Is running in the halls wrong because of the rule? Or is there a rule because running in the halls is dangerous? Lock in your gut call. There's no right answer; a few screens in, you'll see what you were really choosing between.

    A new, deeper question

    Everyone argues about what is right. This challenge asks what "right" even is.

    The Good Life asked what makes a life go well for the person living it. Now we move to morality: what we owe each other. Underneath every moral argument sits one question. When we say "breaking a promise is wrong," what makes that true, if anything does?

    One word will keep coming up. A claim is objective if it's true or false no matter what anyone thinks, feels or wants. "The Earth goes around the Sun" is objective: it was true before anyone believed it. "Pineapple on pizza is delicious" isn't; it depends on whose tongue you ask. The big question of this challenge: are moral claims more like the first kind or the second?

    Today's case tests the oldest answer: right and wrong come from someone with authority saying so: a parent, a ruler, a law, or something higher. A 2,400-year-old argument says it's not that simple.

    Start on the field: two kinds of "because"

    In chess, the rule really does make it right. For lying, that sounds very strange. Why?

    A soccer referee blows the whistle and disallows a goal. Two different sentences could explain that, and they point in opposite directions:

    Why was the goal disallowed?
    "Because the ref said so." The official decision is what makes it count or not. Before the whistle, nothing was settled.
    "Because the striker was offside." The call reports a fact about where the players were. A good ref gets it right; a bad ref gets it wrong.

    Your instinct: can a referee's call be wrong? (Not graded.)

    Some rules really do make things right. In chess, the bishop moves diagonally because the rules say so; there's nothing deeper. Driving on the right in the US and on the left in the UK is the same: before the law, either side was fine, and the law picked one. Rules like these, which settle a choice where any option would have done, are called conventions. Other rules seem to report something that was right or wrong already. A ban on poisoning the town's water didn't make poisoning harmful. The harm came first.

    Exhibit A: the dilemma, any authority

    A dilemma is a choice between two options where both have a cost. Each option is called a horn (picture a bull: whichever horn you grab, you get poked). For any authority and any rule, ask:

    Horn 1 Is it right because it's commanded? (The command makes it right.)
    Horn 2 Or is it commanded because it's right? (The command reports it.)

    Back to your gut call. "Wrong because of the rule" was Horn 1. "A rule because it's dangerous" was Horn 2. Many people pick Horn 2 for the hallway, and Horn 1 for chess. Hold on to that.

    The king's quill: what each horn costs

    If a king's word makes things right, then praising the king's laws becomes a sentence that says nothing at all.

    Meet King Ozric, an imaginary ruler with a quill and a lot of confidence. Take a strict Horn 1 theory of him: "right" just means "commanded by Ozric." Two problems show up fast.

    Problem 1 for Horn 1: arbitrariness

    Something is arbitrary when it's chosen without a reason, so it could just as easily have been otherwise. If Ozric's say-so is all that makes things right, then if he decreed that mocking newcomers for fun is required, it would become right. He couldn't be mistaken. And if he had good reasons for his decrees, those reasons would be doing the real work, and we'd be on Horn 2. So on a strict Horn 1, the decrees rest on nothing at all.

    Problem 2 for Horn 1: emptiness

    Subjects love to say "Ozric's laws are good!" On Horn 1, "good" means "commanded by Ozric."

    Try it: swap "good" for "commanded by Ozric" in "Ozric's laws are good." What's left? (Not graded.)

    The cost of Horn 2: independence

    So try Horn 2: Ozric commands things because they're right. Now there's a standard of right and wrong that is independent of Ozric: it would hold even if he said the opposite. Ozric becomes like a good teacher pointing to true answers, not the person who makes them true. His decrees can be wise or foolish. But on Horn 2, the rule-maker is not the source of right and wrong.

    One wrinkle. For conventions, Horn 1 is fine: if Ozric decrees "drive on the left," driving on the left really does become the right thing to do. Even then, the reason to obey (nobody wants head-on crashes) was there before he picked up the quill.

    Athens, about 399 BCE: the original

    Heads up: two routes from here

    The original version of this puzzle is about gods, and for centuries people have asked it about God.     Route: original version.

    The whole argument started with a man at the courthouse to accuse his own father.

    Plato's dialogue Euthyphro is set outside a courthouse in Athens, around 399 BCE. Socrates is there because he's been charged with impiety (disrespecting the city's gods); at his trial he'll be sentenced to death. He meets Euthyphro, a religious expert who has come to bring a serious charge against his own father, and who is completely sure that doing so is pious: holy, approved of by the gods.

    Socrates asks: so what is piety? With Socrates' help, Euthyphro settles on: the pious is what all the gods love. (Socrates suggests adding "all", because in Greek myths the gods quarrel, so what one loves another might hate.) Then comes the famous question. In our words:

    Exhibit B: Socrates' question

    Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious? Horn 2
    Or is it pious because it is loved by the gods? Horn 1

    The whole argument started with a conversation outside a courthouse in ancient Athens, around 399 BCE.

    The puzzle comes from Plato's dialogue Euthyphro. The philosopher Socrates asks a confident man named Euthyphro what piety is. (Piety means holiness: doing right by a higher authority.) Euthyphro's answer: the pious is whatever that authority approves of. Socrates asks the question you already know from Ozric. On your route, the authority is the Lawgiver: an imagined ruler who is perfectly wise, perfectly informed and never changes their mind.

    Exhibit B: Socrates' question, Lawgiver edition

    Does the Lawgiver approve of kindness because it is right? Horn 2
    Or is kindness right because the Lawgiver approves of it? Horn 1

    Predict: which horn does Euthyphro grab? (Not graded.)

    He picks Horn 2: the gods love pious things because they're pious. Socrates pounces. Then "loved by the gods" can't be what piety is. It's just something that happens to pious things, the way being admired happens to a great painting without being what makes it great.

    In Plato's version, Euthyphro picks Horn 2: the authority approves of pious things because they're pious. Socrates pounces. Then "approved by the authority" can't be what piety is. It's just something that happens to pious things, the way being admired happens to a great painting without being what makes it great.

    The dialogue ends with no answer. Euthyphro suddenly remembers he's in a hurry and leaves. Plato leaves the puzzle with us.

    Divine command theoryLawgiver command theory

    Jewish, Christian and Muslim thinkers have all taken up this question, and religious philosophers have defended both horns.

    Divine command theory is the view that what makes an action morally right or wrong is God's commanding or forbidding it. It's a theory about the logic of definitions: what the word "right" refers to, and what explains what. You can't tell from someone's faith which horn they pick.

    Run Ozric's two problems on the strictest version:

    • Arbitrariness. If God's command alone makes things right, then if God had commanded cruelty for its own sake, cruelty would have been right. And on the strictest version, God could have no moral reasons for the commands, since moral reasons would be a standard deeper than the command (Horn 2). Most modern defenders avoid this by saying the commands come from God's good nature; that's the next screen.
    • Emptiness. "God is good" is a central claim in many faiths. On the strictest version it shrinks to "God commands what God commands."

    And Horn 2 has its own cost: if goodness is a standard independent of God, it looks like something God didn't create and can't change. For thinkers who hold that everything depends on God, that's a real worry.

    +Bonus history. Short on time? Skip this one.

    Some medieval thinkers, often linked with William of Ockham in the 1300s, held strong versions: God's will alone settles what is right. (Historians still argue about how far Ockham went.) In Islamic theology, a famous classical debate set the Ash'arites, who held that good and bad are fixed by God's command, against the Mu'tazilites, who held that reason can see good and bad for itself.

    The philosopher Gottfried Leibniz pressed the emptiness problem in a work he wrote in 1686: if things are good just because God wills them, why praise God for willing them?

    Swap King Ozric for a perfect ruler, and the puzzle gets harder, not easier.

    Lawgiver command theory (our name for your route) is the view that what makes an action morally right or wrong is the Lawgiver's commanding or forbidding it. Philosophers call views like this command theories of morality. It's a theory about the logic of definitions: what the word "right" refers to, and what explains what.

    Run Ozric's two problems on the strictest version:

    • Arbitrariness. If the Lawgiver's command alone makes things right, then if the Lawgiver had commanded cruelty for its own sake, cruelty would have been right. And on the strictest version, the Lawgiver could have no moral reasons for the commands, since moral reasons would be a standard deeper than the command (Horn 2). Defenders avoid this by saying the commands come from the Lawgiver's good nature; that's the next screen.
    • Emptiness. "The Lawgiver is good" shrinks to "the Lawgiver commands what the Lawgiver commands." Like praising a referee for agreeing with himself.

    And Horn 2 has its own cost: if goodness is a standard independent of the Lawgiver, the Lawgiver didn't create it and can't change it. A perfect guide, but not the source.

    Which problem looks more serious to you? (Not graded. It's about the logic, not about anyone's faith.)

    The replies: escaping the horns

    The cleverest reply says the dilemma offers a false choice.

    People have been answering this dilemma for 2,400 years. Tap each card to open the main moves and the other side's comeback. Open all four to unlock Lead 3.

    Reply 1: bite the bullet. What if Horn 1's cost is no cost at all?

    Accept Horn 1. "Right" simply means "commanded by Godthe Lawgiver," so "if cruelty were commanded, it would be right" is true, but no more alarming than "if the chess rules said bishops move sideways, they would."

    The other side says: almost everyone, including most divine command theorists, finds it impossible to believe cruelty for its own sake could ever be right, and the emptiness problem stays.

    Reply 2: the nature reply. What if goodness is who the commander is?

    Goodness isn't a rulebook outside Godthe Lawgiver, and it isn't a whim either. It's grounded in God'sthe Lawgiver's own nature, which is perfectly loving and just and can't change. Commands flow from that nature, so they could never be cruel: not arbitrary (Horn 1's problem), and not answering to an outside standard (Horn 2's). The modern philosopher Robert Adams argued that wrongness is being contrary to the commands of a loving God.

    An analogy used by the philosopher William Alston: from 1889 to 1960, the meter was defined by one special metal bar kept at Sèvres, just outside Paris. The bar didn't "meet" the standard; it was the standard. Likewise, says this reply, Godthe Lawgiver is the standard of goodness.

    The other side says: ask Socrates' question one level up. Is that nature good because it's God'sthe Lawgiver's? Then "good" means "like Godthe Lawgiver," and the praise looks empty again. Or is it good because it's loving and just? Then love and justice look like the real standard: Horn 2. (Defenders answer that the meter bar shows a standard can be one particular thing without being empty. The argument continues.)

    Reply 3: take Horn 2, happily. What if the commander is a perfect guide, not an inventor?

    Many religious philosophersthinkers simply accept Horn 2: Godthe Lawgiver commands kindness because it's good, the way a perfect mathematician states true theorems without making them true. Thomas Aquinas in the 1200s, who did not hold a divine command theory, grounded morality in God's reason (the "eternal law"); some later thinkers built a nature reply from his idea that God is goodness itself.

    The other side says: for those who think everything depends on Godthe Lawgiver, a moral law Godthe Lawgiver didn't make is a lot to give up.

    The scoreboard. Where does that leave the dilemma?

    Reply 1 keeps Horn 1 and pays its price. Reply 3 takes Horn 2 and pays its price. Reply 2 says the choice is false; its critics say the dilemma returns one level up. Serious philosophers, religious and not, defend each move today.

    The other side says: whichever way you go, you now know exactly what you're committing to. That's the real lesson of the Euthyphro.

    So what does "objective" mean?

    The two horns give two different answers to whether morality is objective, and both can say yes.

    Objective means true no matter what anyone thinks, feels or wants. The Euthyphro shows "anyone" can be read two ways.

    Objective, sense 1: independent of human opinion. No person, crowd or culture can vote cruelty into being right. A command theory can be objective in this sense: God'sthe Lawgiver's commands hold whatever humans think of them.
    Objective, sense 2: independent of every will at all. True no matter what anyone, even the highest authority, commands or wants. Only Horn 2 gives this. Moral truths become more like truths of math: 2 + 2 = 4 didn't need anyone's permission.

    The strict Horn 1 makes morality depend on a will, a mind's choices. Take the commander away, and on the strict Horn 1 nothing is commanded, so nothing is right or wrong. That's why critics call it a cousin of subjectivism (the view that moral claims just report someone's attitudes), and why defenders insist the commander's will is perfect and unchanging, so it isn't like anyone's whim. The nature reply tries to have sense 1 and most of sense 2 at once.

    The Euthyphro test, pocket edition

    You can run Socrates' question on almost any "it's right because..." you'll ever hear.

    A culture: is it right because my culture approves of it, or does my culture approve because it's right? (That's the next case file: relativism.)
    A majority: is it right because most people voted for it, or did they vote for it because it's right?
    Your feelings: is it wrong because it disgusts me, or does it disgust me because it's wrong?
    The law: is it right because it's legal, or did it become legal because it's right? (History has laws that were changed because people came to see them as wrong.)

    Horn 1 risks arbitrariness; Horn 2 says there's a standard the authority answers to. The test doesn't give you the answer. It shows what you're committing to when you give one.
