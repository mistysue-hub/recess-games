    People Who Don't Exist Yet

    The people you'll affect most may not be born for centuries. They can't vote, complain or thank you. Some of them will only exist because of what you choose. What could you possibly owe them?

    The 300-Year Button. Press it, and every person alive today gets a noticeably better life: more comfort, less stress, a few extra good years. The catch: people living 300 years from now get noticeably harder lives (still worth living, just harder). Nobody alive now will ever meet them.     From far away to far ahead

    So far this challenge has asked what we owe strangers who are far away in space. Remember the drowning child in the pond: most people decide that a child being 5,000 miles away instead of 5 meters away doesn't make the child matter less.

    This file runs the same test through time. If miles don't shrink what you owe, do years?

    Three words you'll need

    Generation: everyone born around the same time. Here we'll count one generation as about 25 years (real gaps between parents and children are often closer to 25 to 30, but 25 keeps the math easy).

    Future people: people who will exist but don't yet. Not babies alive now (they already exist), but the ones who haven't been conceived.

    Posterity: an old word for all the future generations together.

    Today's question is about morality (what we owe each other), not well-being. You'll still use Challenge 1, Case 1's tool: keep "good for someone" and "the right thing to do" apart. It turns out to matter enormously here.

    Do we owe them anything?

    The builders who laid the first stones of Cologne Cathedral in 1248 never saw it finished. It was completed in 1880.

    Our choices reach a long way forward. Some do it on purpose, like a cathedral, a tree you plant or a law. Some do it by accident, like waste that stays dangerous for thousands of years. Either way, the people on the receiving end mostly aren't born yet.

    you+1+2+3+4+5+8+12+40

    The case for "yes, we owe them": the nineteenth-century philosopher Henry Sidgwick argued that, looked at impartially, when someone lives can't change how much their happiness matters. A pain in the year 2326 matters exactly as much as a pain today. That's the pond again, with years instead of miles.

    Why future people are a strange case:

    • They don't exist yet. Can someone who doesn't exist have rights or claims? Who exactly is holding them?
    • No reciprocity. Reciprocity means give and take: I help you, you help me. Future people can't pay us back, punish us or even thank us. An old joke, with many claimed authors, asks: "Why should I do anything for posterity? What has posterity ever done for me?" If morality is just a deal for mutual benefit, future people are left out of the deal.
    • Uncertainty. We don't know who they'll be, how many, or what they'll want.
    • Power runs one way. We can shape their whole world; they can't touch ours. Some philosophers think that's exactly why our responsibility is so big.

    Discounting: how the future shrinks

    Count the future just 5% less each year, and one death next year outweighs more than a billion deaths 500 years from now.

    Governments and companies often have to compare costs and benefits that land at different times. A standard tool is the discount rate: a percentage by which you count something less for every year it lies in the future. At 3%, something next year counts about 3% less than the same thing today (its weight is 1 ÷ 1.03, about 97%); the year after, about 3% less again; and so on.

    Because the shrinking compounds (each year's cut is taken from what's left after the last one), it snowballs:

    weight today = 1 ÷ (1 + rate)years

    Quick gut check: does a pain 500 years from now matter less than the same pain next year? (Not graded.)

    People give several different reasons for discounting, and philosophers think it's vital to keep them apart:

    • Uncertainty. A future harm might never happen, or we might find a fix. Fair enough, but that's a reason to weigh by probability, not by date.
    • Richer descendants. If future people are wealthier, an extra dollar matters less to them (economists call this diminishing marginal utility: the more you have, the less each extra bit adds). This discounts money, not people's well-being.
    • Investment. Money set aside now can grow, so a dollar today can become more than a dollar later.
    • Pure time preference. Counting a harm less simply because it's later, with no other reason.

    Philosophers attack the last one hardest. The Cambridge mathematician and philosopher Frank Ramsey, in 1928, called discounting later enjoyments "ethically indefensible", a product of weak imagination. Derek Parfit made the point vivid in Reasons and Persons (1984): "At a discount rate of five per cent, one death next year counts for more than a billion deaths in 500 years."

    The careful position many philosophers hold: discount money and goods for good reasons (uncertainty, growth), but don't discount a person's well-being just for being far away in time. (Some economists defend a small pure rate; the debate is live.)

    The non-identity problem

    If your parents had conceived a child one month later, that child would not have been you.

    Start with biology. Which person gets born depends on which egg and which sperm meet. A month later means a different egg, and almost certainly a different sperm: a different child, with different genes. Not you with a different birthday. Someone else.

    Next, a definition. On the most widely used understanding (philosophers debate others), to harm someone is to make them worse off than they would otherwise have been. Call this the "worse off than otherwise" test. A life worth living is one that's good for the person living it, overall, despite its hard parts.

    Now put them together. Here's our version of a puzzle the Oxford philosopher Derek Parfit made famous in Reasons and Persons (1984). (A few other philosophers spotted versions of it around the same time.)

    Now
    Two years later
    Sam and Alex have a child now, in a chaotic year: little money, a cramped apartment, lots of stress. Their child, Jo, has a tougher start but a good life overall.
    If they'd waited two years, they'd have been settled, and their child would have had an easier start. But that child would have been a different person, Kit, not Jo.

    Your call before the reveal: was Jo harmed by the choice to have a child now? (Not graded.)

    Run the test: is Jo worse off than Jo would otherwise have been? Otherwise, Jo wouldn't exist at all. So by the "worse off than otherwise" test, Jo wasn't harmed. Many people still feel the parents could have done better. But better for whom?

    Exhibit A: scale it up (Parfit's "depletion" case, our telling)

    A society can either use up a precious resource now or save it. Using it up makes life slightly better for the next two centuries, then much worse for a long time after. But a choice this big changes who meets whom and when children are conceived. After a few generations, everyone born under "use it up" is a different person from anyone who would have been born under "save it".

    The people living 300 years later still have lives worth living, and none of them would exist otherwise. Who did "use it up" harm? Yet it seems clearly wrong.

    That's the non-identity problem: some choices seem wrong, yet make no particular person worse off, because the choice itself decides who exists. Almost every big long-term choice changes who gets born.

    ?Short on time? Skip this one: another way to think about harm

    Some philosophers reject the "worse off than otherwise" test. They think you can harm someone by causing them a bad condition, like pain or a serious hardship, even if they wouldn't otherwise have existed. On that kind of view, Jo might count as harmed after all, and the puzzle looks different. Many discussions of the problem start from the comparative test, so that's the one this file grades.

    Two ways out: "bad for someone" vs "bad, full stop"

    The puzzle forces a choice between two families of views.

    The person-affecting view

    An outcome can only be worse if it's worse for someone. No victim, no wrong (at least as far as outcomes go).

    The philosopher Jan Narveson put its spirit into a famous slogan in 1973: "We are in favour of making people happy, but neutral about making happy people." (Quotes on this page keep their original British spelling.)

    In its strict (often called "narrow") form, the view compares what happens to each particular person under each option. Someone who wouldn't exist under one option can't be better or worse off because of it. So creating extra happy people adds nothing, and "use it up" wrongs no one.

    Softer ("wide") person-affecting views try to fix this. Instead of tracking named individuals, they compare whoever would exist under each option: if the people of 2326 are worse off under "use it up" than the (different) people of 2326 would be under "save it", that counts against it.

    Do you agree with Narveson's slogan? (Not graded.)

    Impersonal views

    What matters is how much well-being (and suffering) there is in the world, whoever has it. An outcome can be worse even if it's worse for no one in particular.

    In Reasons and Persons (1984), Parfit rejected the idea that what is bad must be bad for someone. He argued that in cases like Sam and Alex, it makes no moral difference whether the better-off child would be the same child or a different one (his No-Difference View). He spent years looking for the right replacement principle; late in life he defended a wide person-affecting principle.

    The trade-off. The strict person-affecting view fits a deep idea (wrongs need victims), but it can't condemn "use it up". Impersonal views condemn it easily, but, as the next screen shows, they lead somewhere very strange.

    The repugnant conclusion

    Parfit named this result himself, and spent decades hunting for a way out of it.

    If what matters is the amount of well-being in the world, how do you add it up? Two simple impersonal rules:

    • The total view: the better world is the one with the greatest total well-being (everyone's well-being added together).
    • The average view: the better world is the one with the highest average well-being per person.

    Picture each world as a box: its width is how many people live there, its height is how good each life is. The total is the area.

    Make Z wide enough and its area beats A's. So the total view says Z is better. Parfit's own statement: "For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better even though its members have lives that are barely worth living." He called that the Repugnant Conclusion.

    So try the average view instead. First, a gut check: which world is better, a world of just one person whose life is very slightly better than anyone's in A, or World A with its ten billion wonderful lives? (Not graded.)

    The average view also says adding a happy person makes the world worse if they're a bit below average. Neither simple rule escapes trouble.

    ?Short on time? Skip this one: where the debate stands

    Some philosophers try a critical level (a life only adds value if it's above some bar). Some deny that "better than" always works in a chain. A few accept the conclusion, arguing it only looks repugnant because we can't picture huge numbers or what "barely worth living" really means. Some have even argued that no theory can avoid every result people find unacceptable. There's no agreed solution.

    Thinking in centuries

    Some of the carbon dioxide released today will still be in the air thousands of years from now.

    Philosophers use long-lasting effects as test cases because they bring every tool in this file into play at once. Two examples, at the level of ideas (this file takes no side on any policy):

    • The climate case. A sizeable share of the carbon dioxide we add to the air stays for centuries, and some of it for many thousands of years. So the costs and benefits of today's choices land across many generations. The discount rate chosen can decide how much those far-off effects count at all. And because big choices change who is born, the non-identity problem applies: most of the people affected far ahead wouldn't exist under a different choice.
    • Long-lived waste. Plutonium-239 builds up in the fuel of ordinary uranium-fueled nuclear reactors, and it takes about 24,000 years for half of it to decay. Any storage plan has to think in hundreds of generations.

    Longtermism is a recent view, made popular by the Oxford philosopher William MacAskill's 2022 book What We Owe the Future. It says that because the future could contain so many people, positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time. Critics push back on three fronts. Tap each one open:

    "We can't see that far."

    We can barely predict the next fifty years, let alone the next thousand, so we can't know which actions help.

    The other side says: some risks (like extinction-level disasters) are bad in almost any future, so reducing them helps whatever happens.

    "What about people suffering now?"

    Focusing on vast future numbers risks neglecting people who need help today.

    The other side says: many long-term actions (like preventing pandemics) also protect people alive now.

    "Tiny chances times huge numbers."

    Multiply a one-in-a-trillion chance by trillions of future lives and you can justify almost anything. Critics call this "fanaticism".

    The other side says: the risks longtermists focus on, like pandemics or nuclear war, aren't one-in-a-trillion. They're in the range of risks people normally take precautions against.

    Notice the trap the non-identity problem sets. If a long-term harm is wrong, it's usually not because it makes particular future people worse off than they'd otherwise be. So whoever says it's wrong needs another explanation: an impersonal view, or a wide person-affecting view.
