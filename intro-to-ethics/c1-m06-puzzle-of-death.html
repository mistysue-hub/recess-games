    The Puzzle of Death

    A 2,300-year-old argument that claims to prove death is nothing to fear, a mirror made of time, a hospital puzzle about yesterday and tomorrow, and a pill that would let you live forever. Would you take it?

    Your toolkit so far

    For three cases you've been asking one question: what makes a life go well for the person living it? That's well-being. You've met the three big families of answers:

    Hedonism (Case 3): only pleasure is good for you in itself, and only pain is bad for you in itself.
    Desire theory (Case 4): your life goes well when the things you want actually happen, whether or not you find out.
    Objective list theories (Case 5): some things, like friendship, knowledge and achievement, are good for you whether or not you want them or enjoy them.

    Plus the Case 2 move: an argument is valid when, if its premises (the starting claims) are true, its conclusion has to be true too. If you think a valid argument's conclusion is false, you must reject at least one premise.

    Today all of it gets stress-tested from the strangest angle there is. Two questions run through this file. Can being dead be bad for the one who's dead? And would never dying be good?

    Epicurus: "nothing to us"

    An ancient philosopher thought he could cure the fear of death with a logic puzzle.

    Epicurus (341 to 270 BCE) taught in Athens, in a house with a garden; his school was simply called "the Garden." He was a hedonist (yes, Case 3's Epicurus), and he thought the best life was a calm one: no pain in the body, no worry in the mind. One worry, he believed, spoils more lives than almost any other: dread of death. So he built an argument to dissolve it. Here it is in our words:

    Exhibit A: Epicurus's argument 1. Something can be bad for you only if you experience it. 2. Once you're dead, you experience nothing at all. So, being dead can't be bad for you.

    He had a catchy second version too, which philosophers call the no-subject argument: while a person exists, their death hasn't happened; once it has, they no longer exist. The two never meet, so there's never a subject (a someone) for it to be bad for.

    Notice how narrow the claim is. It's about being dead, and whether that is bad for the one who is dead. Not about whether dying can hurt, and not about how other people are affected. Those are different questions, and not the ones in this file.

    Now the Case 2 check. The argument is valid: if both premises are true, the conclusion can't be false. So anyone who thinks Epicurus is wrong has to point at a premise and say "that one's false."

    And don't misread him: Epicurus wasn't saying life doesn't matter. He thought a pleasant, peaceful life was the best thing there is. His aim was to let people enjoy it without dread.

    What death takes away

    You can lose something without ever knowing it was coming to you.

    Quick tap: a letter offering you a scholarship gets lost in the mail, and you never learn it existed. Was losing it bad for you?

    If losing it was bad for you, even though you never felt a thing, then Premise 1 is false. Things can be bad for you without being experienced.

    That's the heart of the most popular answer to Epicurus, the deprivation account: something is bad for you when it makes your life go worse than it otherwise would have, by depriving you of (taking away) good things you would have had. The American philosopher Thomas Nagel gave a classic defense of it in a 1970 essay simply called "Death."

    Exhibit B: the comparison test

    To see how bad something is for you, compare two versions of your life: the one where it happens, and the one where it doesn't. The badness is the gap.

    letter arrives
    letter lost

    The striped part is the good you never got. You never feel the stripes, but they're still missing from your life.

    Here's a distinction that cracks the puzzle open. Something is intrinsically bad for you if it's bad in itself, the way pain is. Something is comparatively bad (philosophers also say extrinsically bad) if it's bad because of the good it stops you from having. On the deprivation account, being dead isn't bad the way pain is. It's bad the way the lost letter is.

    Now look at Premise 1 again. "Bad only if experienced" sounds true for intrinsic badness: a pain nobody feels isn't a pain. It sounds false for comparative badness. So the argument may get its power by sliding between the two.

    And the no-subject argument? The deprivationist says death is bad for the person who lived: it's a fact about their life as a whole, which held less good than it would have. (Exactly when it's bad is still argued over; Nagel thought a misfortune doesn't need a date.)

    How your three theories answer

    Plot twist: you can be a hedonist and still disagree with Epicurus.

    Each theory of well-being from Cases 3 to 5 gives its own reason why death can deprive someone:

    Hedonism: being dead isn't painful, so it isn't intrinsically bad. Epicurus stopped there. But a hedonist can still call it comparatively bad: it cuts off all the pleasures the person would have enjoyed. Hedonism and the deprivation account fit together fine.
    Desire theory: death leaves desires unsatisfied: plans never finished, hopes never met. Desire theorists already say a desire can go unsatisfied without the person finding out, so never feeling it is no problem for them.
    Objective list: death takes away future goods on the list, like friendship, knowledge and achievement. They'd have been good for the person whether or not they wanted or felt them.

    So who is still on Epicurus's side? Only someone who says the only way anything can be bad for you is by being an unpleasant experience. That's a much narrower club than "hedonists."

    Lucretius's mirror

    A Roman poet held up a mirror made of time.

    Lucretius (about 99 to about 55 BCE) was a Roman poet and a big fan of Epicurus. He wrote a long poem, On the Nature of Things (in Latin, De rerum natura), that explains Epicurus's ideas in verse, everything from atoms to the weather. In its third book he adds a famous argument.

    Think about all the time that passed before anyone alive today was born: the pyramids being built, the dinosaurs, the whole history of the universe. Nobody frets about having missed it. Lucretius says nature is holding up a mirror: the time after a life is the reflection of the time before it. If nobody is troubled by the first, why should anyone be troubled by the second?

    Exhibit C: the symmetry argument 1. A person's prenatal nonexistence (not existing before they were born) isn't bad for them. 2. A person's posthumous nonexistence (not existing after they die) is just like prenatal nonexistence in every way that matters. So, posthumous nonexistence isn't bad for them either.

    Quick tap: is the mirror a fair comparison?

    Meet Sam, an imaginary person we'll use from here on. Turned against the deprivation account, the mirror looks like this: if Sam dying at 80 instead of 90 deprives Sam of ten good years, doesn't Sam being born in 1999 instead of 1989 deprive Sam of ten good years too? Yet nobody mourns being born late. So either both are bad, or neither is.

    The deprivationist wants to keep "death can be bad" and drop "a late birth is bad." To do that, they need to break the mirror: find a difference between the two stretches that matters.

    Yesterday vs tomorrow

    Most people would rather have had a long unpleasant experience yesterday than face a short one tomorrow.

    The British philosopher Derek Parfit made this vivid in his 1984 book Reasons and Persons. Here's a gentler version of his hospital case.

    Exhibit D: the mix-up (softened)

    You wake up in a hospital bed after a mix-up with the paperwork. You're one of two patients, and the nurse isn't sure which. Patient A had a long, very unpleasant procedure yesterday. Patient B has a much shorter one of the same kind still to come. Either way you'll be given a medicine so you won't remember it afterward, and either way you'll be fine. Which patient do you hope you are?

    Most people hope to be Patient A, even though A's total unpleasantness is far bigger. Parfit called this pattern being biased toward the future: caring more about bad things ahead of you than about equally bad (or worse) things behind you. (In Parfit's original the procedures are painful operations; we've kept it mild.)

    Break-the-mirror attempt 1: future-bias. Maybe the stretch before birth doesn't trouble us for the same reason yesterday's procedure doesn't: it's behind us. If caring more about the future is reasonable, the two stretches really do differ in a way that matters, and premise 2 of the symmetry argument fails. But that's a big "if." Parfit himself argued we'd be better off without the bias: as we aged, we'd have less to look forward to but more to look back on, and what's behind us would count just as much. Philosophers still argue about it.

    Break-the-mirror attempt 2: Sam couldn't have been born earlier. Thomas Nagel's reply. Anyone born substantially earlier than Sam would, Nagel says, have been someone else (one common way to see why: they'd have started from a different egg and sperm). But Sam could easily have lived longer: the same person, with more years. So the time after a life takes away good that could have been that person's; the time before took nothing that could have been. (In a footnote Nagel admitted he was still troubled by the mirror.)

    Would living forever be good?

    A woman who has lived for more than 300 years gives up the chance to keep going.

    If death is bad because of the good it takes away, never dying sounds wonderful: endless good, nothing taken. The British philosopher Bernard Williams (1929 to 2003) thought that was too quick. In a 1973 essay, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality," he argued that death can be bad and that living forever would be bad too.

    His title comes from a 1922 play by the Czech writer Karel Čapek (later an opera by Leoš Janáček). Its heroine, Elina Makropulos, took a potion that has kept her alive for over three centuries. When she finally gets the formula that would renew it, she gives it up. Everything has gone flat for her; nothing matters anymore.

    Quick tap: Elina gives up more life. Is she making a mistake?

    The key term: a categorical desire is a want that doesn't depend on your being alive; it's part of what gives you a reason to keep living. "I want to finish the novel I'm writing" is categorical. A conditional desire like "if I'm alive tomorrow, I want a decent breakfast" tells you what you want if you're around, but gives no reason to be around. Williams says that as long as someone has categorical desires, death is bad for them, because it blocks them. That's his answer to Epicurus.

    Exhibit E: Williams's dilemma for living forever

    Picture an endless life. Either:

    You stay the same person, with the same character and interests. Then over endless time you'd eventually do everything you care about, again and again, and run out of categorical desires. Endless boredom. Or you change enough to keep finding new things fascinating. Then, after enough centuries, the person living that life has so little in common with you that it's no longer clear the life is yours in the way you wanted. Either way, immortality isn't something to wish for.

    So Williams lands in an interesting spot: dying too soon is bad, but a person can also die too late. The best deal is a life long enough for your projects, not an endless or vastly long one. One more view for the next lead. A hedonist would ask just one question about an endless life: will it keep feeling good?

    Williams under fire

    Your favorite song might be the best argument for living forever.

    Not everyone bought the boredom argument. In 1994 the American philosopher John Martin Fischer replied with a distinction:

    • Self-exhausting pleasures lose their pull once you've had them. Once you've finally beaten a particular game or climbed a particular mountain, you may never want to do it again.
    • Repeatable pleasures stay good every time: a favorite song, a great meal, a long walk with a friend. Coming back to them after a break is part of the joy.

    If an endless life could be built around repeatable pleasures, mixed with new projects as you slowly change, maybe boredom never has to win. Williams's defenders reply that over thousands of years even repeatables might wear thin.

    And here's how your three theories handle the question:

    Hedonism: if the pleasures keep coming, more life is more good, forever. Boredom only matters because it feels bad. Fischer's repeatables are good news for the hedonist.
    Desire theory: it depends whether you keep having things to want. Williams's argument runs on desires: once categorical desires run dry, he thinks more life stops being good for you. (Whether that commits him to desire theory is a fair question.)
    Objective list: endless time means endless chances for friendship and knowledge. But some ask whether achievements would mean as much if you had forever to get around to them.
