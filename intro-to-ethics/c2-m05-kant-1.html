    Meet Kant, the rule person

    He changed philosophy without ever traveling far from his home town.

    Immanuel Kant (1724 to 1804) lived in Königsberg, a port city in Prussia. (Today it's Kaliningrad, in Russia.) In 1785 he published the Groundwork, a short, dense book about right and wrong. This whole file is built on it.

    Duty, inclination and four kinds of action

    A duty is something morality requires of you. An inclination is a feeling or want that pulls you toward an action: you love it, enjoy it, or just want it. Kant says an action can be:

    • In line with duty: it's what duty requires, whatever your reason.
    • From duty: you do it because it's your duty. That reason does the work.

    Only actions done from duty have moral worth, Kant's word for the kind of value that earns real moral respect. He sorts actions into four groups:

    1. Against duty. Doing what duty forbids.
    2. Self-interest. The right action, done for a payoff: money, a good reputation, or dodging a fine.
    3. Direct inclination. The right action, done because you want that very thing. Example: keeping yourself alive is a duty, but most of us do it because we want to live.
    4. From duty. The right action, done because it's right, even when no feeling pulls you there. Only this group has moral worth.

    (In the games, "Inclination" means group 3 only. Payoffs and penalties are group 2.)

    Word help: "will" (optional)

    "Will" has three jobs in this file. "I will" is the future. "The will" (as in "good will") is your power to choose and act for reasons. "To will something" means to choose it.

    The only thing that's always good

    Intelligence, courage, even happiness: Kant says every one of them can turn bad.

    The Groundwork opens with a bold claim: there's nothing in the world, or even beyond it, that is good without qualification except a good will. "Without qualification" means good in every situation, with no "it depends".

    Test it. Intelligence is great, until it belongs to a criminal mastermind. Courage is great, until a bully has it. Even happiness can be bad: Kant thinks success and good fortune can make people smug and arrogant if a good will isn't steering them. A cool, calm villain is more dangerous than a nervous one.

    So what is a good will? Roughly: a will that chooses what's right because it's right. That's group 4 from Lead 1. And here's the part that makes Kant Bentham's opposite:

    Exhibit B: the jewel

    Suppose a person tries with everything they've got to do the right thing, and bad luck means they achieve nothing at all. Kant says their good will would still shine like a jewel, all by itself, as something with full value in itself. Success doesn't add to its value. Failure doesn't take any away.

    Quick call (not graded): Nia tries hard to save a stranger's lost dog and fails. Ben, chasing his own frisbee, scares the dog home by accident. Who deserves more moral credit?

    Kant's honest shopkeeper

    The famous shopkeeper example is easy to retell wrong. You already met a modern version in Lead 1: Mo.

    Exhibit C: the shopkeeper, as Kant actually tells it

    A shopkeeper doesn't overcharge customers who don't know the going price. He keeps one fixed price for everybody, so even a child could shop there safely. Customers are treated honestly. But Kant points out that this doesn't show he acted from duty: honesty is simply good for business. And Kant adds that we can't assume he has a warm feeling for his customers either. His verdict: the shopkeeper acted neither from duty nor from a direct inclination, but out of self-interest. Group 2.

    A tempting retelling says the shopkeeper is honest "because he likes his customers". That's not Kant's example; Kant explicitly rules it out. Kant gives separate examples for group 3.

    Quick gut check (not graded): would you call this shopkeeper a good person?

    Your own life. Kant says looking after your life is a duty. But most of us do it simply because we want to live, so it's direct inclination, group 3.

    The kind helper. Kant then describes people with a naturally sympathetic temperament, who feel joy in spreading happiness around them. Their kindness is lovely and deserves praise and encouragement, he says, but it doesn't have true moral worth.

    The grief step. Now imagine one of those people hit by grief so heavy that they stop feeling anything for anyone. If they still help someone, not from feeling but simply because it's their duty, now the action has moral worth.

    Careful: Kant does not say enjoying doing good is bad. The point is about what's doing the work. If the feeling disappeared, would you still do it? A will that would is the good will. (The idea is easy to caricature. The poet Friedrich Schiller, who admired Kant, wrote a famous joke verse about someone who worries he isn't virtuous because he enjoys helping his friends.)

    Two kinds of "ought"

    "You ought to practice piano" and "you ought not to lie" sound alike. Kant says they're completely different animals.

    Kant calls any "ought" that reason gives you an imperative: a command. He splits them into two kinds.

    Exhibit D: hypothetical imperatives

    "If you want X, do Y." The command depends on a goal you happen to have. "If you want to pass the driving test, practice parallel parking." Drop the goal (you don't care about driving) and the command loses its grip on you.

    Kant names two types: rules of skill (how to reach some particular goal, like winning at chess) and counsels of prudence (advice) (how to reach your own happiness, like "save money for later").

    Exhibit E: the categorical imperative

    "Do Y." Full stop. No goal it depends on, no escape clause. It applies to you whatever you want. Kant thinks moral commands are like this: "don't make lying promises" doesn't evaporate if you say "but I don't care about being trusted".

    Your take (not graded): a friend says, "Don't lie to people? I don't care about being trusted, so that rule doesn't apply to me." Is your friend off the hook?

    That's why morality has to be categorical for Kant. If morality were only hypothetical, anyone could get out of it by not wanting the goal. "Don't steal, if you want people to like you." "Cool, I don't care what people think." Done, off the hook? Kant says no way.

    Two detective tips.

    • Hidden ifs. Lots of hypothetical imperatives hide their "if". "Eat your vegetables" really means "if you want to stay healthy, eat your vegetables". Ask: is there a goal this depends on?
    • Fake ifs. The word "if" doesn't make a command hypothetical. "If you've borrowed something, give it back" names a situation, not a goal you want. It applies whether or not you want anything. That one is categorical.

    Maxims: the rule behind what you do

    Two people can do exactly the same thing and be acting on totally different rules.

    Kant thinks whenever you act on purpose, you're following a personal rule, even if you never say it out loud. He calls it a maxim: your own principle of action. A handy way to write one out has three parts:

    When [situation], I will [action], so that [purpose].

    Example: "When I'm tired after school, I will nap for twenty minutes, so that I can concentrate on homework."

    The purpose matters. Remember Kant's shopkeeper? His maxim is something like "when a customer doesn't know the price, I'll charge the normal price, so that I keep my reputation". A different shopkeeper doing the same thing might act on "...so that I treat people fairly". Same action, different maxims. That's how Kant can say one has moral worth and the other doesn't.

    Three ways to write a maxim badly:

    • Leaving out the part that matters. "I'll ask my sister for money" hides the fact that the promise to repay is fake.
    • Writing a feeling, not a rule. "I feel stressed about money" isn't something you do.
    • Stuffing in random detail. "When it's a Tuesday and I'm wearing blue..." Good maxims keep what's relevant to why you act.

    The test: could your rule be a law of nature?

    Kant claims one test can sort every maxim into "allowed" and "forbidden".

    Kant's categorical imperative comes in a few versions. The first is called the Formula of Universal Law. In plain words: only act on a maxim that you could, at the same time, will to be a universal law, a rule that everyone follows. ("Will" here means choose, or sign up for. So the question is: could you choose for everyone to follow your rule?) Kant also gives a vivid version. This one is in Thomas Kingsmill Abbott's classic 19th-century translation:

    Exhibit F: the formula

    "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature."

    ("Thy" is old English for "your".)

    Predict (not graded): when everyone makes lying promises, what breaks first?

    A law of nature is something everyone does automatically, the way objects fall. So the test is a thought experiment in four steps:

    1. Write your maxim. (You did this in Lead 3.)
    2. Universalize it. Imagine a world where everyone acts on that maxim, automatically, always.
    3. Test A, contradiction in conception. Can that world even be conceived (thought through consistently) with your maxim still working in it? Or does the maxim destroy the very thing it needs?
    4. Test B, contradiction in will. If the world can exist, could you consistently will (choose) to live in it, given what every rational person must want?

    Fail either test and acting on that maxim is wrong. Pass both and it's allowed. (Allowed, not necessarily required: most maxims, like "I'll have toast for breakfast", pass and are simply fine.)

    Your hunch (not graded): "I'll copy answers on a test whenever I can get away with it." Could that rule pass both tests?

    Exhibit G: Kant's lying promise, step by step Maxim: when I need money, I'll borrow it by promising to repay, even though I know I never will, so that I get the money. Universalize: everyone who needs money makes false promises to repay, automatically. Test A: in that world, nobody would believe a promise to repay. Kant says people would laugh at such promises as empty words. So the lying promise couldn't even work: the maxim needs a world where promises are trusted, and universalizing it wipes that world out. Verdict: fails Test A. The maxim contradicts itself, so acting on it is wrong.

    Notice what the argument does not say. It doesn't say "trust would collapse, and that would be a bad result". It says your maxim would undermine itself: you'd be relying on everyone else not doing what you're doing. You'd be making an exception of yourself. That's the heart of Kant's idea.

    Before you read on (not graded): could a world where nobody ever helps anybody even exist?

    Test B and the two kinds of duty

    Some maxims pass Test A but still fail. Kant's examples:

    Exhibit H: the person who never helps

    Maxim: "I'll never help anyone in need, so that I keep all my time and money for myself." Universalize it: nobody ever helps anybody. Could that world exist? Sure; humans would survive, grimly. It passes Test A.

    But could you will it? Kant says no. You can't rule out needing help yourself; almost everyone does at some point. Willing this world means willing that you'd be refused help when you need it most. Your will would be at war with itself. Fails Test B.

    Be honest (not graded): would you choose to live in a world where nobody ever helps anybody?

    Exhibit I: the person who lets their talents rust

    Maxim: "I'll never develop any of my abilities, so that I can just relax and enjoy myself." Kant thinks a world like this could exist. But as a rational being you necessarily want your abilities to be developed, because they're useful for all sorts of possible purposes. So you can't consistently will a world where nobody develops any. Fails Test B.

    This gives Kant two kinds of duty:

    Perfect duties
    Imperfect duties
    In Kant's own examples, these come from failing Test A. Never act on the maxim. No exceptions for what you'd like.
    In Kant's own examples, these come from failing Test B. You can't adopt "never", but you have leeway over when and how.
    Example: don't make lying promises.
    Example: help others sometimes; develop some of your talents.

    Kant's original list has four examples. We've skipped one about ending one's own life; it's a heavy topic, and the other three show both tests clearly.

    Four ways people get Kant wrong

    One of the most famous objections to Kant may rest on misreading him.

    Tap each misreading open. Lead 5 opens once you've read all four.

    1. "What if everyone did it? Bad results!"

    People often treat Kant's test as a consequence check: imagine everyone doing it, and if the results would be bad, it's wrong. That turns Kant into Bentham with extra steps. Kantians say the test asks something else: does your maxim defeat itself, or put your will at odds with itself, once it's universal? The lying promise is wrong because it only works if you're the exception, not because a low-trust world is unpleasant.

    The other side says: John Stuart Mill complained in 1861 that Kant never shows a real contradiction, only that everyone doing it would have results nobody would choose. Whether the Kantian reply fully answers Mill is still argued about.

    2. "What if everyone became a dentist?"

    "I'll become a dentist. But what if everyone did? Nobody would grow food! So it's wrong." Obviously not. Two mistakes: it's consequence thinking (misreading 1), and the maxim is written badly. What you're really acting on is closer to "I'll choose a career I like from the ones open to me". Universalize that and nothing contradicts itself.

    The other side says: if you can always rewrite a maxim until it passes, can't a clever cheater do the same? Kantians answer that a maxim has to state your real reasons.

    3. "It's just the Golden Rule"

    The Golden Rule asks what you would mind. Kant's test asks whether your maxim could be a law for everyone without contradiction, whatever you'd mind. In a footnote, Kant says the familiar rule "don't do to others what you wouldn't want done to you" can't serve as a universal law. One of his reasons: plenty of people would happily agree to get no help from others, as long as they never had to help anyone.

    The other side says: the two often give the same answer in everyday cases, which is why people mix them up.

    4. "Kant says feelings are bad"

    Enjoying doing good isn't wrong. Feelings just can't be the thing that gives an action moral worth. Kant's question is whether you'd still do it if the feeling were gone.

    The other side says: a friend who visits you only "from duty" sounds a bit cold. Many philosophers think Kant undervalues love and care here.

    One more thing to keep straight: the test tells you whether an action is permitted. Moral worth is a separate question about your motive. The profit-minded shopkeeper's maxim passes the test (his action is fine), but it still has no moral worth.
