    How Do We Know Right From Wrong?

    Nobody has ever spotted wrongness under a microscope. So where does moral knowledge come from? A runaway trolley, a philosopher's balancing trick, and some unsettling news about where your gut feelings were made.

    Think of something you're sure is wrong: say, humiliating someone in front of everyone, just for laughs. You don't need a textbook to tell you. But is that knowledge, like knowing 7 × 8 = 56? Or just a very strong feeling? Lock in your gut call now. You'll make the call again at the end.

    Where we are

    The last two case files asked whether morality is objective: true no matter what anyone thinks. Case 1 asked if God's commands could make things right or wrong. Case 2 asked if it's all just opinion or culture.

    This file asks a different question, and it's a sneaky one. Suppose there are moral truths. How could we ever find out what they are? You can't weigh kindness, photograph unfairness, or run cruelty through a lab test. If moral truths exist but nobody can know them, that's almost as awkward as there being none.

    Toolkit from earlier: a counterexample is a case where a general claim gives the wrong answer. You'll use it a lot today.

    How do we know anything?

    Many philosophers treat your gut reactions to imaginary cases the way scientists treat lab results.

    The study of knowledge is called epistemology (from epistēmē, ancient Greek for knowledge). Its question: what makes a belief more than a lucky guess? Moral epistemology asks that question about right and wrong.

    Start with ordinary knowledge. Most of what you know comes from three places:

    SeeingYour senses: sight, hearing, touch. Philosophers call this perception.
    Being toldTrusting a teacher, a book, a map app. Philosophers call this testimony.
    Working it outLogic and math: getting from what you know to something new. That's reasoning.

    Now try morality. Seeing? Your eyes can see someone shove a kid; they can't see the wrongness, the way they see the color of his jacket. Being told? Fine, but how did the person who told you know? Working it out? Reasoning needs starting points. Where do the first moral premises come from?

    Exhibit A: the fourth source

    A moral intuition is a moral claim that seems true to you when you think about it, before any argument and not just because someone said so. "Punishing someone for a crime everyone knows she didn't commit is unfair." You don't deduce that. You consider it, and it just seems right.

    Many philosophers say intuitions are evidence: the raw data a moral theory has to fit, just as a scientific theory has to fit observations. The Oxford philosopher W. D. Ross said something close to this in 1930: the moral convictions of thoughtful, well-educated people play the same role in ethics that sense perceptions play in science.

    Evidence doesn't have to be perfect. A stick in a glass of water looks bent. Your eyes still give you evidence, but it can be overturned. Philosophers call evidence like that defeasible (it can be "defeated"). Intuitions are defeasible too. Keep that word: the whole second half of this file is about what could defeat them.

    The trolley files

    The most famous moral puzzle of the last 60 years began as a side example in a paper about something else.

    In 1967 the British philosopher Philippa Foot imagined the driver of a runaway tram who can steer onto a side track: kill one person instead of five. Her paper was mostly about other questions. Then the American philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson took the idea and ran with it. Her 1976 paper gave the puzzle its name, the trolley problem, and in 1985 she wrote up the versions below. Philosophers have been arguing about them ever since.

    In every case a runaway trolley is about to kill five people on the track. And in every case, the numbers are the same: act, and five live while one dies.

    1. Switch

    You're standing by a lever. Pull it and the trolley turns onto a side track, where it will kill one person instead.

    2. Loop

    Same lever, but the side track loops back to the five. The trolley stops only because it hits the one person on the loop. His body is what saves them.

    3. Footbridge

    No lever. You're on a bridge over the track next to a stranger carrying a huge, heavy pack. Push him off and his weight stops the trolley. (You're too light to stop it yourself.)

    Here's why philosophers love these cases. The numbers never change. If only numbers mattered, everyone would give the same answer to all three. When people's verdicts split between cases, something besides the numbers is steering them. Changing one detail at a time and watching the verdicts move is like a scientist changing one variable at a time. The cases are a lab for intuitions.

    A huge online study, published in 2020, put versions of these three cases to about 70,000 people in 42 countries and asked what the person at the lever or on the bridge should do. Lead 2 shows what they said, after you guess.

    Hunting for the rule behind the verdicts

    If your verdicts aren't just about numbers, what are they about? Nobody has fully cracked it.

    A principle is a general rule meant to cover many cases at once, like "never kill an innocent person." Philosophers look for principles for two reasons. A principle that fits all your intuitions might explain them, the way a law of physics explains many observations. And once you have a principle, you can use it on new cases where your gut is silent.

    A principle fits a case if it gives the same verdict as your intuition. A case where it gives the opposite verdict is a counterexample to it. Here are four candidates for the trolley cases. "Act" means pull the lever or push.

    The Numbers rule: always do whatever saves the most lives.
    The No-Killing rule: never do anything that kills an innocent person, even to save more. (Pulling a lever that sends a trolley into someone counts as killing him.)
    The Means rule: never use a person's body as your tool for saving others when doing so will kill him. A death that's only a side effect of saving them is allowed.
    The Hands-Off rule: never kill someone with your own physical force, like a push. Causing a death from a distance, with a switch, is allowed.

    Look closely at the Means rule. In Switch, the one person's death is a side effect: if he weren't there, the five would be saved just the same. In Footbridge, the stranger's body is how you save them. That difference is the heart of an old idea called the doctrine of double effect, usually traced back to the medieval philosopher Thomas Aquinas. Foot's 1967 paper was largely about it.

    But now think about Loop. The one person on the loop isn't a side effect: his body is what stops the trolley, exactly as in Footbridge. Thomson built Loop to make trouble for the Means rule.

    Reflective equilibrium: the balancing trick

    The most widely used method in moral philosophy works a bit like tuning a guitar.

    So principles and intuitions clash. Which one wins? The American philosopher John Rawls (1921 to 2002) gave the best-known answer in A Theory of Justice (1971), where he named the method reflective equilibrium. He borrowed the core idea from the philosopher Nelson Goodman, who described how we justify the rules of logical reasoning in a similar way.

    Exhibit B: the method 1. Collect your considered judgments: verdicts you hold with confidence, made calmly, with the facts in front of you and nothing to gain. (Not judgments made in a rage, or when you have money riding on the answer.) 2. Propose principles that would explain them. 3. Where they clash, revise. Sometimes you change the principle. Sometimes the principle is so convincing that you change the judgment. Repeat until principles and judgments support each other. That balance is the "equilibrium."

    Notice that nothing is untouchable. A principle isn't automatically right because it's neat, and an intuition isn't automatically right because it's strong. Each can correct the other. That's the tuning: tighten one string, check it against the others, adjust again.

    Keeping a principle and giving up the clashing intuition has a nickname: biting the bullet. The most famous bullet-biter is Immanuel Kant. In a 1797 essay he argued that lying is wrong even to a would-be murderer who asks whether your friend is hiding in your house. Most readers find that verdict very hard to accept. Kant accepted it to keep his principle.

    Rawls himself also described a wide version, and the philosopher Norman Daniels (1979) developed it: check your principles and judgments against everything else you have good reason to believe, including scientific theories about people and society. Hold on to that. It's exactly where trouble arrives in a few screens.

    Fast thinking, slow thinking

    In 2001, scientists put people in brain scanners and asked them trolley questions.

    The psychologist and philosopher Joshua Greene and his colleagues at Princeton published the study in the journal Science. Their finding, roughly: up-close, hands-on cases like Footbridge showed more activity in brain areas linked to emotion than remote cases like Switch.

    Greene built a theory on this, often called the dual-process theory of moral judgment. We have two systems. One is fast: automatic, emotional, gut-level. The other is slow: deliberate, effortful, calculating. In his book Moral Tribes (2013) he compares them to a camera's automatic settings and its manual mode. (The fast/slow language was popularized more widely by the psychologist Daniel Kahneman's 2011 book Thinking, Fast and Slow.)

    On Greene's picture, the alarm that screams "don't push!" is the fast system reacting to up-close personal force. Automatic settings are great in situations our ancestors met over and over. But in strange new situations, like a trolley and a footbridge, Greene argues they can misfire, and the slow system deserves more say.

    Quick check (not graded): imagine your hands on the stranger's back on the footbridge. Does something in you flinch first, before any reasoning?

    Later researchers questioned parts of the original data, and the clean split between "emotion" and "reason" looks messier than it first seemed. The philosopher Selim Berker argued in 2009 that brain scans alone can't tell us which intuitions are right; you need a moral argument for that. The strongest version of Greene-style arguments supplies one: some intuitions respond to features that don't seem to matter morally.

    That move, "your intuition responds to something that shouldn't matter, so trust it less," has a name. It's a debunking argument, and it's the next screen.

    Where did your intuitions come from?

    Imagine finding out your strongest belief was put there by a pill.

    Suppose you're certain your team will win on Saturday. Then you learn you swallowed a pill that makes people certain their team will win, whatever the facts. Your certainty hasn't changed. But you should trust it less, because now you know where it came from, and that source would have produced it whether or not it was true.

    Your call (not graded): once you know about the pill, should you trust your certainty less?

    Exhibit D: the debunking argument 1. You believe X because of process P. 2. P would have given you belief X whether or not X is true. So the fact that you believe X gives you no evidence that X is true.

    Now swap the pill for things that really did shape your moral intuitions:

    • Evolution. Caring for your children, paying back favors, and punishing cheats probably helped our ancestors survive and pass on their genes. The philosopher Sharon Street (in a 2006 paper she called "A Darwinian Dilemma") pushes this hard. Either evolution's pressures had nothing to do with moral truth, and then it would be a fluke if our values matched it; or they did track the truth, but the simpler scientific story is that those values just helped our ancestors survive, no moral truth needed. Richard Joyce, in The Evolution of Morality (2006), argues along related lines that evolution can explain our moral beliefs without their being true.
    • Culture. What seems obviously right depends a lot on where and when you grew up. Some things that once seemed obviously fine now seem obviously wrong.
    • Mood and framing. In a well-known 1981 study, the psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed that people choose differently between the same two plans depending on whether the outcomes are described as lives saved or lives lost. In other studies, a funny video clip just beforehand made people more willing to say pushing in Footbridge was OK. A good source of evidence shouldn't flip with the wording or your mood.

    The philosopher Kwame Anthony Appiah titled a whole chapter of his book Experiments in Ethics (2008) "The Case Against Intuition." So: is this the end of moral knowledge? Not so fast. Tap each reply to open it.

    Reply 1: companions in guilt. Evolution shaped your math skills too...

    Our ability to count and reason evolved too. Hardly anyone thinks that debunks arithmetic. If evolution doesn't wreck math, maybe it doesn't wreck all of ethics.

    The other side says: math beliefs can be checked against proofs and the world; moral beliefs have no such outside check.

    Reply 2: overlap. What helped survival might also be good...

    Maybe what helped our ancestors survive overlaps with what's actually good. Pain feeling bad helped them survive, and pain may also really be bad.

    The other side says: then the overlap is doing all the work, and it would be a lucky coincidence.

    Reply 3: debunk selectively. Throw out some intuitions, not all...

    Greene doesn't debunk everything. He targets intuitions that respond to features that don't seem to matter, like whether you used your hands. That fits the wide reflective equilibrium from earlier: the science becomes one more thing your principles and judgments have to fit.

    The other side says: deciding which features "don't matter" already uses moral intuitions.

    How moral minds actually change

    In 1829 the sitting British Prime Minister, the Duke of Wellington, fought a duel. (Both men walked away unhurt.)

    Culture doesn't only bend intuitions the wrong way. Sometimes it's how they get fixed. Appiah's book The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen (2010) looks at moral changes such as the end of dueling among British gentlemen and the end of foot-binding in China, a practice that had painfully deformed girls' feet for centuries.

    His surprising finding: the arguments against these practices were around long before the practices ended. For centuries dueling had been condemned as murderous and irrational, by the law, by the churches and by moral critics. What finally shifted things, Appiah argues, was honor: dueling started to look ridiculous rather than noble, and foot-binding came to be seen as a source of national shame. Knowing the argument wasn't enough. People changed when what they respected changed.

    Your guess (not graded): what changes people's minds more?

    Two lessons for this file:

    • Debunking cuts both ways. If social forces shaped your intuitions, some of those forces may have been pushing toward the truth, not away from it.
    • Knowing isn't the same as being moved. Reflective equilibrium is about what you have reason to believe. Appiah's history is a reminder that people often don't act on the reasons they already have.
