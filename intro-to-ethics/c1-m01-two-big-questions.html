    The two big questions

    Plato wrote a version of your button about 2,400 years ago.

    Ethics is the part of philosophy about how to live. Most of its questions land under one of two headings.

    Question one: well-being

    What makes a life go well for the person living it? Not "is this right?" but "is this actually good for me?" Pleasure, health, friendship, getting what you want, achieving things: these are all candidates for what makes a life go well, and philosophers fight about which ones really count. A reason of this kind is sometimes called a prudential reason.

    Question two: morality

    What do we owe each other? Fairness, honesty, keeping promises, not harming people. When you ask "would this be wrong?" you've switched questions. A handy test: when a reason is about someone else's good, or what you owe them, it's on the moral side.

    Look at the button. "$1,000 would be great for me" answers question one. "A stranger loses $1,000 they did nothing to lose" answers question two. Same choice, two completely different kinds of reasons.

    Some facts aren't reasons of either kind. "The button is red" is true, but it doesn't count for or against pressing it.

    And there's a third question lurking behind both: is any of this objectively true, or is it all just opinion? Philosophers call that one metaethics. Park it. We're coming back for it later in the course.

    When the questions collide

    Most of the time the two questions agree. Being honest tends to keep your friendships alive; being cruel tends to make you miserable. But not always.

    Quick, honest gut check: you find a ring that makes you invisible whenever you like. Would you behave exactly the same as you do now?

    Exhibit A: the oldest version of your button

    In Plato's Republic, written around 2,400 years ago, a character named Glaucon tells a story about a shepherd (called Gyges in many translations) who finds exactly that ring. With nobody able to see him, he uses it to seize power for himself. Glaucon's challenge: if you could never get caught, would you still have any reason to be just? Or are people only good because they're afraid of being seen?

    "Nobody will ever know" is the Ring of Gyges in button form. Socrates spends most of the rest of the book trying to prove that being just is actually good for the just person.

    Fast forward to 1874. In The Methods of Ethics, the Cambridge philosopher Henry Sidgwick found two principles that both seemed self-evident to him on careful reflection:

    Do what's best for you
    Do what's best for everyone, you counting as one
    Usually: they point the same way.
    Sometimes: they split. (Hello, button.)
    Sidgwick's verdict: he couldn't prove either one always wins. He called the standoff the dualism of practical reason.

    So when the button feels hard, that's not you being confused. You've walked into a problem Sidgwick never managed to declare a winner on.

    How philosophers fight (politely)

    An argument can be perfectly logical and still completely wrong. Next case file, you'll see how.

    In philosophy an argument isn't a shouting match. It's a package: some claims, called premises, offered as support for one more claim, the conclusion.

    Exhibit B: an argument, laid out flat 1. Anyone on the team has to come to practice. 2. Jordan is on the team. So, Jordan has to come to practice.

    In real life arguments come scrambled. The conclusion might be first, last, or stuck in the middle. Watch for signpost words:

    • so, therefore, which means, that's why usually come right before a conclusion.
    • because, since, given that, after all usually come right before a premise.

    Premises can also be chained on with plain and. And not every sentence in a paragraph is part of the argument. Some are just vibes.
