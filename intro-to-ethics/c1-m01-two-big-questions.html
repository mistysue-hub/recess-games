    MeSomeone else

    The two big questions

    Ethics is the part of philosophy about how to live. Most of its questions fall under two headings. Every card in the warm-up was one of them: "me" was question one, "someone else" was question two.

    Question one: well-being

    What makes a life go well for the person living it? Not "is this right?" but "is this actually good for me?" Pleasure, health, friendship and getting what you want are all candidates.

    Question two: morality

    What do we owe each other? Fairness, honesty, keeping promises, respecting what belongs to others, not harming people. A handy test: a reason about someone else's good (their well-being, their feelings) is a moral reason for you, because it's what you owe them.

    The button has both. "$1,000 would be great for me" answers question one. "A stranger loses $1,000 they did nothing to lose" answers question two.

    Some facts are neither. "The button is red" is true, but it counts for nothing either way.

    Optional: more on question one, and a third question

    Philosophers fight about which things really make a life go well. A reason of that kind is sometimes called a prudential reason.

    A third question sits behind both: is any of this objectively true (true for everyone), or is it all just opinion? Philosophers call the study of questions like that metaethics. We come back to it later in the course.

    When the questions collide

    Plato wrote a version of your button about 2,400 years ago.

    Most of the time the two questions agree. Being honest tends to keep your friendships alive; being cruel tends to make you miserable. But not always.

    Quick, honest gut check: you find a ring that makes you invisible whenever you like. Would you behave exactly the same as you do now?

    Exhibit A: the oldest version of your button

    In Plato's Republic, written around 2,400 years ago, a character named Glaucon tells a story about a shepherd (often named Gyges in English) who finds exactly that ring. With nobody able to see him, he uses it to take the throne for himself. Glaucon is playing devil's advocate: he argues the harsh side on purpose, to test the idea. His challenge: if you could never get caught, would you still have any reason to be just? Or are people only good because they're afraid of being seen?

    "Nobody will ever know" is the Ring of Gyges in button form. Socrates spends most of the rest of the book trying to prove that being just is actually good for the just person.

    Fast forward to 1874. The Cambridge philosopher Henry Sidgwick wrote The Methods of Ethics. He found two principles that both seemed self-evident to him: obviously true once he thought hard about them.

    Do what's best for you
    Do what's best for everyone, you counting as one
    Usually: they point the same way.
    Sometimes: they split, like in the button.
    Sidgwick's verdict: he couldn't prove either one always wins. He called the standoff the dualism of practical reason: two rival guides for deciding what to do.

    Which way do you lean? No right answer. When the two principles split, which one do you usually follow?

    So when the button feels hard, that's not you being confused. You've run into a problem Sidgwick never managed to settle.

    How philosophers fight (politely)

    An argument can be perfectly logical and still completely wrong. Next case file, you'll see how.

    In philosophy an argument isn't a shouting match. It's a package: some claims, called premises, offered as support for one more claim, the conclusion.

    Exhibit B: an argument, laid out flat 1. Anyone on the team has to come to practice. 2. Jordan is on the team. So, Jordan has to come to practice.

    In real life arguments come scrambled. The conclusion might be first, last, or stuck in the middle. Watch for signpost words:

    • so, therefore, which means, that's why usually come right before a conclusion.
    • because, since, given that, after all usually come right before a premise.

    Premises can also be chained on with plain and. And not every sentence in a paragraph is part of the argument. Some are just side comments.
