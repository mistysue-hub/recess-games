    The Greatest Happiness

    One rule meant to settle every moral question. It led an 18th-century lawyer to argue for animals and against cruel laws, well ahead of the laws of his day. It also seems to give a surgeon some truly terrible advice.

    The one-rule offer: some philosophers think all of morality comes down to this. Do whatever makes things go best overall, counting everyone's happiness equally.     Same man, new question

    You've met Jeremy Bentham (1748 to 1832) before. In Challenge 1 he was the pleasure-counter, with a checklist for measuring how much pleasure an experience holds. That was about well-being: what makes a life go well for the person living it.

    This file asks the other big question: what makes an action right or wrong? A theory of right action is an answer to that. It isn't a list of rules like "don't steal". It's the reason behind the list: the one feature that all right actions share.

    So far this challenge has asked whether morality can be objective and how we could ever know right from wrong. Now come the great theories: Bentham first, then Kant, then Aristotle.

    Exhibit A: words you'll need

    Consequences are the results of an action: everything that turns out differently because you did it. A consequentialist theory says the rightness of an action depends only on its consequences. Utilitarianism, Bentham's theory, is the most famous kind.

    Bentham's move, in one breath: if well-being is the only thing that's good in itself, then (he reasoned) the right thing to do is whatever produces the most of it. For everyone.

    The Greatest Happiness Principle

    Bentham's most famous slogan wasn't originally his.

    In 1776, in a book he published without his name on it, A Fragment on Government, Bentham announced what he called a fundamental axiom: "it is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong." The phrase was older; Bentham built an entire system on it.

    ?Short on time? Skip this one: who said it first, and what's "the community"?

    Earlier writers, including the philosopher Francis Hutcheson in 1725, had used versions of the phrase. And "the community"? Bentham said it's a kind of fiction. It's only the people in it, and its interest is just their interests added together.

    In An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789) he called it the principle of utility: judge every action, whether it's done by one person or by a whole government, by whether it tends to increase or decrease the happiness of everyone it affects. ("Utility" here just means usefulness for producing happiness.)

    An act is right if no other act you could do instead would produce more total well-being, counting everyone affected.

    Five ideas are packed into that one line:

    Consequences only. Results decide. Not intentions, not traditions, not rules for their own sake.
    Well-being only. The only result that matters in itself is how well lives go. (For Bentham: pleasure and the absence of pain.)
    Add it up. Gains and losses for different people go into one total. Philosophers call this aggregation.
    Everyone counts as one. John Stuart Mill passed on a slogan he credited to Bentham: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one." Your happiness, your best friend's and a total stranger's all weigh the same. This is called impartiality.
    Maximize. On the classic version, doing some good isn't enough. The right act is the best one available, and anything less is wrong.

    Quick gut check (not graded): "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" names two things. When they clash, which should win?

    Here's the official answer. The two usually agree, but when they clash (most happiness in total versus most people helped), utilitarians go with the total. Lead 1 puts that to the test.

    Who counts?

    In 1789, in a footnote in a book about law, Bentham asked a question animal-welfare thinkers still quote today.

    Impartiality is a radical idea. No extra weight for yourself, your family, your country, your religion or your skin color. To get into the sum, it doesn't matter who you are. It only matters whether things can go better or worse for you.

    So where's the edge of the circle? Bentham's answer comes in a footnote in the Introduction. The French, he noted, had already realized that dark skin is no reason to abandon a human being to cruelty. One day, he hoped, people would see that the number of legs an animal has is no better a reason. Then comes the famous line:

    Exhibit B: the test

    "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

    A being that can feel pleasure and pain is called sentient. For a utilitarian, sentience is the ticket into the moral sum. That has consequences:

    • Distance doesn't matter. Pain on the other side of the world hurts exactly as much as pain next door.
    • Time doesn't matter. Someone who will be born in 2150 will feel just as much as you do. When they're born doesn't change that.
    • Things that can't feel don't count in themselves. A river or a statue isn't in the sum. But what happens to it can still matter, because of its effects on beings who can feel: a poisoned river hurts the people and fish who depend on it.

    Your guess (not graded): can a fish feel pain?

    Can a fish feel pain? An insect? A future AI? Those are questions for science. Bentham's test doesn't answer them. It tells you which question to ask.

    Bentham the reformer

    Around 1785 Bentham wrote an essay arguing that sex between consenting men should not be a crime. Guess how long it waited to be published.

    Bentham pointed his principle at the laws of his day and kept finding needless suffering. Three of his positions were well ahead of those laws:

    Exhibit C: animals

    That "Can they suffer?" footnote belongs to a passage complaining that the law ignored animals' interests. If an animal can suffer, its suffering goes in the sum, and cruelty to it is a real harm.

    Exhibit D: slavery

    Bentham condemned the slave trade, at a time when a large British abolition movement was already campaigning against it. In a 1789 newspaper letter he mocked the idea of paying compensation to slave traders, asking, in effect, whether stealing handkerchiefs was worse than stealing people. For ending slave-holding itself, he favored a step-by-step plan meant to make sure freed people had a living and were safe. Historians still argue about how far he went.

    Exhibit E: sex laws

    In Bentham's England, sex between men could be punished by death. In an essay written around 1785 he argued that it should not be a crime at all.

    Guess (not graded): when was that essay first published?

    1978, almost two centuries later. Bentham never published it. His argument was the utilitarian test: whose happiness do these private, consenting acts reduce? Nobody's, he argued, while the punishments caused terrible suffering. So the law was making the world worse. Its 1978 editor, the scholar Louis Crompton, called it the first known argument for reforming these laws in England.

    ?Short on time? Skip this one: the prison Bentham designed

    Not every idea aged well. In 1791 Bentham published plans for the Panopticon, a round prison in which a hidden guard could watch every cell at once. Bentham saw it as a reform: prisoners would be reformed by being watched, and the contractor running it would be fined for every prisoner who died. It was never built the way he planned it, and today it's a symbol of constant surveillance.

    "Nonsense upon stilts." Many reformers argue from natural rights: rights you have just by being human, before any law gives them to you. Bentham thought that idea was confused. Attacking the French Declaration of the Rights of Man, he called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts". His reforms rested on one thing only: their effects on happiness. That's a strength (no mysterious extras) and, as you'll see later, a possible weakness (no rights to protect you if the sum goes against you).

    What counts as a consequence?

    To a utilitarian, "but I meant well" is mostly beside the point.

    "Look at the consequences" sounds simple. It hides three big choices (and a bonus one about motives).

    1. Compared with what?

    A consequence is the difference your act makes compared with what would have happened if you'd done something else, including doing nothing. You never judge an act alone; you judge it against the alternatives.

    2. All of them, for everyone

    Not just the obvious, immediate result. Side effects and long-run effects count too: whether people can still trust you, the habits you're building, the example you set. A "harmless" lie can become harmful once you count what happens to trust if it comes out, or what it does to your habit of honesty.

    3. Actual or expected?

    You can't see the future. Bentham talked about an action's tendency: what it's likely to bring about. Many utilitarians judge a choice by its expected value: each possible outcome multiplied by its chance of happening, then added up.

    Your call (not graded): a sure +60, or a 50% shot at +100 (and a 50% chance of nothing)?

    Luck can still make the actual result better or worse than expected.

    ?Short on time? Skip this one: what about motives?

    What about motives?

    Bentham argued that no motive is good or bad in itself. The same desire, say wanting money, can drive honest hard work or a burglary. It's the effects that differ. Motives still matter for judging people, because they predict what someone will do next. But for a utilitarian they don't decide whether this act was right.

    Neat twist: praising and blaming are acts too. So a utilitarian should praise and blame in whatever ways do the most good. That might mean not blaming someone who did her honest best and got unlucky, even if her act turned out wrong.

    Act or rule?

    If everyone cut across the lawn, there'd be no lawn. So should you?

    Philosophers now split utilitarianism into two main versions:

    Act utilitarianism
    Rule utilitarianism
    Judge each act by its own consequences. "Don't lie" is a handy rule of thumb, but you break it whenever breaking it does more good.
    Find the rules that work out best when nearly everyone follows them. Then an act is right if it follows those rules.
    The lawn: one shortcut across the grass does no harm and saves you time. Cut across.
    The lawn: "Keep off the grass" works out best when nearly everyone follows it. Keep off.
    Both are utilitarian: in the end, everything is justified by consequences for well-being. They differ on what gets judged by those consequences.

    Which sounds more like you (not graded)?

    Rule utilitarianism fits common sense better: keep promises, don't punish the innocent, even when breaking the rule looks useful this once. But it faces a famous dilemma. If breaking the rule really would do more good this time, isn't sticking to it just rule-worship? And if you patch the rule ("keep off the grass, unless nobody will notice"), keep patching, and you end up back at act utilitarianism.

    Which camp was Bentham in? He is usually read as an act utilitarian, since he stated his principle for every single action. But he wrote mostly about laws, which are rules for everyone, so some scholars think the picture is more complicated.

    Objection file 1: the surgeon

    The most famous objection to utilitarianism happens in a hospital.

    Here's a case made famous by the philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson, in our own words. A surgeon has five patients, each of whom will die soon without a different organ transplant. A healthy young man comes in for a check-up, and he happens to be a perfect match for all five. If the surgeon secretly ended his life and used his organs, five people would live instead of one. Five beats one.

    Your call (not graded): would the surgeon be doing the right thing?

    Nearly everyone says it would be monstrous. Two objections grow out of cases like this.

    Objection A: rights (using a person)

    Some things you may not do to a person, even to produce more good overall. The healthy man has a right not to be killed, and that right isn't up for trade just because the numbers favor it. Utilitarianism seems to allow using people as mere tools. (Kant builds a whole theory around that complaint. You'll meet that part of it in Case 6.)

    Objection B: fair shares vs the total

    Only the total counts, so how it's shared doesn't matter in itself. Compare two plans for 100 people. Plan X: everyone gets 10 happiness points, a total of 1,000. Plan Y: 50 people get 25 and 50 get nothing, a total of 1,250. The theory picks Y. In the same way, a policy that makes a small minority miserable can win if the majority gains enough.

    Think back to slavery. Utilitarians condemned it because its suffering hugely outweighed any gains. Critics reply: right answer, wrong reason. Slavery would be unjust even in a world where the sums somehow came out the other way.

    Objection file 2: too much, too merged

    Objection C: demandingness (it asks too much)

    Maximizing means anything less than the best is wrong. The money you'd spend on concert tickets could do more good given to an effective charity. The free afternoon you'd spend on your hobby could go to tutoring a kid who's struggling. It's hard to find a moment when the theory doesn't require you to do more. It seems to leave almost no room for your own life, projects and people. (Challenge 3 comes back to this with the philosopher Peter Singer.)

    Does that one land for you (not graded)?

    Objection D: the separateness of persons

    Inside one life, trade-offs make sense. The dentist hurts you today so your teeth are healthy for years. The person who pays is the person who gains. Utilitarianism makes the same trade between people: your pain is "paid for" by my pleasure, as if society were one giant person. But nobody experiences the total. In A Theory of Justice (1971), John Rawls argued that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons.

    How these fit together: D is a diagnosis. It explains why B happens. Because the theory merges everyone into one sum, it can't see that the losers in Plan Y are real, separate people who get nothing back.

    The utilitarian fights back

    Utilitarians have answers. Some of them are surprisingly good.

    Tap each reply to open it. Each one comes with what the other side says back.

    Reply 1 (to the surgeon): what would hospitals become?

    Count all the consequences. If surgeons did this, everyone would be terrified of hospitals and nobody would go for check-ups. Add in the fear, the lost trust and the chance of being found out, and the sum says: don't.

    The other side says: then make it perfectly secret. The theory still seems to say yes.

    Reply 2 (to the surgeon): some rules are worth keeping

    Go rule. "Doctors never harm one patient to help others" is a rule that works out best when nearly everyone follows it. A rule utilitarian keeps it, even in the secret case.

    The other side says: rule-worship, or a slide back into act utilitarianism (see "Act or rule?").

    Reply 3 (to fair shares): a dollar means more to the poor

    Diminishing returns. An extra $100 means far more to someone with almost nothing than to a millionaire. Extra resources produce less and less well-being the more you already have. So maximizing the total usually means spreading things more equally, not less.

    The other side says: "usually" isn't "always". In Plan Y the points were already happiness, so the trick doesn't help.

    Reply 4 (to demandingness): right isn't the same as blameworthy

    The theory tells you what's best. But blaming is an act too, and condemning everyone who isn't a saint would do little good. A sensible utilitarian praises people for doing more good than before, not for being perfect.

    The other side says: the act is still called wrong. Some utilitarians bite the bullet: maybe morality really is that demanding.

    Reply 5 (to separateness): equal counting is respect

    Every person's well-being goes into the sum separately, with exactly the same weight as anyone else's. Nobody's interests are ignored. What could taking persons seriously mean, if not that?

    The other side says: counting everyone isn't the same as protecting anyone.

    Reply 6 (to all of them): maybe your gut is wrong

    Bite the bullet. Maybe our gut reactions are the mistake. They were shaped by life in small groups, not for cases involving huge numbers. And utilitarians were sometimes right while gut feelings were wrong: about animals and about sex laws.

    The other side says: the track record cuts both ways. Bentham also designed the Panopticon, a prison that became a symbol of constant surveillance.
