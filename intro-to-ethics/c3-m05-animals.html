    What Do We Owe Animals?

    A footnote from 1789 that later inspired a movement, scientists signing declarations about octopus minds, and a very old question with very new evidence: whose pain counts?

    Heads-up before you start. This file talks about how people use animals (for food, research and as pets), but only at the level of ideas: no graphic details. Nobody here will tell you what to eat or how to live, and nothing you eat is graded. Every lead has a Skip button; no reason needed.

    A dog, an octopus and a person each get hurt in exactly the same way, with the same amount of pain. Does it matter morally? Does it matter equally? Lock in your gut call now. At the end of the file you'll make the call again, knowing what philosophers know.

    Moral status, and what this file won't do

    Kicking a rock and kicking a dog are both kicks. Only one can be unfair to the thing you kicked.

    A being has moral status if it matters morally for its own sake: what happens to it can wrong it, not just damage it. You have moral status. A rock doesn't.

    Exhibit A: wronged, or just damaged?

    Scratch your neighbor's car and you've done something wrong. But who did you wrong? Your neighbor, not the car. The car has no moral status; it only matters because of the person who owns it. Philosophers call that kind of concern indirect. Concern for a being for its own sake is direct.

    So the big question of this file: are animals more like your neighbor or more like the car? Or somewhere in between?

    Before we start, one tool that keeps this conversation fair. Statements about animals come in three very different kinds:

    Science claimsWhat is the case. "Can crabs feel pain?" You settle these with evidence.
    Moral claimsWhat matters, what's right or wrong. "Does a crab's pain count?" You settle these with arguments.
    Personal instructionsWhat one person should do in their own life. "You should stop eating X."

    Science alone can't hand you a moral claim. Knowing that a crab feels pain doesn't, by itself, tell you whether its pain matters. Philosophers call this the is-ought gap, after the Scottish philosopher David Hume, who pointed out that arguments often slide from "is" to "ought" without saying how.

    And getting from a moral claim to a personal instruction takes even more steps: facts about someone's health, budget, culture, religion, family, what food they can get, and what difference one person's choice makes. Thoughtful people disagree about every one of those steps. This file sticks to science claims and moral claims. Your plate is your business.

    "Can they suffer?"

    One of the most quoted sentences in animal ethics is hiding in a footnote.

    You met Jeremy Bentham (1748 to 1832) back in Challenge 1 (Case 3) and Challenge 2 (Case 4): the English reformer who wanted to do math with pleasure and pain. In his 1789 book An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, near the end, he added a footnote about animals.

    In it, he points out that the French had already recognized that skin color is no reason to leave a human being at the mercy of someone who would hurt them. Maybe, he suggests, people will one day see that the number of legs or having fur is an equally bad reason. And what about intelligence? A grown horse or dog, he notes, is more rational and easier to communicate with than a baby a day, a week, or even a month old. Then comes the line:

    Exhibit B: the footnote

    "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

    The ability Bentham is pointing at has a name: sentience. A being is sentient if it can have experiences that feel like something to it, good or bad: pain, fear, comfort, pleasure. Sentience is not the same as intelligence, language or being able to reason. A being could be sentient and not very clever at all.

    Exhibit C: the two candidates Sentience view: a being has moral status if it can feel. (Bentham's bet.) Rationality view: a being has moral status if it can reason and choose by principles. (Kant's bet; he arrives in a few screens.)

    On the sentience view, the question "does this animal matter?" partly turns into a science question: can it feel? Which raises a problem...

    How would you know if a crab feels pain?

    You've never felt anyone's pain but your own. Not even your best friend's.

    Philosophers call this the problem of other minds. You can't climb inside someone else's head; you infer that they feel things from how they act and how they're built. Scientists studying animals do the same thing, carefully. They look for markers. Try one first:

    A hermit crab gets a mild shock inside its shell. It's more likely to move out of a poor shell than a really good one. Reflex, or weighing? (Not graded; your first tap is final.)

    Here are the markers scientists use:

    Damage detectors. Nerve cells that respond to injury. Needed, but not enough: your hand jerks off a hot pan before you feel it. A reflex isn't a feeling.
    More than a reflex. Guarding a wound, learning to avoid a place where it got hurt.
    Trade-offs. Paying a cost to avoid harm. Hermit crabs given a mild shock in their shell sometimes leave it, but they're less likely to leave a really good one, and it takes a stronger shock to make them. That looks like weighing, not just flinching.
    Drugs that change it. Painkillers or anti-anxiety drugs changing the behavior, the way they would in us.

    More markers make a stronger case. None of them is proof. In recent years, groups of scientists have summed up where the evidence points:

    • 2012The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness. At a conference in Cambridge, England, scientists declared that humans aren't unique in having the brain machinery for consciousness. They named all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses.
    • 2021A review for the UK government, led by philosopher Jonathan Birch at the London School of Economics, looked at more than 300 studies. It concluded that octopuses and their relatives, and crabs, lobsters and their relatives, should be regarded as sentient. The UK government then added them to a new law, the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, which formally recognizes them as sentient and sets up a committee to check how government policies affect sentient animals. (It didn't extend every older animal-welfare protection to them.)
    • 2024The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness. Signed at first by 39 researchers. It says there's strong scientific support for consciousness in mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility of it in all other vertebrates (fish, reptiles, amphibians) and many invertebrates, including at least octopuses and squid, crabs and lobsters, and insects. Its examples include bumblebees that roll little balls with no obvious reward, as if playing, and crayfish whose anxiety-like behavior changes with anti-anxiety drugs.

    "Realistic possibility" doesn't mean "proven". A declaration isn't a vote that makes something true; it's experts summarizing the evidence.

    Exhibit C2: spot the "ought"

    The New York Declaration's third statement goes further. It says that when there's a realistic possibility an animal is conscious, it's irresponsible to ignore that possibility in decisions that affect it. Notice the switch: the first two statements are science claims about evidence. The third is a moral claim about what we should do. Same document, two kinds of claim. The is-ought gap from screen 2, live.

    Speciesism: a word with a punch

    The word "speciesism" was invented in 1970, on a pamphlet a psychologist printed himself.

    The British psychologist Richard Ryder coined speciesism in 1970. The Australian philosopher Peter Singer, the same Singer behind the drowning-child pond in this challenge's needy-strangers case (Case 2), made it famous in his 1975 book Animal Liberation, crediting Ryder for the word.

    Speciesism means favoring the interests of your own species just because they're your species. Singer's provocative comparison: racism and sexism pick out a feature that doesn't matter morally (skin color, sex) and use it to decide whose interests count. Speciesism, he argues, does the same with species.

    Exhibit D: equal consideration of interests

    Singer's core principle: equal interests deserve equal weight, no matter whose interests they are. If a pig and a child would suffer equally, the two sufferings count equally.

    Notice what this does not say. Equal consideration isn't equal treatment. A pig can't vote, so it makes no sense to talk about its right to vote. Different beings have different interests. Singer also doesn't claim every life is the same: a being with plans and hopes for its future may have more to lose by dying. The principle is only about weighing like with like.

    Singer is a utilitarian: he weighs everyone's interests and asks what gives the best overall result. In his book he applied the principle to how animals are used in farming and research, and argued that a lot of common practice causes suffering that the benefits don't justify. We're not going into the details of those practices here, and you don't have to agree with him. The point for now is the shape of his argument.

    Kant: reason is the ticket

    Kant thought cruelty to animals was wrong. His reason surprises almost everyone.

    The German philosopher Immanuel Kant (1724 to 1804) built his ethics around rational beings: beings who can step back from their urges, ask "what principle am I acting on?", and choose. That ability, he thought, gives persons a special worth he called dignity. So you must never treat a person merely as a tool for your own goals.

    Animals, in Kant's view, don't reason in that sense. So we have no duties to them, only duties concerning them: indirect concern, like the neighbor's car. Then why not be cruel to animals? Kant's answer, in his Metaphysics of Morals (1797) and in lectures his students wrote down: cruelty to animals dulls our natural sympathy for suffering, and that makes us likelier to treat people badly. Kindness to animals is practice for kindness to humans.

    Exhibit E: the objection everyone raises

    Kant puts the wrong in the wrong place. If someone hurts a dog for fun, the problem seems to be what happened to the dog, not what happened to the person's character. And Kant's view has a strange implication: if being cruel to an animal somehow left your character untouched, there'd be nothing wrong with it at all.

    Modern Kantians have pushed back from inside the tradition. The philosopher Christine Korsgaard, in Fellow Creatures (2018), argues that Kant's own ideas, rethought, give us duties to animals themselves, because animals, like us, are beings for whom things can go well or badly.

    Exhibit F: the two tests, side by side Sentience test: Can it feel? If yes, it counts directly. Rationality test (as Kant understood it): Can it reason about principles and choose by them? If yes, it counts directly. If no, concern is only indirect.

    Rights: not up for trade

    Tom Regan thought Singer and Kant were both wrong, for opposite reasons.

    The American philosopher Tom Regan (1938 to 2017) published The Case for Animal Rights in 1983. His complaint about Singer: utilitarianism weighs everything. In principle, anyone's suffering could be outweighed if it brought enough benefit to others. (You saw this worry in Case 1 of this challenge, about people.) His complaint about Kant: tying moral status to reason leaves out beings who obviously matter, human babies included.

    Regan's alternative: what matters is being a subject-of-a-life. A subject-of-a-life has beliefs and desires, memory, a sense of its own future, an emotional life, and a welfare: its life can go better or worse for it, whatever use it is to anyone else. Such beings have inherent value, and with it rights. Above all, the right not to be treated merely as a means, even for good results. That's Kant's "never merely as a means", stretched past reason. Regan was confident that at least normal mammals about a year old or older qualify.

    Singer (utilitarian)Count every sentient being's interests equally, then weigh for the best outcome.
    Kant (classic)Only rational beings count directly. Cruelty to animals is wrong because of what it does to us.
    Regan (rights)Subjects-of-a-life have rights. Rights aren't traded away for good results.

    Could a big enough benefit to others ever justify overriding someone's right? (Not graded; your first tap is final.)

    Having rights doesn't make every loss the same size, though. Regan argued that death harms a being by closing off its future, and a normal adult human usually has more possibilities to lose than a dog. So when someone must be harmed and everyone involved has the same rights, his rights view says to spare the one who would lose more.

    The other side: critics ask what Regan's view says about beings that may not be subjects-of-a-life, like insects or very young animals. And Peter Singer argued that Regan's "spare the one who loses more" reasoning sits uneasily with Regan's flat opposition to all harmful research on animals. (Regan replied that lab animals, unlike the dog in the lifeboat, are forced into the risk.)

    The overlap argument (handled with care)

    This argument only works because everyone agrees on one thing: every human being matters fully.

    Suppose someone says moral status comes from some ability, call it T: being able to reason, or to use language. Here's the problem. Some human beings don't have T, or don't have it yet, or don't have it any more: newborn babies, people with severe cognitive disabilities, people with advanced dementia. And we are completely sure they matter fully. So T can't be what gives a being moral status.

    Exhibit G: the argument 1. Every human being has full moral status. 2. Some human beings have less of ability T than some animals do. 3. So ability T can't be what grounds moral status. So whatever does ground it (feeling? being a subject-of-a-life?) many animals may share.

    It's traditionally called the "argument from marginal cases". Some philosophers now prefer the name argument from species overlap, because "marginal" can sound as if those humans are on the edge of mattering. That's the opposite of what the argument assumes: premise 1 says they matter fully. Bentham's newborn-versus-dog comparison was an early version.

    Be careful with this one. The argument is never a reason to think any human matters less. Anyone who uses it that way has thrown out its first premise.

    Three common replies. Tap each open; Lead 5 opens once you've read all three.

    Reply 1: kind, not ability

    What counts is the kind of being you are: what's normal for your species.

    The other side says: why should how much I matter depend on other individuals' abilities, not my own?

    Rejoinder: defenders say moral status belongs to kinds of beings, not to test scores, and that's exactly why no human ever falls below it.

    Reply 2: relationships

    We have special bonds and duties to fellow humans, like the ones we have to family.

    The other side says: family bonds explain why we owe some people more, but do they explain whether a being counts at all?

    Rejoinder: defenders say being human is itself a morally important bond we all share, and the overlap argument simply assumes that only individual abilities could matter.

    Reply 3: potential

    Babies will grow into the ability.

    The other side says: not every human has that potential, and they still matter fully.

    Rejoinder: defenders reply that what matters is belonging to a kind whose members normally develop reason, which leads back to Reply 1.

    What the arguments settle, and what they don't

    Common ground. Every view in this file condemns hurting animals for fun. Even classic Kant does, though for a reason most people find too thin.

    Still open. Which beings count directly, how much weight their interests get, and what that means for particular practices. This is a live debate among serious philosophers, not a solved problem.

    The gap. Remember the three kinds of claim. Even if you accept a moral claim like "equal suffering counts equally", turning it into a personal instruction needs more steps: your health, your family, your culture and faith, what you can afford, what's available, which practices really cause harm, and what difference one choice makes. Thoughtful people who agree on the moral claim still end up living very differently.

    In this course your reasoning gets feedback. Your lifestyle never does, and nobody is owed an explanation of your dinner.
