    Are You Using Me?

    A 240-year-old rule about how to treat people, why your best friend has no price tag, what Kant really said at the door, and the exact spot where Kant and the happiness-maximizers stop being friends.

    Tonight is your best friend's surprise party, and you're in on it. At 4 pm she texts you: "Be honest. Is something happening tonight?" Lying keeps the surprise she'll love. What do you text back? Lock in your gut call now; at the end you'll see what Kant would say, and make the call again.

    Thirty-second recap

    Last case, Kant told us the only thing good without any strings attached is a good will, and that morality speaks in categorical commands (do this, full stop) rather than hypothetical ones (do this if you want that). His first test, the Formula of Universal Law: act only on a maxim, your personal rule for what you're doing, that you could will everyone to follow as a universal law. That test gave two kinds of duty: perfect (never, no exceptions) and imperfect (a goal, with leeway). And you predicted Kant's answer at the door; today you'll see his actual argument.

    Today: Kant's second way of stating the same supreme rule. He called his formulas "so many formulae of the very same law." Many people find this one easier to feel in their gut: don't use people.

    The Formula of Humanity

    You use people every single day. Kant is fine with that.

    In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), Kant gave a version of his supreme moral rule that philosophers now call the Formula of Humanity. In our words:

    Exhibit A: the rule

    Always treat humanity, in other people and in yourself, as an end, and never merely as a means.

    • Humanity doesn't mean "the human species." It means rational nature: the ability to think things through, set your own goals, and act for reasons.
    • An end is a goal: something you aim at for its own sake. A means is a tool for getting to a goal. To treat a person as an end is to treat them as someone whose own goals count.
    • Merely is the word everything hangs on. When you buy a coffee, you use the barista as a means to get caffeine, and she uses you as a means to get paid. Nobody's wronged. You'd treat her merely as a means only if you treated her as nothing but a tool.

    Quick tap: you ask a friend for a ride to practice. Are you using her?

    Both answers are half right. You are using her to get somewhere, and that's fine, because she knows what you're asking and can say no.

    Exhibit B: the test

    Remember the lying promise from Case 5? It failed the universal law test because it undermines itself. The new formula condemns it for a different reason. Kant says the lender couldn't possibly agree to how he's being treated. Later Kantians such as Onora O'Neill and Christine Korsgaard turned this into a general test: could the other person agree to the way you're treating them, if they knew exactly what was going on? If your plan only works because they don't know, or because they have no real choice, you're using them merely as a means.

    So there are two classic ways to use someone merely as a means: deception (tricking them, so they can't agree to what's really happening) and coercion (forcing them, so their agreement doesn't matter). Both go around a person's reasoning instead of through it.

    The rule has a positive side too. Not using people is the minimum. Treating them fully as ends also means sometimes helping with their goals: an imperfect duty, with leeway about when and how.

    Dignity vs price

    Kant's claim: you are not worth a lot. You're worth something that can't be counted.

    Why should humanity get this special treatment? Kant's answer comes in one of his most quoted passages. Everything, he says, has either a price or a dignity.

    • Something has a price if something else could be put in its place as an equivalent. Your phone has a price: lose it, and another phone (or enough money) makes up for it.
    • Something has a dignity if it is above all price: nothing counts as its equivalent.

    Kant splits price into two kinds:

    Market price: worth tied to what people generally need or want. Kant's examples: skill and hard work at a job.
    Fancy price (some translators say "affective price"): worth tied to taste, to what people happen to enjoy. Kant's examples: wit, a lively imagination, humor.
    Dignity: what Kant gives to morality, and to humanity because it is capable of morality. His examples of inner worth: keeping your promises and kindness done on principle.

    Notice the twist. Being hilarious or brilliant at coding is great, but in Kant's scheme those talents have a price: someone else could, in principle, be just as funny or skilled. What has a dignity is the person.

    Quick tap: a stranger moves to town who is exactly as funny as your funniest friend. Is your friend now replaceable?

    Kant's split in one line: her humor, maybe. Her, no.

    That's why using someone merely as a means is so serious for Kant. It treats a being with dignity as if it had a price: as a tool you'd swap for a better tool.

    Dignity isn't the same as a very high price. A diamond the size of a car still has a price. Dignity is off the scale, not at the top of it.

    Autonomy and the kingdom of ends

    Kant's idea of freedom: you're only free when you're the one writing the rules.

    Where does a person's dignity come from? Kant's answer is autonomy, from the Greek for "self" and "law." Autonomy means giving the moral law to yourself: following a rule because your own reason sees it's right, not because someone's pushing you or you happen to crave something. Kant put it plainly: autonomy is the basis of the dignity of human nature, and of every rational nature.

    The opposite is heteronomy ("other-law"): when something outside your reason, like fear, a craving or someone else's pressure, is calling the shots.

    Exhibit C: the kingdom of ends

    Picture a community where every member is an autonomous rule-maker. Each helps write the rules, and each is bound by them. Kant called this ideal the kingdom of ends (a "kingdom" here just means a system of beings joined by shared laws). The moral question becomes: could this rule be passed in a community where everyone is both lawmaker and citizen, and nobody is anybody's tool?

    Quick tap: your group chat must adopt rules every member could sign off on, including the quiet ones. Which rule would you propose?

    Kingdom-of-ends check: "post anyone's DMs" fails, because the person whose DMs get posted couldn't sign off. "Ask first" passes easily. "Admins can delete anything" is arguable: everyone could agree to moderators, as long as the rule also binds the admins.

    Later Kantians add a third way of using someone, alongside deception and coercion: manipulation. Persuasion gives people reasons and lets them decide. Manipulation pulls levers that skip their reasoning: catching them when they're too tired to think, piling on fake pressure, or playing on guilt.

    Persuasion
    Manipulation
    "Here's why this concert would be amazing. Want to come?"
    "Everyone's going. If you don't come, people will think you're weird. Decide now."
    Persuasion goes through their reasoning. Manipulation goes around it.

    Can you use yourself?

    Kant's rule has three words most people skip: "in your own person."

    The Formula of Humanity covers you too. So Kant believed in duties to oneself, and thought that without them there could be no duties at all. Remember perfect and imperfect duties from Case 5? Kant uses the same split here (in The Metaphysics of Morals, 1797).

    Exhibit D: three duties to yourself

    Don't be servile (perfect). Servility means treating yourself as a doormat: groveling, letting others trample your rights, giving up your own goals just to be liked. Kant's advice, roughly: be nobody's lackey.

    Don't lie (perfect). Kant called lying the greatest violation of a person's duty to themselves as a moral being: a liar throws away their own dignity.

    Develop your talents (imperfect): the rusting-talents case from Case 5, now seen as a duty to yourself. You choose which talents and how far.

    Quick tap: can you really wrong yourself? If you agree to it, isn't that consent?

    Kant's answer: your rational nature has dignity, and it isn't yours to throw away, even by your own choice. Plenty of philosophers don't buy that. Bring your side to Rocky.

    Back to the door: Kant's actual argument

    A French writer handed Kant one of the hardest cases in ethics, and Kant didn't blink.

    Last case you predicted Kant's verdict on the question at the door. Here's how the fight really went. The Swiss-born French political writer Benjamin Constant (1767 to 1830) attacked "a German philosopher" for saying you mustn't lie even to a would-be murderer asking whether your friend is hiding in your house. Kant admitted he'd said it somewhere, though he couldn't remember where, and in 1797, in his seventies, he replied with On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy. In our words:

    Exhibit E: Kant's reply 1. Being truthful in statements you can't avoid making is a duty you owe to everyone, however great the cost to you or anyone else. 2. A lie wrongs humanity in general, not just the person lied to, because it weakens trust in what people declare, which every agreement depends on. 3. Suppose you lie ("he's not here"), but your friend has already slipped out, and the person at the door meets him outside. Then you could fairly be blamed. If you told the truth, maybe the neighbors stop the attacker. Either way, Kant says, if you told the truth, no one can justly hold you responsible for what follows. So: no right to lie, even here.

    Notice the framing. Kant sets the case up as one where you can't avoid answering yes or no, and he never says you must help the attacker. His duty covers statements, so by his own definition, silence or a truthful dodge isn't a lie, though the 1797 essay never considers that way out. He had also been softer earlier: in his ethics lectures (as his students recorded them), he allowed a lie in self-defense when you're being forced and silence won't save you.

    Most commentators reject Kant's answer here; many Kantians think he went too far, though some defend a narrower reading of what he meant.

    Case 5 met Korsgaard's repair: the universal law test may allow lying to someone who is deceiving you. Her twist matters for today's formula: the Formula of Humanity seems to forbid the lie, because lying treats someone's reason as a tool.

    Korsgaard's way out: two levels of rules

    Her solution is a two-level view. The Formula of Humanity is the ideal we live by in ordinary life. When someone is out to do evil, you may fall short of that ideal, but never below the floor set by the universal law test, a bit like the rules for self-defense or war.

    The other side says: once you allow "special rules for bad people," who decides who counts as bad? Kant's strictness was meant to stop exactly that slide.

    Kant vs the utilitarians

    Two great theories agree on most of everyday life, then collide head-on.

    Back in Case 4 (Bentham) you met utilitarianism: the right act is the one that produces the most total happiness (well-being) for everyone affected. A slogan Mill credited to Bentham: everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one. Then you add it all up.

    Utilitarian
    Kant
    What matters: results. Which act makes things go best overall?
    What matters: how you treat people. Does your maxim respect everyone as a reasoner?
    People: each one's happiness counts equally, but one person's loss can be outweighed by bigger gains for others.
    People: beings with dignity. No pile of gains for others is an "equivalent" that makes using someone OK.
    Lying: allowed, even required, when it truly makes things go better (and because trust is so valuable, that's rarer than it sounds).
    Lying: never.
    Where they agree: in ordinary life, both say don't cheat, bully or deceive people, and help others when you can. The fight starts when using one person would make things better overall.

    Open both sides before you predict:

    The Kantian complaint: you can't pay someone off with someone else's happiness

    John Rawls (1971) said utilitarianism doesn't take seriously the distinction between persons. Adding everyone's happiness into one big account treats separate people like one big person, where a loss here can be "paid for" by a gain there.

    The utilitarian comeback: what's so respectful about making everyone worse off?

    "Rules are nice, but if your rule makes everyone worse off, including the people you're 'respecting', what exactly is so respectful about it?" Think of the knock at the door.

    Predicting verdicts: a utilitarian asks "what produces the most happiness, counting everyone?" Kant asks "does this treat anyone merely as a means, or break a perfect duty, like the duty not to lie?"
