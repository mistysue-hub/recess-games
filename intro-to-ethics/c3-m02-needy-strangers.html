    The Drowning Child

    A 25-year-old philosopher, a muddy set of clothes, and an argument so simple you'll agree with every step, and then find out where it was taking you.

    Picture this. Somewhere far away, a child will die of an illness that cheap treatment could prevent, unless someone pays for it. You could chip in without any real hardship. If you don't, is that as wrong as walking past a child drowning in a shallow pond right in front of you? Gut call first, one sentence of reason. You'll make the call again at the end.

    Thirty-second toolkit

    Last case was mostly about what you must not do to people. This one flips it: what, if anything, must you do for strangers you'll never meet? You'll need the argument toolkit from Challenge 1:

    Exhibit A: the toolkit

    Premises are the claims an argument starts from. The conclusion is the claim they're meant to support.

    An argument is valid when the conclusion has to be true if all the premises are true. It's sound when it's valid and all its premises really are true.

    The rule: if an argument is valid and you reject its conclusion, you have to reject at least one premise. So you owe an answer to "which one?"

    A principle is a general rule ("whenever X, you ought to Y"). A case is one particular situation.

    This whole file is one valid argument. Your job isn't to agree with it. It's to find exactly where you'd push back, if anywhere, and whether you can defend that spot.

    The pond and the principle

    One of the most famous essays about helping strangers was written by a 25-year-old, about a crisis in that month's news.

    In November 1971 the Australian philosopher Peter Singer wrote about East Bengal (now Bangladesh). Poverty, a cyclone and a civil war had made at least nine million people into destitute refugees, and many were dying from lack of food, shelter and medical care. His essay, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," came out in Philosophy & Public Affairs in 1972. More than fifty years on, it's one of the most widely discussed essays in applied ethics.

    The essay starts with a case. You're walking past a shallow pond and see a small child drowning. You can wade in and pull the child out, but your clothes will get muddy.

    Quick: would you wade in? (Not graded.)

    Practically everyone says yes instantly, and would be horrified by anyone who said "but these are new jeans." Singer's principle spells out why. In our words:

    Exhibit B: Singer's principle (our wording)

    If it's in your power to prevent something bad from happening, without giving up anything of comparable moral importance, you ought, morally, to do it.

    • "Prevent," not "cause." It's about harm you could stop, not harm you do. Last case, Foot said duties to help are weaker than duties not to harm. Singer's principle is a duty to help, and he argues it's far stronger than most people assume.
    • "Comparable moral importance" means something that matters morally about as much as the bad thing you'd prevent: for example, losing your own life, or doing something wrong in itself. Muddy clothes don't come close.
    • Two strengths. Singer also offered a milder version: if you can prevent something very bad without giving up anything morally significant, you ought to. Keep both in your pocket.

    Notice the move. Nobody needs persuading about the pond. The argument borrows the certainty you already have and tries to carry it somewhere else.

    The argument, numbered

    Here's where the pond takes you, laid out the Challenge 1 way (our wording, in Singer's spirit):

    Exhibit C: the drowning-child argument 1. Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad. 2. If you can prevent something bad from happening without giving up anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it. 3. Many people in rich countries could prevent some of that suffering and death, by giving money to aid organizations, without giving up anything of comparable moral importance. So, many people in rich countries ought to give money to prevent it. And the pond? It isn't a numbered line. It's support for premise 2: a case so clear it makes the principle hard to deny.

    If 1, 2 and 3 are all true, the conclusion has to be true. It's valid.

    First instinct: if you had to attack one line, which? (Not graded. You'll get a proper go in Lead 1.)

    Each premise is a different kind of claim, and you attack each kind differently:

    • Premise 1 is a value claim: it says something is bad. Hardly anyone rejects it.
    • Premise 2 is the principle, a general moral rule. Most of the philosophical fighting happens here.
    • Premise 3 is a factual claim about what's possible, what works and what it costs. You test it with evidence.

    And the conclusion is stronger than it looks. Singer didn't say giving is nice. He said not giving, when you could, is wrong.

    "But that's different!"

    Almost everyone's first reply to Singer is "the pond is different." The hard part is saying how, and why that matters.

    Of course the cases differ. You need a difference that's morally relevant: one that changes what you ought to do. The test: change only that one thing, keep everything else the same, and see whether the right answer changes. Singer took on the two differences people raise most. Tap each open.

    Difference 1: distance. The child is 10,000 miles away.

    Singer: it makes no moral difference whether the person you can help is a neighbor's child a few yards away or someone whose name you'll never know on the other side of the world. Distance might change how sure you are that you can help. But if you're equally able to help, being far away doesn't make a child matter less. And with fast communication and transport, distance no longer stops us helping the way it once did.

    The other side says: we have special obligations to people close to us, like family and neighbors, that we don't have to strangers. (The next case takes this on.)

    Difference 2: many others could help, and most don't.

    Singer: picture the pond with a crowd of adults on the bank, all just standing there. Are you any less obliged to wade in? Ask it, and the idea that numbers shrink your duty looks absurd. And "if everyone gave a little there'd be enough, so I only owe my share"? True, if everyone gave. They don't. The duty is about what you can prevent in the world as it is.

    The other side says: it seems unfair that the few who give must make up for everyone who doesn't. (The next case takes this on.)

    Not every reply is a fight about the principle. Some are really about the facts: "I can't be sure my money gets there." Those attack premise 3, and facts can be checked. And some "differences" aren't differences at all, once you look closely.

    Why the pond feels so different, even if it isn't morally different, is a psychology story. That's the next case.

    Why the argument is startling

    By November 1971 Britain had given about £14.75 million for the East Bengal crisis. Singer pointed out that its share of the Concorde jet project was already over £275 million, and expected to reach £440 million: about thirty times as much.

    So far the argument seems harmless. But premise 3 is about price. If saving a faraway life cost millions, premise 3 would be false for most people.

    Guess: roughly what does it cost to save one life through the health programs that charity evaluators rate highest? (Not graded. One tap: your first guess is the one that counts.)

    Exhibit D: the price tag APPROXIMATION

    Nobody can give an exact figure. One well-known charity evaluator, GiveWell, wrote in 2024 that for the grants it makes to its top-rated charities, it had "typically estimated" an average cost of about US$3,000 to $5,500 per life saved, and that it expects that cost to rise over time.

    A rough range, not a fact: one organization's estimate, for particular programs, from one year. Others differ, critics dispute the method, and it moves. Named so you can check it, not as a recommendation.

    Far more than muddy jeans, nowhere near millions. If the estimate is roughly right, the cost is within reach for many households in rich countries without real hardship. So premise 3 looks true for a lot of people. That's the startling part.

    Singer drew three consequences, in our words:

    • The line between duty and charity moves. A duty is something it's wrong not to do. Charity, as most people use the word, is good but optional; philosophers call that supererogatory ("beyond the call of duty"). Singer said giving to famine relief isn't supererogatory: not giving is wrong.
    • The strong version asks a lot. At full strength, the principle says to keep giving until giving more would cost you (or people who depend on you) about as much as it would relieve. Singer called that point "the level of marginal utility," borrowing a term from economics. In plain words: until you're nearly as badly off as the people you're helping.
    • Even the mild version asks a lot. It still rules out a lot of spending on things like clothes bought to look good rather than to meet any real need.

    Singer knew this is far more than most people give. He took that as a criticism of our ordinary standards, not of his argument. The next case asks whether he's right.

    Pond variations: let the principle vote

    Philosophers have built dozens of pond variations to stress-test one principle.

    Peter Unger's 1996 book Living High and Letting Die is full of them, and Singer himself later swapped the muddy clothes for a brand-new pair of shoes in his 2009 book The Life You Can Save. The method: change one feature, then ask two separate questions.

    1. What does the principle say? Just apply the rule. Is something bad at stake? Is it in your power to prevent it? Would preventing it cost anything of comparable moral importance? Yes, yes, no means the principle says you must.
    2. What do you say? Your considered judgment about the case.

    When the two match, the principle gets more believable. When they clash, something has to give: the principle, or your judgment about the case. Going back and forth like this, revising whichever seems less believable until principles and cases fit together, is called reflective equilibrium. You met it in Challenge 2, Case 3.

    SSealed file: who named it (short on time? skip this one)

    John Rawls gave the method its name in A Theory of Justice (1971). You never get to stop thinking, but you get more and more coherent. Singer is betting that, on reflection, you'll keep the principle and revise your comfort about faraway children. His critics bet the other way.
