# Cold Case Academy — Design for M02–M05 (DRAFT for review)

Two real unsolved cases, two modules each. Part 1 rebuilds **what happened**. Part 2 weighs **what became of them** and ends with the learner's own verdict.

Every module keeps the M01 flow: **hook vote → case-file reading (manila folder look) → 75% too-fast check → look back at vote → 2 talk-back questions → activity (Twine game, with the debate/interest activity as backup) → complete.**

Readings: original case files, about 1,200–1,300 words each (about 8–9 minutes; too-fast check under 7 minutes). No gore. Nobody is shown getting hurt.

**The big change from M01:** these cases have no answer. So every game ends on **"your verdict + how sure are you?"** and scores how well the learner used evidence, never whether they "solved" it. "We don't know, and here's why" is always a full-credit ending.

## M02 — Case 001, Part 1: The Man in the Last Row
**Case:** D.B. Cooper skyjacking, Northwest Orient Flight 305, Thanksgiving Eve, November 24, 1971
**File:** `modules/M02-Case-the-man-in-the-last-row.md` · reading `resources/M02Case.md`
**Skill:** Building a timeline from witness statements

### Reading beats
1. **Portland airport, 2:00 p.m.** A quiet man in a dark suit and clip-on tie pays cash for a one-way ticket to Seattle, a 30-minute flight, under the name "Dan Cooper." He takes a seat in the last row, orders a bourbon and 7-Up, and lights a cigarette (normal on planes in 1971).
2. **The note.** After takeoff he hands flight attendant Florence Schaffner a note. She thinks it's a lonely businessman's phone number and drops it unopened into her purse. He leans over: she'd better look at it. He has a bomb.
3. **The briefcase.** He opens it just enough: wires, red sticks, a battery. His demands: $200,000 in $20 bills, four parachutes (two main, two reserve), and fuel trucks waiting in Seattle.
4. **Circling Seattle.** The plane circles Seattle for more than two hours while the FBI gathers the money. Before handing it over, the FBI photographs all 10,000 bills on microfilm, recording every serial number (this matters in Part 2). Most passengers never know anything is wrong.
5. **The swap.** In Seattle the passengers walk off in exchange for the money and parachutes. Flight attendant Tina Mucklow stays near him; he's calm and polite, and even asks for meals for the flight crew.
6. **Into the night.** He orders the plane toward Mexico, flying low and slow, with the back stairs ready to open. At about 8:13 p.m., on a dark, stormy night with sleet and freezing temperatures at that height, over the forests of southwest Washington, the crew feels a bump in the tail. When they land in Reno, he's gone. So are the money, one main parachute and one reserve.
7. **What he left behind.** His black clip-on tie with a mother-of-pearl tie clip, eight cigarette butts, one unused main parachute, and a reserve parachute with some of its cords cut. (Part 2 picks up the tie.)
8. **Detective's notes:** witnesses remembered him differently (age, height, hair); the name "D.B." came from a reporter's mistake; the time of the bump is the most important clue for where to search.

### Hook vote
> Quick vote before you open the file. A man hijacks a plane, collects a pile of money, and then does something no hijacker had ever done in the U.S. What do you think it was?
> A) He jumped out of the plane with a parachute
> B) He hid on the plane and walked off with the crew
> C) He gave all the money back

(Answer: A.)

### Talk-back
1. Why did the FBI write down the serial number of every single bill? *(Good answer: so if any of the money was ever spent or found, they could prove it was Cooper's.)*
2. Why does it matter so much exactly when the crew felt the bump? *(Good answer: the plane's speed and route at that moment tell searchers where he probably landed.)*

### Activity 1: Twine game — "Flight 305"
- **You are:** an FBI agent in Seattle, the morning after.
- **Mechanic:** interview witnesses (2 questions each) and pin their statements onto a **timeline board** from 2:00 p.m. to landing in Reno. Witnesses disagree on details. The learner decides which details to trust (two witnesses agreeing beats one).
- **Witnesses:** ticket agent, Florence Schaffner, Tina Mucklow, the co-pilot, a passenger who noticed nothing.
- **Deductions (combine cards):** "He planned this" (four parachutes + low, slow flight path); "He knew planes" (back stairs + flight orders); "Witnesses disagree, so the sketch is uncertain" (two descriptions).
- **Finale:** draw the **search zone**. Pick where to send searchers based on the bump time, speed and wind. Score: how close the zone is to the FBI's real search area, and whether the timeline uses agreed-upon facts.
- **Leads:** 6. Revisits free.

### Activity 2 (backup)
- **Debate:** "Should D.B. Cooper be called a 'folk hero'? Some people wrote songs about him."
  - Hero side: nobody was hurt; he was polite; he took on an airline, not people.
  - Not-a-hero side: he threatened a whole plane with a bomb; the crew was terrified; he committed a serious crime.
- **Interest:** "Think of a story in {interest} where witnesses remember the same event differently. How would you decide who's right?"

## M03 — Case 001, Part 2: Money on the Riverbank
**Case:** D.B. Cooper, the 45-year search (1971–2016)
**File:** `modules/M03-Case-money-on-the-riverbank.md` · reading `resources/M03Case.md`
**Skill:** Weighing evidence for competing theories

### Reading beats
1. **The search.** Soldiers, police and volunteers comb the forests below the flight path. They find nothing. The FBI looks at more than 800 suspects over the years.
2. **February 10, 1980.** An eight-year-old named Brian Ingram is helping his family dig a fire pit on a sandy beach called Tena Bar on the Columbia River. He uncovers bundles of rotting $20 bills, still held with rubber bands: about $5,800.
3. **The match.** The FBI checks the serial numbers against their list from 1971. It's Cooper's money. It's the only ransom money ever found.
4. **The riddle.** How did it get there? Washed down a river? Buried by someone? Carried by a dredging boat that dumped sand on the beach years earlier? Experts still argue, and none of the explanations fits perfectly.
5. **The tie.** In 2017, a team of volunteer scientists examine Cooper's clip-on tie and find tiny metal particles, including rare ones used in special factories. Maybe he worked in aircraft or chemical plants. Or maybe the tie was secondhand.
6. **2016.** After 45 years the FBI stops actively working the case. It says it will still look at real physical evidence, like the parachutes or the money.
7. **The two big theories.**
   - *He didn't survive:* freezing rain at night, no proper jumpsuit, dark forest, a parachute he may not have known well, and most of the money never spent.
   - *He survived:* he planned carefully, chose the slow route and low height, and some money turned up far from where a body would be.
8. **Detective's notes:** finding the money proved part of the story, but raised new questions; "we don't know" can be the most honest answer.

### Hook vote
> Quick vote. Nine years after Cooper jumped, a kid digging on a beach found some of his money. How much of the $200,000 do you think was there?
> A) Almost all of it
> B) About $5,800
> C) Just one $20 bill

(Answer: B.)

### Talk-back
1. How did the FBI know the beach money was really Cooper's? *(Good answer: the serial numbers matched their list from 1971.)*
2. Give one clue that suggests Cooper did NOT survive the jump. *(Good answer: any of freezing rain, night, forest, no proper gear, most money never spent.)*

### Activity 1: Twine game — "The Tena Bar Riddle"
- **You are:** an FBI agent in 1980, called to the beach.
- **Mechanic:** collect evidence at the beach, the FBI lab, a river expert, the dredging company, a skydiving instructor and the old search maps. Each card is tagged **supports "survived"**, **supports "didn't survive"**, or **doesn't decide either way** (hidden until the end, like M01).
- **Deductions:** "Money traveled by water" (river expert + bundles still banded); "Bad night to jump" (weather + gear); "Not a pro skydiver" (instructor + parachute choice).
- **Finale:** choose **Survived / Didn't survive / Can't tell yet**, then a confidence slider (Hunch / Fairly sure / Very sure), then pin 3 cards.
- **Score:** the evidence you pinned supports your verdict, your confidence matches how strong the evidence is (overconfidence loses a star), and you considered at least one card from the other side.
- **Leads:** 6. Revisits free.

### Activity 2 (backup)
- **Debate:** "Should the FBI have stopped working the Cooper case in 2016?"
  - Yes: 45 years, hundreds of suspects, agents are needed for current crimes.
  - No: new science (like the tie particles) might crack it; a crime shouldn't go unsolved because it's old.
- **Interest:** "Think of something in {interest} where one surprising find changed what everyone believed. What should people do when a clue raises more questions than it answers?"

## M04 — Case 002, Part 1: Spoons, Soap and Fake Heads
**Case:** The Alcatraz escape, night of June 11, 1962
**File:** `modules/M04-Case-spoons-soap-and-fake-heads.md` · reading `resources/M04Case.md`
**Skill:** Reconstructing a sequence of events from physical evidence

### Reading beats
1. **"The Rock."** A prison on an island in cold, fast-moving San Francisco Bay, built so no one could escape. Guards counted prisoners many times a day.
2. **Four men, one plan.** Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin, serving time for robbery, plus a fourth man, Allen West, serving time for car theft. Morris is known as the planner.
3. **Months of quiet work.** Behind each cell's sink is a small air vent. Using spoons from the dining hall, old saw blades, and a drill made from a vacuum-cleaner motor, they slowly widen the holes. Morris plays an accordion during music hour to cover the noise. They hide the holes with painted cardboard.
4. **The workshop on the roof.** Behind the cells is a tall utility corridor. They climb it to the top of the cellblock and build a secret workshop.
5. **The raft.** Following ideas from a magazine, they stitch and glue more than 50 stolen raincoats into life vests and a raft, sealing the seams with plastic glue from the prison shops and the heat of the steam pipes.
6. **The heads.** Each man makes a dummy head from soap, toilet paper, concrete dust, paint and real hair from the prison barbershop, to leave in bed.
7. **June 11.** Lights out. Three men slip through their vents, climb to the roof, carry the raft across, slide down a pipe, and reach the water. Allen West had used cement to hold his vent cover in place, and it hardened, jamming the cover. By the time he breaks through, the others are gone.
8. **7:00 a.m.** A guard reaches into Clarence Anglin's cell to wake him, and the dummy head falls onto the floor.
9. **Detective's notes:** the evidence in the cells tells the story step by step; Allen West is the only witness, and he only knows the plan, not the ending.

### Hook vote
> Quick vote. In 1962, three prisoners fooled the guards at Alcatraz for an entire night. What did they leave in their beds?
> A) Pillows stuffed under the blankets
> B) Fake heads with real human hair
> C) Other prisoners who swapped cells

(Answer: B.)

### Talk-back
1. Why did Morris play the accordion during music hour? *(Good answer: to cover the sound of digging through the vents.)*
2. Why didn't Allen West escape with the others? *(Good answer: cement he'd used on his vent cover hardened and jammed it, and by the time he got through, the others had left.)*

### Activity 1: Twine game — "Count at Dawn"
- **You are:** a junior officer at Alcatraz, 7:00 a.m., June 12. The alarm is sounding.
- **Mechanic:** search the cells, the utility corridor, the cellblock roof, the shoreline and the prison workshop. Each find is an **evidence card**. Interview Allen West (2 questions; he's nervous and only tells what he knows).
- **Puzzle:** put the escape in order on a **route board** (vent → corridor → roof workshop → roof vent → pipe down the wall → fences → water). A wrong order shows where the logic breaks ("They couldn't reach the roof before widening the vent").
- **Deductions:** "Months of planning" (dust in the vent + cardboard cover); "They had help from the magazine" (raincoat seams + Popular Mechanics page); "The heads bought them hours" (dummy head + bed check times).
- **Finale:** report to the warden: how they got out, and how many hours' head start they had. Scored on a correct route and a correct head-start estimate.
- **Leads:** 6. Revisits free.

### Activity 2 (backup)
- **Debate:** "Was Alcatraz's design to blame for the escape, or the guards?"
  - Design: old crumbling concrete, salty air rusting metal, a hidden corridor behind every cell.
  - Guards: the heads fooled them all night, noise during music hour went unnoticed, nobody checked the vents.
- **Interest:** "Think of a big project in {interest} that took months of small, quiet steps. What kept the people going?"

## M05 — Case 002, Part 2: Did They Make It?
**Case:** The Alcatraz escape, the search and the 60-year mystery
**File:** `modules/M05-Case-did-they-make-it.md` · reading `resources/M05Case.md`
**Skill:** Reaching an honest verdict when evidence points both ways

### Reading beats
1. **The search.** Boats, planes and police search the bay for days. Searchers find a paddle near Angel Island, life vests washed up on a beach across the bay and near Alcatraz, and a plastic-wrapped packet holding the names, addresses and photos of Anglin relatives. No bodies are ever found.
2. **The water.** The bay is cold, usually in the 50s °F, with strong tidal currents. Of the 36 men who tried to escape Alcatraz over 29 years, most were caught or drowned. Six months after this escape, another prisoner, John Paul Scott, really did reach the shore, but he was found unconscious and nearly frozen.
3. **1979.** The FBI closes its case: the men probably drowned. The U.S. Marshals keep it open and keep the three men on their wanted list for decades.
4. **Clues that they survived.**
   - Family members say they got unsigned Christmas cards afterward.
   - A family friend later showed a photo he said was taken on a farm in Brazil in 1975, showing two men who look like the Anglin brothers. Experts disagree, and the Marshals were never convinced.
   - In 2013 a letter arrived at a San Francisco police station, claiming to be from John Anglin, saying all three made it out and that the other two had since died. The FBI tested it and couldn't confirm it either way.
   - In 2003, the TV show *MythBusters* built a raincoat raft and made it to shore. In 2014, scientists at Delft University in the Netherlands modeled the bay's tides and found that if the men launched around 11:30 p.m., the current could have carried them to land near the Golden Gate Bridge. Leaving earlier or later would likely have swept them out to sea.
5. **Clues that they didn't.** Cold water, darkness, an untested homemade raft, no confirmed sighting ever, and men who would have needed money and help on the outside.
6. **Today.** *[Verify before writing: the Marshals said they would keep the men on the wanted list until about September 2026. Check what the Marshals have said since, and write the current status here.]*
7. **Detective's notes:** some clues can't be tested (a photo, a letter); "possible" and "proven" aren't the same thing.

### Hook vote
> Quick vote before you read. The three Alcatraz escapers were never found. What do you think happened to them?
> A) They drowned in the bay
> B) They made it and lived in secret
> C) Nobody can know for sure

(Opinion vote: the look-back asks whether the reading changed their mind.)

### Talk-back
1. What did searchers find after the escape? *(Good answer: any of a paddle near Angel Island, life vests, and a packet with Anglin family names, addresses and photos.)*
2. Why couldn't the FBI prove the 2013 letter was real? *(Good answer: tests couldn't confirm who wrote it, and the writer never came forward.)*

### Activity 1: Twine game — "The Marshals' File"
- **You are:** a new U.S. Marshal handed the oldest open file in the office.
- **Mechanic:** review evidence from the 1962 search, the Anglin family, the Brazil photo, the 2013 letter, the tide study and the raft test. Each card is secretly tagged **testable fact**, **untestable claim**, or **expert opinion**.
- **Interviews (2 questions each):** a Coast Guard captain from 1962, an Anglin relative, a photo expert, a tide scientist.
- **Extra evidence card:** John Paul Scott's December 1962 attempt (he reached land but nearly died of cold), which counts as evidence for both sides.
- **Deductions:** "Possible isn't proven" (tide model + raft test); "We can't test this" (photo + letter); "The pouch went somewhere on purpose" (pouch + family addresses).
- **Finale:** **Drowned / Made it / Can't tell**, a confidence level, and 3 pinned cards. Then the Marshals' decision: **keep the file open or close it?**
- **Score:** verdict supported by testable facts (not just claims), confidence matching the evidence, and the open/close choice explained by evidence. Every honest ending gets full credit.
- **Leads:** 7. Revisits free.

### Activity 2 (backup)
- **Debate:** "Should the Marshals ever close a case when the people would be over 90 years old?"
  - Close it: they are almost certainly dead; time and money matter.
  - Keep it open: it warns other fugitives; new evidence might appear; closing feels like giving up.
- **Interest:** "Think of something in {interest} that people still argue about because the proof is missing. What would it take to settle it?"
