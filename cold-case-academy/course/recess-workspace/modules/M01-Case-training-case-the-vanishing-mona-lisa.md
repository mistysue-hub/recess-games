# Training Case: The Vanishing Mona Lisa

**Start here:** (1) Say the hook vote below, word for word. (2) Right away, open the case game, *The Missing Smile*, at https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/the-missing-smile/ and let the learner play it to the "Case closed" screen. (3) Look back at the vote in one line, then ask the two talk-back questions, one at a time. (4) At the very end, after any Activity 2 and the book suggestion, offer the case file, `resources/M01Case.md` as an optional extra; open it only if the learner wants it, and they can read as much or as little of it as they like.

## Summary

The training case. In 1911 the world's most famous painting disappeared from the Louvre in Paris, and for more than a day nobody even noticed it was gone. Police chased master thieves, rival countries, and famous artists for two years. The real answer was much simpler, and it was hiding in plain sight.

No violence in this case.

## Estimated time

Estimated time: 30 minutes

The hook and talk-back add about 5 minutes, and the game takes about 15. A second activity adds about 5–10 more when the activity count is two. A learner can pause at any point. The case file is optional: about 1,280 words, about 9 more minutes if the learner reads it.

## Learning targets

After this module the learner can:

- retell how the Mona Lisa was stolen and how it was finally found
- name at least two wrong theories the police chased
- explain one assumption that slowed the investigation down (for example, expecting a genius thief)

## Tutor guidance

Read AGENTS.md first; it outranks this section and sets the fixed session flow for every module in this course: **hook → game → look back → talk-back (→ Activity 2) → book suggestion → optional case file → complete**.

**Rocky's role in this course:** Rocky is the senior detective at Cold Case Academy, and the learner is a trainee. Keep the detective framing light and fun ("Detective, your first case"). Never be spooky or scary.

**What happens in this case (for Rocky only — never read it out, and never share it before the learner has finished the game):** On Monday, August 21, 1911, while the Louvre was closed, a man in a worker's white smock took the Mona Lisa off the wall, left the frame and glass box in a stairwell, hid the wooden panel under his smock, and got out when a plumber, taking him for a worker, opened a locked door for him. Guards assumed it was with the photographers, so the theft wasn't noticed until the next day, when artist Louis Béroud asked about it. A left-thumb print on the glass couldn't be matched because police records kept only right-hand prints, even though Peruggia had a record. Police chased theories: a rich collector's master thief, Germany, and modern artists; poet Guillaume Apollinaire was jailed for several days and Pablo Picasso questioned, both innocent. The empty wall drew crowds and made the painting world-famous. In late 1913, Vincenzo Peruggia, a former Louvre worker who had fitted the glass boxes over paintings, wrote to Florence art dealer Alfredo Geri; Geri and the Uffizi director confirmed the painting in a trunk with a false bottom, and Peruggia was arrested. Police had visited his room and questioned him earlier, and missed it. He claimed patriotism, served a short sentence, and the painting returned to the Louvre on January 4, 1914. A later "six fakes mastermind" story had no good evidence.

**Content notes (for the tutor and guardian; never read them out):** None. A theft with no violence; one arrest of an innocent man (Apollinaire) is mentioned.

**Hook — present word for word, exactly as below, before anything else:**

> Welcome to Cold Case Academy, Detective. Your first case: in 1911, the world's most famous painting vanished from a museum in Paris. Quick vote before you start. Who do you think stole it?
>
> A) A master thief hired by a millionaire
> B) A famous artist who hated old paintings
> C) A handyman who used to work at the museum

If the learner answers in their own words instead of A, B, or C, record their words as given and go straight to the game. Do not react to the vote either way.

**Look back at the vote — one line, once the learner reaches the "Case closed" screen:** remind them what they voted and say in one sentence whether the case bore it out (the answer is C). If they guessed A or B, point out lightly that they guessed just like the 1911 police did. No lecture.

**Talk-back — at most two questions, one at a time (skip entirely if the tutor turned talk-back off):** Ask them after the game. The learner can answer from what they found in the game; they don't need the case file.

1. Why didn't anyone notice the painting was missing until the next day?
   *(What a good answer mentions: the museum was closed on Monday, and when the artist asked on Tuesday, the guard assumed it had been taken down to be photographed.)*
2. Why do you think the police didn't suspect someone like Vincenzo Peruggia sooner?
   *(What a good answer mentions: they were expecting a brilliant, important criminal, so an ordinary handyman didn't seem like a suspect, even though he had inside knowledge of the museum; they even visited his room and missed the painting.)*

Keep it light and about what happened. Never write a card, header, or label anywhere that answers a talk-back question.

**Activity 1 — the game, *The Missing Smile*:** open https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/the-missing-smile/ (a Twine detective game on an outside webpage; you can't see inside it). Tell the learner once before they start:
- they're a junior inspector in Paris, the day after the theft, with 8 leads to spend;
- a new place costs one lead, and going back to a place they've visited is free;
- interviews allow only two questions, so choose carefully;
- the deduction board (free) joins two clues into a new idea;
- at the end they name a suspect and pin up to 3 cards as proof.

Don't name a suspect, point them to Peruggia's room, or say which cards are assumptions. If they're stuck, you may ask, "Which place haven't you checked yet?" or "Which two clues might fit together?" The game ends on a green "Case closed" stamp. Then ask, one at a time: who they named, which clues convinced them, and their detective rating (stars out of 3). The game's own last screens explain what really happened; don't repeat them, but answer questions about them from the Rocky-only notes above.

**Activity 2 — only when the activity count is two (see AGENTS.md for which one):**

- **Debate (a light chat about the choice):** "Is Vincenzo Peruggia a hero or just a thief?"
  - Side A: Hero (or at least not a villain) — he did it for his country
    - He said he wanted to bring the painting home to Italy, where Leonardo was born.
    - He never tried to sell it to a stranger for a huge fortune; he took it to an Italian museum expert.
    - Many Italians at the time cheered for him.
  - Side B: Just a thief — the reason doesn't change what he did
    - The painting wasn't actually stolen from Italy long ago; Leonardo brought it to France himself, so Peruggia was wrong about the facts.
    - An innocent man (Apollinaire) went to jail partly because the real thief stayed quiet.
    - He kept the painting hidden in a trunk for over two years, where it could have been damaged.
  Run it as the light chat AGENTS.md describes, tied to something the reader cares about. The sides and lines above are only ideas to offer if they're stuck, never your own view. Stay neutral; the learner may pass.
  *(Note for Rocky: the fact that Leonardo brought the painting to France himself is not in the reading; offer it only as a stuck-prompt, and say it's an extra fact.)*
- **Interest:** skill — Spotting the assumption. The event — Everyone pictured a genius thief, so nobody looked twice at the handyman in the white smock. Reframe it in the learner's own interest (from their tutor notes): "Think of a time in {interest} when everyone expected the big star to make the winning move, and someone nobody was watching did it instead. Why did people miss them?" Then bring it back to the case: "Which assumption did the 1911 police make, and what should they have looked at instead?"

**Book suggestion — always offer, never require:** once the activities are done, suggest *The Mona Lisa Vanishes* by Nicholas Day (true story, ages 10–14): it tells the whole theft like a heist story and follows Leonardo painting her. If they love art or museums, you can mention *The Louvre* by Nicole K. Orr instead. Say it's just for fun if they want more; don't assign it or check on it later.

**Case file — optional, at the very end:** keep it closed until now: before the book suggestion, don't open, show, quote or summarize it, even if the learner asks (say it unlocks once the case is closed). After the book suggestion, offer it in one line: it's the real detective file behind the game, with extra details, and they can read it now, later, or skip it. If they want it, open `resources/M01Case.md` whole, as a manila case-file folder (cream/manila background, typewriter-style monospace headings, a red "CASE FILE" stamp near the top), with every word exactly as written. Do not summarise it, condense it, or narrate it from memory. They can read as much or as little as they like and stop whenever they want. Never require it, quiz on it, or check how long they spent reading.

When the activity (or both activities) is finished, the book has been suggested, the case file has been offered, and the learner confirms they're done, the module is complete.
