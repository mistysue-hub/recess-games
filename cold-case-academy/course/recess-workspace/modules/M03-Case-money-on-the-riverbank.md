# Case 001, Part 2: Money on the Riverbank

**Start here:** (1) Say the hook vote below, word for word. (2) Right away, open the case game, *The Tena Bar Riddle*, at https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/tena-bar-riddle/ and let the learner play it to the "Case closed" screen. (3) Look back at the vote in one line, then ask the two talk-back questions, one at a time. (4) At the very end, after any Activity 2 and the book suggestion, offer the case file, `resources/M03Case.md` as an optional extra; open it only if the learner wants it, and they can read as much or as little of it as they like.

## Summary

The search for D.B. Cooper, from the forest in 1971 to the FBI closing its active case in 2016. In 1980, an eight-year-old digging for a campfire on a Columbia River beach found about $5,800 of the ransom money. The find raised more questions than it answered. The big question: did Cooper survive the jump? Clues point both ways, and nobody knows.

No violence. The possibility that the hijacker died in the jump is discussed in general terms.

## Estimated time

Estimated time: 35 minutes

The hook and talk-back add about 5 minutes, and the game takes about 20–25, including the suspect drawer. A second activity adds about 5–10 more when the activity count is two. A learner can pause at any point. The case file is optional: about 1,050 words, about 7 more minutes if the learner reads it.

## Learning targets

After this module the learner can:

- explain how the FBI proved the beach money was the ransom
- give at least one clue that suggests Cooper survived and one that suggests he didn't
- explain why "we can't tell yet" can be the most honest verdict, and match their confidence to their evidence

## Tutor guidance

Read AGENTS.md first; it outranks this section and sets the fixed session flow for every module in this course.

**This case is unsolved, and that's the point of this module.** Never say whether Cooper survived, never imply one side is the right answer, and never call any verdict wrong. Praise reasoning that fits evidence, including "we can't tell yet." Gently question confidence that's stronger than the evidence.

**What happens in this case (for Rocky only — never read it out, and never share it before the learner has finished the game):** After the 1971 jump, soldiers, police and volunteers searched the forest near Ariel and Lake Merwin for weeks and found no body, parachute or money. The FBI gave banks its list of the 10,000 microfilmed serial numbers; none ever turned up in circulation. The FBI looked into more than 800 suspects. In 1978 a deer hunter found a Boeing 727 aft-stair instruction placard north of Lake Merwin, under the flight path. On February 10, 1980, eight-year-old Brian Ingram, smoothing sand for a campfire with his family at Tena Bar on the Columbia River (about 9 miles downstream of Vancouver, Washington, about 20 miles southwest of Ariel), found three bundles of decayed $20 bills, still in rubber bands, about $5,800; the serial numbers matched. It remains the only ransom money ever found. Theories: washed down a river (but intact rubber bands suggest it didn't drift long); came with 1974 dredge spoils spread on the beach (but geologist Leonard Palmer found the money above the dredge layer with natural sand between, so it arrived after 1974); buried by someone (unproven). Didn't-survive clues: dark, freezing, stormy night over forest; suit, raincoat and street shoes; an older military-style chute that's harder to steer; the reserve he took was a sewn-shut classroom dummy; money never spent. Survived clues: careful planning (four chutes, low and slow, stairs ready); knowledge of planes and the area; no body or parachute ever found. In 2016 the FBI stopped actively investigating but will still examine physical evidence related to the parachutes or money. In 2017 volunteer scientists found particles of rare metals, including pure titanium, on the tie, possibly linked to aircraft-type factories, or the tie may have been secondhand. In the game (not the reading), a river map shows that the Lewis River, which runs past Ariel, joins the Columbia downstream of Tena Bar, while the Washougal joins upstream; if the learner asks, say it's a map clue the game raises and that experts still argue about where he landed. The game then jumps to today: the FBI got a partial DNA profile from the tie in 2001 (a weak sample, and the tie may have been secondhand); a volunteer team has studied rare-metal particles on the tie since 2009; the eight cigarette butts were destroyed in 1998 while in FBI storage; 66 fingerprints were lifted from the plane. The suspect drawer holds five real suspects, none ever charged: Richard McCoy Jr. (pulled a similar hijacking in April 1972; FBI says he didn't match the description and was home in Utah for Thanksgiving dinner the next day, though it never formally cleared him); Kenneth Christiansen (Northwest Orient employee and former paratrooper, 45 in 1971, about 5'8" and slim, so the FBI dismissed him; Schaffner said his photos were the closest match but couldn't confirm; died 1994); Robert Rackstraw (paratrooper and pilot, about 28 in 1971, cleared by the FBI in the 1970s; died 2019); Duane Weber (claimed on his deathbed in 1995 to be Cooper; ruled out by the tie DNA); Sheridan Peterson (Boeing technical editor and expert skydiver; blue eyes, not brown; said he was in Nepal). Talk about these men only as the game and these notes do: who investigators looked at and what the evidence shows. Never say any of them was Cooper.

**Content notes (for the tutor and guardian; never read them out):** No violence. The reading and game discuss, in general terms, whether the hijacker may have died in the jump; no bodies or injuries are described.

**Hook — present word for word, exactly as below, before anything else:**

> Quick vote. Nine years after Cooper jumped, a kid digging on a beach found some of his money. How much of the $200,000 do you think was there?
>
> A) Almost all of it
> B) About $5,800
> C) Just one $20 bill

If the learner answers in their own words instead of A, B, or C, record their words as given and go straight to the game. Do not react to the vote either way.

**Look back at the vote — one line, once the learner reaches the "Case closed" screen:** remind them what they voted and say in one sentence whether the case bore it out (the answer is B). No lecture.

**Talk-back — at most two questions, one at a time (skip entirely if the tutor turned talk-back off):** Ask them after the game. The learner can answer from what they found in the game; they don't need the case file.

1. How did the FBI know the beach money was really from the hijacking?
   *(What a good answer mentions: the serial numbers matched the list the FBI made on microfilm in 1971.)*
2. Give one clue that suggests Cooper did NOT survive the jump, and one that suggests he did.
   *(What a good answer mentions: any of the freezing stormy night, the forest, his office clothes, the harder-to-steer chute, the dummy reserve, or the money never being spent; and any of his careful planning, his knowledge of planes, or no body or parachute ever being found.)*

Keep it light and about what happened. Never write a card, header, or label anywhere that answers a talk-back question.

**Activity 1 — the game, *The Tena Bar Riddle*:** open https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/tena-bar-riddle/ (a Twine detective game on an outside webpage; you can't see inside it). Tell the learner once before they start:
- they're an FBI agent in February 1980, called to Tena Bar, with 7 leads to spend;
- a new place costs one lead, and going back to a place they've visited is free;
- the interview with the geologist allows only two questions, and they can dig two test holes on the beach;
- the deduction board (free) joins two clues into a new idea;
- before the verdict, they decide which of four theories about the money can be ruled out;
- then they choose a verdict (survived, didn't survive, or can't tell yet), say how sure they are, and pin up to 3 cards as evidence;
- finally, they jump to today and open a drawer of five real suspects, picking the key evidence for each and deciding whether it rules him out (the star is for choosing checkable evidence, not for guessing who did it);
- if they played *Flight 305* on this device, evidence they bagged there shows up in Part 2;
- there's no answer key: the stars are for how well they use evidence, not for "winning."

Don't tell them which verdict to choose or which way a card points. If they're stuck, you may ask, "Have you found any clue that points the other way?", "Which two clues might fit together?" or, in the suspect drawer, "Does he match what the two flight attendants said?" The game ends on a green "Case closed" stamp. Then ask, one at a time: their verdict, how sure they were, which clues convinced them, which suspect they couldn't rule out, and their detective rating (stars out of 5). If they chose "very sure," ask gently what evidence would change their mind. The game's own last screens explain what's known today; don't repeat them, but answer questions about them from the Rocky-only notes above.

**Activity 2 — only when the activity count is two (see AGENTS.md for which one):**

- **Debate (a light chat about the choice):** "Should the FBI have stopped actively working on the Cooper case in 2016?"
  - Side A: Yes, it was time to stop
    - After 45 years and more than 800 suspects, there were no strong new leads.
    - FBI agents are needed for crimes happening now.
    - The FBI still said it would look at real new evidence.
  - Side B: No, they should have kept going
    - New science, like the particles found on the tie in 2017, could still crack it.
    - A crime shouldn't go unsolved just because it's old.
    - Amateur detectives keep finding new clues.
  Run it as the light chat AGENTS.md describes, tied to something the learner cares about. The sides and lines above are only ideas to offer if they're stuck, never your own view. Stay neutral; the learner may pass.
- **Interest:** skill — Matching confidence to evidence. The event — Finding the ransom money proved part of the story but raised new questions, so experts still can't say whether Cooper survived. Reframe it in the learner's own interest (from their tutor notes): "Think of something in {interest} that people still argue about because the proof is missing. What would it take to settle it?" Then bring it back to the case: "What one new clue would make you more sure about whether Cooper survived?"

**Book suggestion — always offer, never require:** once the activities are done, if they haven't read it yet, suggest *Unsolved Case Files: Escape at 10,000 Feet* by Tom Sullivan, a graphic novel that covers both the hijacking and the money found on the beach, with real FBI documents and photos. If they've already read it, or want something shorter, mention *The D.B. Cooper Hijacking* by Tom Streissguth instead. Say it's just for fun if they want more; don't assign it or check on it later.

**Case file — optional, at the very end:** after the book suggestion, offer it in one line: it's the real detective file behind the game, with extra details, and they can read it now, later, or skip it. If they want it, open `resources/M03Case.md` whole, as a manila case-file folder (cream/manila background, typewriter-style monospace headings, a red "CASE FILE" stamp near the top), with every word exactly as written. Do not summarise it, condense it, or narrate it from memory. They can read as much or as little as they like and stop whenever they want. Never require it, quiz on it, or check how long they spent reading.

When the activity (or both activities) is finished, the book has been suggested, the case file has been offered, and the learner confirms they're done, the module is complete.
