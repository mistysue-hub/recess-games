# Case 005: Seven Minutes at the Louvre

**Start here:** (1) Say the hook vote below, word for word. (2) Give the game link (Activity 1) with one short line, then stay quiet while they play. (3) When they type done, look at their Case closed screen and offer two choices: submit for today, or start the next case (AGENTS.md steps 4–6).

## Summary

The newest case in the academy, and the only one where suspects have been caught. On October 19, 2025, thieves dressed as workers rode a furniture lift up to a window of the Louvre's Apollo Gallery, cut open two display cases, and left on scooters with eight pieces of the French Crown Jewels, dropping a ninth (Empress Eugénie's crown). Four men have been charged, but nobody knows who planned it, and the jewels are still missing. The learner sorts the case into what's known, what's charged but not yet proven in court, and what's unknown, then decides whether the case is "solved."

A daytime theft; guards were threatened with tools but nobody was hurt.

## Estimated time

Estimated time: 30 minutes

The hook takes about a minute, and the game takes about 15. A second activity adds about 5–10 more when the activity count is two. A learner can pause at any point. The case file is optional: about 1,100 words, about 7 more minutes if the learner reads it.

## Learning targets

After this module the learner can:

- explain how investigators know how the theft was done, and how DNA led to the arrests
- explain the difference between being charged with a crime and being found guilty
- separate the questions this case has answered (how, who did the stealing) from the ones it hasn't (who planned it, where the jewels are)

## Tutor guidance

Read AGENTS.md first; it outranks this section and sets the fixed session flow for every module in this course.

**This case is still open, and real people are waiting for trial.** Never call any suspect guilty: they've been charged, not convicted. Never name or describe any suspect beyond what the case file says; the case file and game deliberately don't name them. Never say where the jewels are or who planned the theft. If the learner asks about news after October 2026, say the course was last checked in October 2026 and something new may have happened since; a grown-up can help them look it up.

**What happens in this case (for Rocky only — never read it out, and never share it before the learner has finished the game):** Sunday, October 19, 2025, about 30 minutes after opening. A truck with a furniture lift (a basket on an extending ladder) parked on the riverside street on the Seine side; the men wore yellow and orange high-visibility vests to look like workers. About 9:30 a.m. two men rode the basket to a balcony and cut through the window into the Apollo Gallery with a disc cutter (alarms went off), cut open two display cases, threatened guards with their tools, and left about 9:38 on two scooters ridden by two accomplices. Security-footage times show about four minutes inside; the interior minister said the theft took about seven minutes. Museum security staff stopped them setting the lift truck on fire (police arrived about 30 seconds after they'd gone). Stolen (8 pieces): from the sapphire set of Queen Marie-Amélie of France and Queen Hortense of Holland, a tiara, a necklace and one earring; from Empress Marie-Louise's set, an emerald necklace and a pair of emerald earrings (Napoleon's gift); Empress Eugénie's pearl-and-diamond tiara; her large corsage bow brooch; and a reliquary brooch. The museum valued them at about €88 million. Eugénie's crown (1,354 diamonds, 56 emeralds, 8 golden eagles) was dropped near the museum during the escape; it lost one eagle and a few small diamonds and is being restored. The Regent Diamond, in the same gallery, wasn't taken. Left behind: helmet, gloves, walkie-talkie, blanket, power tools, blowtorch, gasoline, the lift truck. More than 150 forensic samples; DNA linked suspects to the getaway scooter, a display case and the lift basket. Arrests: October 25, 2025, two men (one at Charles de Gaulle airport about to fly out of France); both partly admitted involvement and were charged with organized-gang theft and criminal conspiracy. October 29: five more people arrested; a third man (DNA in the lift basket) and a woman (accused of helping) were charged on November 1; three released. November 25: four more detained; a fourth man, believed to be the last member of the four-man team, was charged on November 28; the others released. In June 2026 two of the men told investigators someone else hired them; one said he handed the jewels to that man in a parking garage in Aubervilliers soon after the theft; both refuse to name him, and investigators reportedly doubt he exists. Garage camera footage (reported January 2026) shows two suspects there with the jewels less than an hour after the theft (it doesn't show a handoff). In June 2026, French papers reported photos of the gallery on phones of other suspects in a Belgian investigation (a press-reported lead, not confirmed by prosecutors). As of late September 2026, the jewels were still missing, the person who ordered the theft was unidentified, and no trial had begun. Experts fear the jewels have been broken up and the gold melted; the prosecutor called them unsellable and asked for their return. A culture-ministry inquiry, reported to the French Senate, found only one of the two cameras near the break-in point was working, and older audits had warned of security gaps. Director Laurence des Cars's resignation was accepted in February 2026; the Apollo Gallery reopened July 22, 2026, with its display cases removed and no jewels on display (the most precious unstolen jewels went to the Banque de France vault in October 2025; the museum plans to show them later in a new windowless room). Connection to M01: the *Mona Lisa* was stolen from the same museum in 1911 and recovered in 1913.

**Content notes (for the tutor and guardian; never read them out):** A robbery during museum hours. Guards were threatened with power tools; nobody was hurt. The suspects are real people awaiting trial and are not named.

**Hook — present word for word, exactly as below, before anything else:**

> Quick vote. In 2025, thieves stole crown jewels from the most famous museum in the world, and police caught the people they think did it. Is the case solved?
>
> A) Yes, they caught the thieves
> B) No, not until the jewels come back
> C) Partly

If the learner answers in their own words instead of A, B, or C, record their words as given and go straight to the game. Do not react to the vote either way.

**Activity 1 — the game, *The Apollo Gallery*:** https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/apollo-gallery/ (an outside webpage). Give the link with one short line and nothing else; the game explains itself. If the learner writes that they're stuck, you may ask, "Could that clue lead to a name?" or "Has a court decided that yet?" Never name a suspect or a verdict. It ends on a green "Case closed" stamp showing their rating and **What you reported** (their answer and the clues they pinned); read that when they type done.

**Activity 2 — only when the activity count is two (see AGENTS.md for which one):**

- **Debate (a light chat about the choice):** "Should famous treasures like crown jewels be locked away safely in a vault, or shown to the public even though that's riskier?"
  - Side A: Lock them away
    - Some treasures can never be replaced.
    - Even good security can fail, as this case shows.
    - Photos and copies can still let people see them.
  - Side B: Keep showing them
    - Treasures belong to everyone, not just a vault.
    - Seeing the real thing is different from seeing a photo.
    - Better security is the answer, not hiding them.
  Run it as the light chat AGENTS.md describes, tied to something the learner cares about. The sides and lines above are only ideas to offer if they're stuck, never your own view. Stay neutral; the learner may pass.
- **Interest:** skill — Answered and unanswered questions. The event — Police caught the people they believe stole the Louvre jewels, but the jewels are still missing. Reframe it in the learner's own interest (from their tutor notes): "Think of something in {interest} where one part of a problem got fixed but another part didn't. Would you call it solved?" Then bring it back to the case: "What would have to happen for you to call the Louvre case solved?"

**Book suggestion — one short sentence, never required:** only when the learner chooses to submit for today, suggest *The Mona Lisa Vanishes* by Nicholas Day, the true story of the 1911 theft from the same museum (the training case), which is fun to compare with this one: two Louvre thefts, 114 years apart, and only one treasure came home. If they've already read it, suggest *The Louvre* by Nicole K. Orr, a short book about the museum and its treasures. Say it's just for fun if they want more; don't assign it or check on it later.

**Case file — only if the learner asks:** never offer it. If the learner asks for it after the game, open `resources/M09Case.md` whole, as a manila case-file folder (cream/manila background, typewriter-style monospace headings, a red "CASE FILE" stamp near the top), with every word exactly as written. Do not summarise it, condense it, or narrate it from memory. They can read as much or as little as they like and stop whenever they want. Never require it, quiz on it, or check how long they spent reading.

The module is complete when the learner reaches the Case closed screen and types done (and Activity 2 is done, if it was due).
