# Case 001, Part 1: The Man in the Last Row

**Start here:** (1) Say the hook vote below, word for word. (2) Right away, open the case game, *Flight 305*, at https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/flight-305/ and let the learner play it to the "Case closed" screen. (3) Look back at the vote in one line, then ask the two talk-back questions, one at a time. (4) At the very end, after any Activity 2 and the book suggestion, offer the case file, `resources/M02Case.md` as an optional extra; open it only if the learner wants it, and they can read as much or as little of it as they like.

## Summary

The first real unsolved case. On the night before Thanksgiving 1971, a quiet man in the last row of a short flight from Portland to Seattle hijacked the plane, collected $200,000 and four parachutes, and jumped from the back stairs into a stormy night over the forests of southwest Washington. Nobody knows who he was. This part rebuilds what happened that night, in order.

A bomb threat and a hijacking; nobody was hurt.

## Estimated time

Estimated time: 30 minutes

The hook and talk-back add about 5 minutes, and the game takes about 15. A second activity adds about 5–10 more when the activity count is two. A learner can pause at any point. The case file is optional: about 1,250 words, about 8 more minutes if the learner reads it.

## Learning targets

After this module the learner can:

- put the main events of the hijacking in the right order
- explain why two witnesses who agree are stronger evidence than one who doesn't
- explain how the time of the bump in the tail helped the FBI decide where to search

## Tutor guidance

Read AGENTS.md first; it outranks this section and sets the fixed session flow for every module in this course.

**This case is unsolved.** Never say who D.B. Cooper was or whether he survived; nobody knows. Part 2 (the next module) deals with the survival question, so don't open it here.

**What happens in this case (for Rocky only — never read it out, and never share it before the learner has finished the game):** On Wednesday, November 24, 1971, a man in a dark suit and black clip-on tie paid cash for a one-way ticket from Portland to Seattle on Northwest Orient Flight 305 as "Dan Cooper," and sat in the last row (seat 18E). Soon after takeoff he handed flight attendant Florence Schaffner a note; she put it in her purse unread until he told her he had a bomb. He showed wires, red sticks and a battery in his briefcase and demanded $200,000 in $20 bills, four parachutes (two main, two reserve) and fuel trucks in Seattle. The plane circled Seattle for more than two hours; passengers were told of a mechanical problem. The FBI microfilmed all 10,000 bills. He said "Looks like Tacoma down there" and knew McChord AFB was about 20 minutes from the airport. The plane landed around 5:40 p.m.; the 35 other passengers left; the crew (Captain William Scott, First Officer Bill Rataczak, Flight Engineer Harold Anderson, flight attendant Tina Mucklow) stayed. He was calm and polite and asked for meals for the crew. Orders: south toward Mexico, about 10,000 feet, minimum speed, gear down, flaps lowered, unpressurized, aft stairs ready; refuel in Reno. Takeoff around 7:40 p.m. Around 8:00 he sent Mucklow to the cockpit (she saw him tying something around his waist) and the aft-stair warning light came on; at about 8:13 the tail bumped upward. Two fighter jets and a training plane followed but saw nothing in the clouds. In Reno, late that night, he was gone with the money. Left behind: the tie with a mother-of-pearl tie clip, eight cigarette butts, 66 fingerprints lifted from the plane, one main parachute and a reserve with cords cut; the classroom dummy reserve, sewn shut, was missing. The two attendants described him alike (mid-forties, about 5'10"–6', dark hair, brown eyes); passenger Bill Mitchell remembered a smaller, slimmer man (about 5'9"). A reporter's mistake produced "D.B." The FBI's first estimate put the jump near Ariel and Lake Merwin, north of the Lewis River; the co-pilot later thought it was farther south toward Portland. The search found nothing.

**Content notes (for the tutor and guardian; never read them out):** A hijacking with a bomb threat (whether the bomb was real is unknown). Nobody was hurt. No violence is described.

**Hook — present word for word, exactly as below, before anything else:**

> Quick vote before you start. A man hijacks a plane, collects a pile of money, and then does something no hijacker had ever done in the U.S. What do you think it was?
>
> A) He jumped out of the plane with a parachute
> B) He hid on the plane and walked off with the crew
> C) He gave all the money back

If the learner answers in their own words instead of A, B, or C, record their words as given and go straight to the game. Do not react to the vote either way.

**Look back at the vote — one line, once the learner reaches the "Case closed" screen:** remind them what they voted and say in one sentence whether the case bore it out (the answer is A). No lecture.

**Talk-back — at most two questions, one at a time (skip entirely if the tutor turned talk-back off):** Ask them after the game. The learner can answer from what they found in the game; they don't need the case file.

1. Why did the FBI photograph every single bill before handing over the money?
   *(What a good answer mentions: so they'd have every serial number, and if any of the money was ever spent or found, they could prove it was the hijacker's.)*
2. Why does it matter so much exactly when the crew felt the bump in the tail?
   *(What a good answer mentions: knowing the time, plus how fast and which way the plane was flying, tells searchers roughly where he jumped.)*

Keep it light and about what happened. Never write a card, header, or label anywhere that answers a talk-back question.

**Activity 1 — the game, *Flight 305*:** open https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/flight-305/ (a Twine detective game on an outside webpage; you can't see inside it). Tell the learner once before they start:
- they're an FBI agent in Seattle on Thanksgiving morning, with 7 leads to spend;
- a new place costs one lead, and going back to a place they've visited is free;
- interviews allow only two questions, and witnesses won't always agree;
- the timeline board (free) is where they put the night's events in order, and newspaper rumors don't belong on it;
- the deduction board (free) joins two clues into a new idea;
- at the end they report the timeline, build a wanted poster line by line, and choose where to send the searchers;
- what they collect is saved to an evidence locker for Part 2 (on the same device).

Don't tell them the order of events, what goes on the poster, or where to search. If they're stuck, you may ask, "Which witness haven't you talked to yet?" or "Do any two witnesses agree?" The game ends on a green "Case closed" stamp. Then ask, one at a time: where they sent the searchers, which clues told them to, and their detective rating (stars out of 3). The game's own last screens explain what the real FBI did; don't repeat them, but answer questions about them from the Rocky-only notes above.

**Activity 2 — only when the activity count is two (see AGENTS.md for which one):**

- **Debate (a light chat about the choice):** "Some people call D.B. Cooper a folk hero, and people even wrote songs about him. Should he be called a hero?"
  - Side A: Some people see him as a kind of hero
    - Nobody was hurt, and he was polite to the crew.
    - He let all the passengers go in Seattle.
    - He took on a big airline, not ordinary people, and outsmarted the FBI.
  - Side B: He shouldn't be called a hero
    - He threatened a whole plane full of people with a bomb.
    - The crew and passengers had no way of knowing whether they would be hurt.
    - Calling a hijacker a hero might make the crime seem like a game.
  Run it as the light chat AGENTS.md describes, tied to something the learner cares about. The sides and lines above are only ideas to offer if they're stuck, never your own view. Stay neutral; the learner may pass.
- **Interest:** skill — Weighing witnesses. The event — Two flight attendants who sat near the hijacker described him the same way, but a passenger across the aisle remembered someone smaller. Reframe it in the learner's own interest (from their tutor notes): "Think of a moment in {interest} that people remember differently. How would you decide whose memory to trust?" Then bring it back to the case: "Why did the FBI trust the flight attendants' description more than the passenger's?"

**Book suggestion — always offer, never require:** once the activities are done, suggest *Unsolved Case Files: Escape at 10,000 Feet* by Tom Sullivan, a graphic novel that tells this hijacking minute by minute, with real FBI documents and photos mixed in. If they'd like something shorter, mention *The D.B. Cooper Hijacking* by Tom Streissguth instead. Say it's just for fun if they want more; don't assign it or check on it later.

**Case file — optional, at the very end:** after the book suggestion, offer it in one line: it's the real detective file behind the game, with extra details, and they can read it now, later, or skip it. If they want it, open `resources/M02Case.md` whole, as a manila case-file folder (cream/manila background, typewriter-style monospace headings, a red "CASE FILE" stamp near the top), with every word exactly as written. Do not summarise it, condense it, or narrate it from memory. They can read as much or as little as they like and stop whenever they want. Never require it, quiz on it, or check how long they spent reading.

When the activity (or both activities) is finished, the book has been suggested, the case file has been offered, and the learner confirms they're done, the module is complete.
