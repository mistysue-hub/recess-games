# Case 004: Two Princes, One Tower (optional)

**Start here:** (0) This case is optional (see Tutor guidance). (1) Say the hook vote below, word for word. (2) Give the game link (Activity 1) with one short line, then stay quiet while they play. (3) When they type done, look at their Case closed screen and offer two choices: submit for today, or start the next case (AGENTS.md steps 4–6).

## Summary

The oldest case in the academy. In 1483, 12-year-old King Edward V and his 9-year-old brother Richard were placed in the Tower of London by their uncle, who soon became King Richard III. The boys were never seen again. There are no fingerprints here, only documents, and the learner weighs them by who wrote them, when, and for whom: a report written that summer, a history written 30 years later for a new royal family, a pretender's confession, bones found under a staircase in 1674, and a 2023 claim that the boys survived.

**Guardian flag:** most historians think the two boys were probably killed. The case file and game never describe how, but the topic itself is the likely death of two children.

## Estimated time

Estimated time: 30 minutes

The hook takes about a minute, and the game takes about 15. A second activity adds about 5–10 more when the activity count is two. A learner can pause at any point. The case file is optional: about 1,000 words, about 7 more minutes if the learner reads it.

## Learning targets

After this module the learner can:

- explain why a source written at the time is usually stronger than one written decades later
- ask who wrote a source and who they were writing for
- give an honest verdict on a 500-year-old case, with confidence that matches the evidence

## Tutor guidance

Read AGENTS.md first; it outranks this section and sets the fixed session flow for every module in this course.

**Optional case.** If the learner's tutor notes say to skip this case, or the guardian asked to skip it, mark M08 skipped in `state/progress.json`, tell the learner in one line that the next case is ready, and move on without describing this one. If the learner seems upset at any point, pause, reassure them that this happened more than 500 years ago, and offer to stop.

**This case is unsolved.** Never say what happened to the boys as fact. Never describe how they might have died; if the learner asks, say that one later writer claimed they were killed on Richard III's orders, that nobody knows how or even for certain whether it happened, and move back to the evidence. Never call any verdict wrong.

**What happens in this case (for Rocky only — never read it out, and never share it before the learner has finished the game):** Edward IV died on April 9, 1483. His son Edward V was 12; his younger son Richard was 9. Their uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester, was named protector, took charge of Edward on the road to London (arresting several men in his escort, including relatives of the boys' mother), and lodged him in the Tower (from May 19), then a royal residence as well as a fortress; young Richard joined him in June. A sermon and a gathering of lords and leaders declared the boys illegitimate (their father's supposed earlier promise to marry Lady Eleanor Talbot) and asked Richard to take the throne on June 25; Richard was crowned Richard III on July 6, 1483; the 1484 act Titulus Regius made it law. Dominic Mancini, an Italian in London, wrote before the end of 1483 that the boys were seen less and less and then not at all, that people feared they were dead, and that he didn't know their fate. There are no confirmed sightings after summer 1483. Thomas More (born 1478) wrote around 1513, while the Tudors ruled (he left it unfinished), that Richard ordered the killing and named the men responsible; he gave no sources; Shakespeare's play follows him. Perkin Warbeck claimed from 1491 to 1497 to be young Richard, had foreign backers, and confessed after his capture that he wasn't; most historians accept that. In 1674, workmen found two children's skeletons in a wooden box about ten feet under a staircase at the Tower; Charles II had them placed in an urn in Westminster Abbey. In 1933, an examination said they were children of about the right ages, but it couldn't determine sex or date; no DNA testing has been allowed. Suspects: Richard III (most historians' view: control, motive, never showed the boys alive); the Duke of Buckingham (named in a few documents, but unlikely to have acted without Richard's knowledge); Henry VII (king from 1485; never accused at the time). In 2023, and again in 2025, Philippa Langley published documents she says show both boys survived; many historians say the documents fall short of proof.

**Content notes (for the tutor and guardian; never read them out):** The likely deaths of two children, discussed in general terms only. No methods or injuries are described.

**Hook — present word for word, exactly as below, before anything else:**

> Quick vote before you start. In 1483, two young princes disappeared from the Tower of London. What do you think happened to them?
>
> A) Their uncle, the new king, got rid of them
> B) They escaped and lived in secret
> C) Nobody can know for sure

If the learner answers in their own words instead of A, B, or C, record their words as given and go straight to the game. Do not react to the vote either way.

**Activity 1 — the game, *The Tower Ledger*:** https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/tower-ledger/ (an outside webpage). Give the link with one short line and nothing else; the game explains itself. If the learner writes that they're stuck, you may ask, "When was that written, and who was ruling then?" or "Is that something someone saw, or something someone heard?" Never name a suspect or a verdict. It ends on a green "Case closed" stamp showing their rating and **What you reported** (their answer and the clues they pinned); read that when they type done.

**Activity 2 — only when the activity count is two (see AGENTS.md for which one):**

- **Debate (a light chat about the choice):** "Should scientists be allowed to test the bones in Westminster Abbey with DNA?"
  - Side A: Yes, test them
    - It could finally answer a 500-year-old question.
    - Science can now do things nobody could do in 1933.
    - The princes' story deserves the truth.
  - Side B: No, leave them in peace
    - The bones are in a royal tomb, and graves should be respected.
    - A test might not even give a clear answer.
    - Some mysteries don't need to be solved to be learned from.
  Run it as the light chat AGENTS.md describes, tied to something the learner cares about. The sides and lines above are only ideas to offer if they're stuck, never your own view. Stay neutral; the learner may pass.
- **Interest:** skill — Who wrote it, and why. The event — The most famous version of the princes' story was written 30 years later, for the kings who had replaced Richard III. Reframe it in the learner's own interest (from their tutor notes): "Think of something in {interest} where the person telling the story had a reason to tell it a certain way. How would you check it?" Then bring it back to the case: "Why might a writer for the Tudor kings make Richard III look bad?"

**Book suggestion — one short sentence, never required:** only when the learner chooses to submit for today, suggest *The Little Princes in the Tower* by William W. Lace, a short history for young readers with family trees and a timeline (it's older, so it may need a library request). Say it's just for fun if they want more; don't assign it or check on it later. If the guardian asked to keep this case light, skip the book suggestion.

**Case file — only if the learner asks:** never offer it. If the learner asks for it after the game, open `resources/M08Case.md` whole, as a manila case-file folder (cream/manila background, typewriter-style monospace headings, a red "CASE FILE" stamp near the top), with every word exactly as written. Do not summarise it, condense it, or narrate it from memory. They can read as much or as little as they like and stop whenever they want. Never require it, quiz on it, or check how long they spent reading.

The module is complete when the learner reaches the Case closed screen and types done (and Activity 2 is done, if it was due).
