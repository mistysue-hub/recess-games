# CASE FILE 003 — PART 1

**Case name:** 81 Minutes
**Place:** Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum, Boston, Massachusetts
**Date of crime:** Early morning, Sunday, March 18, 1990
**Missing:** 13 works of art
**Thieves' real names:** ████████ ████████ UNPROVEN
**Status:** UNSOLVED

---

## Welcome back, Detective

Your last two cases were about people who vanished. This one is about paintings that vanished, and they're still missing. Nobody has seen them in more than 35 years. Read carefully: this case has a clock, a map of rooms, and a machine that recorded footsteps.

---

## Part 1: A Palace Full of Art

Isabella Stewart Gardner was a wealthy art collector in Boston. More than a hundred years ago, she built a museum that looks like a Venetian palace, with a flower-filled courtyard in the middle. She filled it with paintings, sculptures, furniture and letters, and she arranged every object herself.

When she died in 1924, her will left strict instructions: nothing in the museum could ever be rearranged, sold, or added. The museum would stay exactly the way she left it.

---

## Part 2: The Knock at the Door

It was the night of St. Patrick's Day, and Boston had been celebrating. Inside the Gardner Museum, two young security guards were on duty. One was 23. The other was 25, working his first night shift.

At **1:24 a.m.**, the buzzer rang at the side door. On the security camera, the guard saw two men in what looked like police uniforms.

"Boston police," one said. "We're here about a disturbance."

The guard wasn't supposed to let anyone in at night. But they were police officers, weren't they? He buzzed them through.

---

## Part 3: "This Is a Robbery"

Inside, the "officers" told the guard he looked familiar, as if there might be a warrant out for him. They asked him to step away from the desk, where the only alarm button was. Then they told him to call the other guard over.

Within minutes, both guards were handcuffed. One of the men said, "This is a robbery."

The thieves led the guards down to the basement and left them tied up there. Neither guard was badly hurt. But nobody in the building could call for help now.

---

## Part 4: The Footstep Machine

The thieves took the tapes from the security cameras. They also took the paper printouts from the museum's motion-detector system. But they missed something.

**EXHIBIT A: The motion detectors**

The museum had about 60 motion detectors, small sensors that notice when someone moves through a room. Every movement was printed out on paper, with the exact time, and also saved on a computer. The thieves took the paper, but they never touched the copy on the computer.

Thanks to it, investigators know:

- Movement in the galleries started at about **1:48 a.m.**
- The thieves went upstairs to the **Dutch Room** and to a long, narrow hall called the **Short Gallery**.
- They made two trips out to their car, and left for good at **2:45 a.m.**

From the knock at the door to the last trip out: **81 minutes**.

---

## Part 5: What They Took

**EXHIBIT B: The 13 missing works**

From the **Dutch Room**:

- *The Concert* by Johannes Vermeer, one of only about 35 Vermeer paintings in the world
- *Christ in the Storm on the Sea of Galilee* by Rembrandt, his only known seascape
- *A Lady and Gentleman in Black* by Rembrandt
- a tiny self-portrait etching by Rembrandt, about the size of a postage stamp
- *Landscape with an Obelisk* by Govert Flinck
- an ancient Chinese bronze cup called a **gu**, about 3,000 years old

From the **Short Gallery**:

- five small drawings by Edgar Degas
- a bronze **eagle**, the decoration from the top of a flagpole from Napoleon's army

From the **Blue Room**, on the first floor:

- *Chez Tortoni* by Édouard Manet

The thieves were rough. They threw frames onto the marble floor, smashing the glass, and cut two of the Rembrandt paintings out of their frames with a blade. Others, like the Vermeer, they pulled out of their frames.

---

## Part 6: The Strange Choices

Some of what the thieves did makes no sense, even today.

- They took a cheap-looking bronze eagle, but walked right past paintings worth far more.
- They pulled a large Rembrandt self-portrait off the wall, then left it leaning against a cabinet. It's painted on a wooden panel, not canvas, so it would have been heavy and impossible to roll up.
- They never went to the third floor, where one of the museum's most valuable paintings, by Titian, was hanging.

---

## Part 7: The Room With No Footsteps

Now look at Exhibit A again.

The motion detectors recorded the thieves moving through the Dutch Room and the Short Gallery. But in the **Blue Room**, where the Manet was taken, the detectors recorded **no movement at all** while the thieves were in the building.

The only footsteps the detectors recorded in the Blue Room that night came earlier, from the guard on his rounds.

Investigators also learned that earlier that night, the guard had opened the side door for a moment and then closed it again. He said it was part of his routine check. Nobody can say for sure why the Blue Room was silent.

---

## Part 8: Morning

The next morning, when the day guard arrived and nobody answered the door, police were called. They found the two night guards still in the basement. They were unhurt.

On the walls upstairs, there were empty frames, broken glass, and bare hooks.

---

## Detective's Notes

- A machine can be a witness too. The motion-detector record tells us where the thieves went, and when.
- Pay attention to what the thieves didn't take. It can tell you as much as what they did.
- One fact doesn't fit: the empty frame in a room with no recorded footsteps. Hold on to it.

**MARCH 18, 1990**

`1:24 a.m.` the buzzer → `1:48 a.m.` movement upstairs → Dutch Room and Short Gallery → `2:45 a.m.` gone → `Morning` guards found in the basement
