## Congratulations, Detective

You've worked a training case and five real cold cases. You followed a hijacker into a storm, three men off an island, thieves through a museum at night, two princes into a tower, and a crew up a ladder at the Louvre.

Now comes the part real detectives do at the end of every case: the **report**. A report isn't a story. It's a careful list of what you know, what you think, and how sure you are.

---

## The Five Parts of a Case Report

**1. The facts.** Only things someone can check: a time, a place, a serial number, a DNA match. Not what someone claims, not what an expert believes, and never a guess.

**2. Your best theory.** Your answer to the big question in the case. Pick the one that fits the evidence best, not the one that makes the best movie.

**3. Evidence for it.** The facts and statements that make your theory more likely.

**4. Evidence against it.** This is the part most people skip, and the part that makes a report trustworthy. Every theory in an unsolved case has something that doesn't fit. A good detective writes it down.

**5. Your verdict, and how sure you are.** Certain, probably, or "can't tell yet." Your confidence should be only as strong as your evidence.

---

## A Sample Report

Here's how a report on your training case might look. Remember, this one was solved.

**EXHIBIT A: Report on The Vanishing Mona Lisa**

> **The question:** Who stole the *Mona Lisa* in 1911?
>
> **The facts:**
> 1. The frame and glass box were found in a stairwell inside the museum.
> 2. There was a fingerprint on the glass.
> 3. In 1913, Vincenzo Peruggia, who had once worked at the Louvre, was found with the painting in a trunk in his hotel room.
>
> **My best theory:** Peruggia, a former museum worker, took it.
>
> **Evidence for it:** He had the painting. He knew the building and how the glass came off, because he had fitted the glass boxes himself.
>
> **Evidence against it:** Police searched his room during the investigation and didn't find anything.
>
> **My verdict:** Peruggia did it. How sure I am: certain. He had the painting, he admitted taking it, and a court found him guilty.

Notice that this detective still wrote down the evidence against. Even in a solved case, something didn't fit at first.

## Your Case Will Be Different

The cases you'll choose from are **not** like the training case. Nobody found the hijacker. No court ever decided what happened to the escapers or the princes. The Gardner art and the Louvre jewels are still missing.

That means "certain" is a trap. The best verdicts in these cases sound like this:

- "Probably, because most of the evidence points one way, but it isn't proven."
- "I can't tell yet. The evidence could go either way."

Those aren't weak answers. They're honest ones, and honesty is the most important tool a detective has.

---

## Detective's Notes

- Facts first. Claims, opinions and guesses go somewhere else.
- Always write down the evidence **against** your own theory.
- Match how sure you are to how strong the evidence is.
