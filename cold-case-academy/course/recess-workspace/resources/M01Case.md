# CASE FILE — TRAINING CASE

**Case name:** The Vanishing Mona Lisa
**Place:** The Louvre Museum, Paris, France
**Date of crime:** Monday, August 21, 1911
**Status:** SOLVED (after more than two years)

---

## Welcome, Detective

Every detective starts with a practice case. This one is real, it happened more than a hundred years ago, and it fooled some of the smartest police in the world. Unlike the other cases in this academy, this one has an answer. Read it carefully, because the mistakes the investigators made are the same mistakes detectives still make today.

---

## Part 1: The Most Boring Monday in Paris

The Louvre is one of the biggest museums on Earth. In 1911 it held hundreds of thousands of objects, spread across rooms so long you could get tired just walking from one end to the other. On Mondays, the museum was closed. Visitors stayed home. Workers came in to clean, fix things, and move paintings around.

That is why nobody worried when a man in a white work smock walked through the quiet halls early that Monday morning. Workers in white smocks were everywhere. He looked like he belonged.

He stopped in a room called the Salon Carré. On the wall hung a small painting, smaller than most people expect. It showed a woman with folded hands and a calm, strange half-smile. It was the *Mona Lisa*, painted by Leonardo da Vinci about four hundred years earlier.

The man lifted the painting off its hooks. He carried it to a nearby stairwell, took it out of its heavy frame and protective glass box, and left the frame behind. The painting itself is on a thin wooden panel, not canvas, so he could not roll it up. He tucked it under his smock and headed down the stairs.

At the bottom, a door was locked. A plumber happened to walk by, and the man in the smock asked for help. The plumber thought he was just another worker, so he opened the door for him. The man walked out of the building.

Nobody stopped him.

---

## Part 2: Nobody Notices

Here is the strangest clue in the whole case: for more than a day, almost nobody realized the painting was gone.

On Tuesday morning, an artist named Louis Béroud arrived to paint a picture of the room. He wanted the *Mona Lisa* in his scene. When he got to the wall, he saw only four iron hooks.

He asked a guard where the painting was. The guard shrugged. Paintings were often taken down to be photographed, he said. It was probably with the photographers.

Béroud waited. And waited. Finally a guard went to check with the photographers. They didn't have it. Nobody had it.

The *Mona Lisa* had been stolen.

---

## Part 3: The Investigation

The museum closed for a week while police searched every room. They found one important piece of evidence: the empty frame and glass box in the stairwell. On the glass was a fingerprint.

In 1911, fingerprinting was brand new. The French police did keep fingerprint records of people who had been arrested, but their system was hard to search, and the print on the glass did not lead them to anyone.

So the police chased theories instead. Lots of them.

**Theory A: A rich collector paid a master thief.** Many people imagined a brilliant criminal, maybe hired by a millionaire who wanted the painting hanging secretly in his mansion.

**Theory B: A rival country did it.** Some newspapers hinted that Germany was behind it, to embarrass France.

**Theory C: Artists who hated old art did it.** This theory led police to a famous poet, Guillaume Apollinaire, and his friend, a young painter named Pablo Picasso. The two men had once been mixed up in a different museum theft (a man they knew had stolen small statues from the Louvre). Apollinaire was arrested and spent several days in jail. Picasso was questioned. Neither had anything to do with the *Mona Lisa*. Both were released.

Meanwhile, something unexpected happened. Crowds lined up at the Louvre when it reopened, not to see the painting, but to stare at the empty space where it used to hang. Newspapers all over the world printed the woman's face. Before the theft, the *Mona Lisa* was admired by art experts. After the theft, it was famous to everyone.

Months passed. Then a year. Then two. Most people believed the painting was gone forever.

---

## Part 4: The Letter from Florence

In late 1913, an art dealer in Florence, Italy, named Alfredo Geri got a letter. The writer said he had the *Mona Lisa* and wanted to return it to Italy, where Leonardo da Vinci was born.

Geri thought it was probably a joke. But he agreed to meet. He brought along the director of Florence's most famous art museum, the Uffizi Gallery.

The man who showed up was named Vincenzo Peruggia. He took them to his small hotel room, pulled a wooden trunk out from under the bed, lifted out a false bottom, and unwrapped a painting.

The museum director studied it closely: the wooden panel, the brushwork, the museum's marks on the back. It was real.

Geri and the director left calmly, as if they planned to buy it. Then they called the police. Peruggia was arrested the same day.

---

## Part 5: Who Was Vincenzo Peruggia?

He was not a master criminal. He was an Italian handyman and house painter who had lived in Paris. Here is the part that stings: he had worked at the Louvre. His job had been fitting glass boxes over paintings, including the one that protected the *Mona Lisa*. He knew the building, he knew Mondays were quiet, and he knew exactly how the glass came off. On the day of the theft, he had hidden inside the museum, then simply walked out in a worker's smock with the painting.

Even more painful for the police: they had actually visited Peruggia's room during the investigation, because he was one of many former workers they checked. They did not find the painting. It was sitting in the trunk the whole time.

And the fingerprint on the glass? Peruggia had been arrested once before, so the police already had his fingerprints on file. But back then, police records only kept prints from a person's right hand. The print on the glass came from a left thumb. The one clue that pointed straight at him couldn't be matched.

At his trial, Peruggia said he stole the painting out of love for Italy. He believed (wrongly) that it had been stolen from Italy long ago, and he wanted to bring it home. Many Italians cheered for him. He was sentenced to a little over a year in jail, and served less than that.

The *Mona Lisa* went on a short tour of Italy, then traveled back to Paris. It returned to the Louvre on January 4, 1914.

---

## Part 6: The Theory That Wouldn't Die

Even after the case was solved, some people refused to believe the simple answer. Years later, a magazine story claimed Peruggia was just a helper for a secret mastermind who made six fake *Mona Lisas* and sold them to rich buyers. There was never good evidence for this story. But it was more exciting than "a handyman walked out with it," so people kept repeating it.

---

## Detective's Notes

- The thief looked like he belonged, so nobody noticed him.
- The guards assumed the painting was somewhere ordinary, so nobody noticed it was gone.
- The police expected a genius, so they overlooked an ordinary man who had the key thing a thief needs: inside knowledge.
- Even a real clue, like a fingerprint, only helps if you can use it.

Keep these notes. You'll need them for every case that follows.
