# CASE FILE 001 — PART 1

**Case name:** The Man in the Last Row
**Place:** Northwest Orient Flight 305, from Portland, Oregon, to Seattle, Washington
**Date of crime:** Wednesday, November 24, 1971 (the night before Thanksgiving)
**Suspect's name:** "Dan Cooper" · **Real name:** ████████ UNKNOWN
**Status:** UNSOLVED

---

## Welcome back, Detective

Your training case had an answer. This one doesn't. The FBI worked on it for 45 years and never found out who the hijacker was, or what happened to him. Read carefully. Your job in this file is to figure out *what happened that night, in order*, and to notice which facts are solid and which depend on one person's memory.

---

## Part 1: A Ticket to Seattle

It was the afternoon before Thanksgiving, and the Portland airport was crowded with families flying home for the holiday. A quiet man in a dark suit, a white shirt and a thin black clip-on tie walked up to the Northwest Orient counter. He carried a briefcase and wore a raincoat.

He paid cash for a one-way ticket to Seattle, a flight of only about 30 minutes. The name he gave was **Dan Cooper**.

He boarded Flight 305, a Boeing 727, and took a seat in the very last row. He ordered a drink, bourbon and 7-Up, and lit a cigarette. (In 1971, people could smoke on planes.) Nobody paid him any attention. He looked like every other businessman going home for Thanksgiving.

---

## Part 2: The Note

Soon after takeoff, the man handed a folded note to Florence Schaffner, the flight attendant sitting in the jump seat near him. She thought it was a lonely businessman's phone number, so she dropped it into her purse without reading it.

He leaned toward her. She had better look at that note, he told her. He had a bomb.

He opened his briefcase just enough for her to see inside: wires, red sticks, and a large battery. Then he told her his demands, and she wrote them down:

**EXHIBIT A: The demands**

- **$200,000** in $20 bills
- **Four parachutes**: two main parachutes and two reserves
- **Fuel trucks** waiting when the plane landed in Seattle

Schaffner carried the message to the cockpit. The captain, William Scott, radioed the airline, and the airline called the FBI.

---

## Part 3: Circling Seattle

The plane did not land right away. For more than two hours, Flight 305 circled over Seattle while people on the ground rushed to gather the money and parachutes.

Most of the passengers never knew anything was wrong. The pilot told them there was a small mechanical problem, and they would circle for a while. One passenger, a college student named Bill Mitchell, was sitting across the aisle from the hijacker. He was mostly bored.

**EXHIBIT B: The microfilm**

On the ground, the FBI did something very smart. Before handing over the money, agents photographed **every single bill** on microfilm. That meant they had a record of all 10,000 serial numbers. If any of those bills ever showed up again, anywhere, they would know it came from the hijacker.

During the long circling, the hijacker looked out the window and said, "Looks like Tacoma down there." He also seemed to know that McChord Air Force Base was only about 20 minutes' drive from the Seattle airport. Most passengers wouldn't know a detail like that.

---

## Part 4: The Swap

Around 5:40 p.m., the plane finally landed in Seattle. The money and the four parachutes were brought aboard. In exchange, the hijacker let the 35 other passengers walk off the plane. They were safe.

The crew had to stay: the two pilots, the flight engineer, and flight attendant **Tina Mucklow**. For the rest of the night, Mucklow was the person closest to the hijacker. She later said he stayed calm and polite. He even asked for meals to be brought aboard for the flight crew.

Then he gave his flight orders, and they were very exact:

- Fly south toward Mexico.
- Stay at about 10,000 feet, and fly as slowly as the plane can safely go.
- Keep the landing gear down and the wing flaps lowered.
- Don't pressurize the cabin.
- Be ready to lower the **back stairs** of the plane in flight.

The Boeing 727 was one of the few airliners with a set of stairs that folded down from under the tail. The crew told him the plane would need more fuel for such a slow, low trip, so they agreed to stop in Reno, Nevada, first.

Around 7:40 p.m., Flight 305 took off again, heading south.

---

## Part 5: Into the Dark

The night was dark and stormy, with sleet. At 10,000 feet it was far below freezing. Two fighter jets and a smaller training plane from McChord Air Force Base followed the airliner, but in the thick clouds they could barely see it.

Around 8:00 p.m., the hijacker told Tina Mucklow to go to the cockpit and stay there. As she left, she saw him tying something around his waist.

At about the same time, a warning light came on in the cockpit. The back stairs had been lowered.

At about **8:13 p.m.**, the crew felt the tail of the plane suddenly jump upward, then settle. It was as if a weight had just left the stairs.

Nobody in the cockpit knew for sure what had happened. They kept flying.

---

## Part 6: Gone in Reno

Late that night, Flight 305 landed in Reno with its back stairs still hanging down. Police and FBI agents surrounded the plane.

The hijacker was gone. So was the money.

**EXHIBIT C: What he left behind**

- his black clip-on tie, with a mother-of-pearl tie clip
- eight cigarette butts
- one unused main parachute
- a reserve parachute with some of its cords cut out

One more strange thing: one of the reserve parachutes he'd been given was a **classroom dummy**, sewn shut, used only for practice. It was not on the plane. He had taken it with him.

---

## Part 7: Who Was He?

**EXHIBIT D: The witnesses**

The FBI interviewed everyone who had seen him. The two flight attendants, who had spent the most time near him, described him the same way: a man in his mid-forties, about 5 feet 10 inches to 6 feet tall, with dark hair and brown eyes. But Bill Mitchell, the passenger across the aisle, remembered a smaller, slimmer man, about 5 feet 9. The FBI's wanted poster had to be a best guess.

Even his name became a mistake. He had called himself Dan Cooper. But a reporter, rushing to meet a deadline, mixed the name up and wrote **"D.B. Cooper."** The nickname stuck, and that's what the world has called him ever since.

---

## Part 8: Where to Search?

The FBI now had to decide where to send searchers. They used the facts: how fast the plane was flying, which way it was going, and the exact time of that bump in the tail.

The FBI's first computer estimate pointed to thick forest near the small town of **Ariel** and **Lake Merwin**, north of the Lewis River in southwest Washington. Soldiers, police officers and volunteers searched there for weeks. The co-pilot, Bill Rataczak, later thought the jump happened farther south, closer to Portland.

![Map of Flight 305's path south from Seattle past Tacoma toward Portland, with the first search area circled north of the Lewis River near Ariel and Lake Merwin](https://mistysue-hub.github.io/recess-games/cold-case-academy/course/recess-workspace/resources/images/M02-flight-path.png)

The search found nothing at all.

## Detective's Notes

- Build the night in order before you decide anything.
- Two witnesses who agree are stronger than one who doesn't.
- Some clues rest on one person's memory. Mark them that way.
- Tiny facts, like the exact minute of a bump, can point a whole search.

The man who called himself Dan Cooper was never found. But this case isn't over. Something will turn up, years later, in a very surprising place.
