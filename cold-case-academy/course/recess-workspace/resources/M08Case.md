# CASE FILE 004

**Case name:** Two Princes, One Tower
**Place:** The Tower of London, England
**Date:** Spring and summer of 1483
**Missing:** Edward, age 12 · Richard, age 9
**Status:** UNSOLVED

---

## Welcome back, Detective

This is the oldest case in the academy, more than 540 years old. There are no fingerprints, no photographs, and no motion detectors. Almost everything we know comes from people who wrote things down, and some of them wrote decades later, for kings who wanted the story told a certain way. Your job in this file: notice **who** wrote each piece of evidence, **when**, and **why**.

---

## Part 1: A Boy King

In April 1483, King Edward IV of England died suddenly. His oldest son, also named Edward, was 12 years old. By law, the boy was now **King Edward V**.

A 12-year-old couldn't rule on his own, so someone had to protect the kingdom until he grew up. The old king had chosen his own brother, the boys' uncle: **Richard, Duke of Gloucester**.

---

## Part 2: Into the Tower

Uncle Richard met young Edward on the road to London and took charge of him. Several of the men traveling with the boy, including relatives of his mother, were arrested.

Edward was taken to the **Tower of London**. Today we think of the Tower as a prison, but in 1483 it was also a royal palace, and new kings often stayed there before their coronation.

A few weeks later, Edward's little brother, 9-year-old **Richard**, was brought to the Tower too, to keep his big brother company before the coronation.

The coronation never happened.

---

## Part 3: A New King

In June 1483, a churchman announced that the boys' parents had never been properly married, because their father had secretly promised to marry someone else first. If that was true, the boys couldn't inherit the crown.

A gathering of lords and leaders asked Richard to take the throne. On **July 6, 1483**, their uncle was crowned **King Richard III**.

**EXHIBIT A: The law that took away the crown**

In 1484, Parliament wrote the claim into law, in a document called the **Titulus Regius**. It doesn't say anything about where the boys were.

---

## Part 4: Seen Less and Less

**EXHIBIT B: An Italian visitor's report**

An Italian named **Dominic Mancini** was in London that spring and summer. He wrote a report before the end of 1483, so it's the only account written at the time.

Mancini wrote that the boys were moved further inside the Tower, and were seen "less and less" behind the windows, until one day they weren't seen at all. He wrote that people in London feared the boys were dead, but he admitted he didn't know what had happened.

After the summer of 1483, there are no confirmed sightings of the princes. Ever.

---

## Part 5: The Story Written Later

**EXHIBIT C: Thomas More's history**

About 30 years later, a writer named **Thomas More** wrote a history of Richard III. By then, a new royal family, the Tudors, ruled England, and the Tudors had taken the crown from Richard III in battle.

More wrote that Richard III had ordered the princes killed in the Tower, and named the men he said did it. It's the most famous version of the story. Shakespeare later turned it into a play.

But More was a small child in 1483. He never said where he got his information, and he was writing in a time when saying bad things about Richard III pleased the new kings.

---

## Part 6: Two Young Men Who Said "It's Me"

Years later, two young men appeared, each claiming to be a lost prince or royal heir. The most famous, **Perkin Warbeck**, claimed for years that he was young Richard, the younger prince, and that he had escaped. Some foreign rulers supported him.

He was captured in 1497 and confessed that he wasn't the prince at all. Most historians believe the confession, but people still argue about it.

---

## Part 7: The Box Under the Stairs

**EXHIBIT D: The 1674 discovery**

In **1674**, almost 200 years later, workmen at the Tower took apart an old staircase. Ten feet down, they found a wooden box holding the bones of two children.

People at the time believed these were the princes. King Charles II had the bones placed in a marble urn in **Westminster Abbey**, where they still are.

In **1933**, scientists examined the bones and said they belonged to two children of about the right ages. But they couldn't even tell whether the children were boys or girls, and they had no way to date the bones. Since then, nobody has been given permission to test them with modern science, like DNA.

---

## Part 8: The Suspects, and a New Claim

Historians have argued about this case for 500 years.

- **Richard III** is the suspect most historians favor. The boys were in his Tower, under his control, and they stood between him and the crown. He never showed them to the public to prove they were alive.
- **The Duke of Buckingham**, Richard's powerful ally, is named in a few old documents. But he couldn't easily have reached the boys without Richard knowing.
- **Henry VII**, the first Tudor king, took the crown in 1485. If the boys were still alive then, they would have been a danger to him too.

And a newer idea: in **2023**, and again in **2025**, a researcher named **Philippa Langley** published documents she says show that **both boys survived** and escaped to Europe. Many historians say the documents are interesting but don't prove it.

---

## Detective's Notes

- Ask of every source: **who** wrote it, **when**, and **who** were they writing for?
- A report written at the time is stronger than a story written 30 years later.
- Bones that have never been fully tested can't settle a case.
- Five hundred years later, "we can't tell yet" is still an honest answer.
