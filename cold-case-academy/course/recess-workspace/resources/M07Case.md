# CASE FILE 003 — PART 2

**Case name:** The Empty Frames
**Place:** Boston, and wherever 13 works of art are hiding
**Dates:** 1990 to today
**Missing:** 13 works of art, worth more than $500 million
**Reward:** $10,000,000
**Status:** UNSOLVED

---

## Welcome back, Detective

In Part 1, two men dressed as police officers walked out of the Gardner Museum with 13 works of art. In this file, you'll follow the hunt for those works across more than 35 years. Watch for two different questions. **Who did it?** And **where is the art now?** Investigators think they know more about the first one than the second.

---

## Part 1: The Empty Frames

Remember Isabella Stewart Gardner's will? Nothing in her museum can be moved, sold, or replaced.

So the museum did something unusual. It hung the **empty frames** back on the walls, right where the paintings used to be. Visitors today can stand in the Dutch Room and look at a frame with nothing inside it but the wallpaper.

The frames are a reminder, and a promise. They're waiting for the paintings to come home.

---

## Part 2: The Biggest Reward

**EXHIBIT C: The reward**

- **1990:** a few days after the theft, the museum offered $1 million.
- **1997:** raised to $5 million.
- **2017:** doubled to **$10 million**, for information that leads directly to getting the art back in good condition.

It's one of the biggest rewards ever offered by a private museum. There's also a separate $100,000 reward just for the bronze eagle. Nobody has claimed either one.

The FBI estimates the 13 works together are worth more than **$500 million**. Vermeer's *The Concert* alone may be the most valuable stolen painting that has never been found.

---

## Part 3: Messages and Glimpses

Over the years, people have claimed to know where the paintings are.

**EXHIBIT D: The 1994 letter.** An anonymous letter arrived at the museum. The writer said the art was being kept safely in another country, and asked for $2.6 million and a promise that nobody would be arrested. The museum answered with a secret coded message, printed in a Boston newspaper. Then the letter-writer went silent and never wrote again.

**EXHIBIT E: The 1997 warehouse.** A man with a criminal record took a newspaper reporter to a warehouse in Brooklyn, New York, late at night. By flashlight, he showed the reporter a rolled-up canvas that looked like Rembrandt's *Storm on the Sea of Galilee*. He gave the reporter paint chips. Experts said the chips were from Rembrandt's time, but they didn't match the *Storm*. When the FBI searched later, there was nothing there.

Neither lead brought back a single painting.

---

## Part 4: The Suspects

Investigators have looked at many people. None of them has ever been charged with the theft.

- **The night guard.** He opened the side door shortly before the thieves arrived, and the only footsteps recorded in the Blue Room that night were his. He always said he had nothing to do with it. He died in 2024.
- **Two men who fit the police sketches.** Witnesses outside the museum saw two men in police uniforms sitting in a car before the theft. Investigators have looked hard at men from Boston's criminal world who resembled those sketches. One of them died in 1991. Relatives later remembered a painting like the Manet hanging in his room.
- **A man in Connecticut.** For years, the FBI believed an older man in Connecticut knew where some of the art went. Agents dug up his backyard and searched his house. They found a hiding place under a shed. It was empty.

---

## Part 5: What the FBI Says

In **2013**, the FBI announced that it believed, "with a high degree of confidence," that it knew who the thieves were. The FBI said they belonged to a criminal group based in the mid-Atlantic states and New England, and that the art was taken to Connecticut and Philadelphia in the years after the theft, where someone tried to sell some of it around 2002.

In **2015**, the FBI said both thieves were dead.

But the FBI never named them in public. And knowing who took the art isn't the same as finding it.

---

## Part 6: Too Late to Punish

There's a time limit on charging someone with a crime like this. For the Gardner theft, that time ran out in **1995**. The thieves can't be put on trial for the robbery anymore.

Prosecutors have also said they would consider not charging someone who returns the art. The museum just wants the paintings back.

---

## Part 7: Still Waiting

In 2026, the FBI agent who led the case for more than 20 years published a book about it. In it, he names the men he believes were involved, and argues that the night guard could have been charged. The FBI itself has still never officially named anyone. The former agent believes the paintings still exist, and that they will turn up someday.

The museum agrees. Its head of security has pointed out that, in history, thieves almost never destroy famous art. They hide it, and sometimes it surfaces decades later.

Until then, 13 empty spaces wait on the walls.

---

## Detective's Notes

- "Who did it?" and "Where is it now?" are two different questions. Solving one doesn't solve the other.
- A claim that can't be checked (a letter, a glimpse by flashlight) isn't proof.
- Suspicion isn't evidence. None of the people investigators looked at was ever charged.
