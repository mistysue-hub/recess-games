# Module 12: Your Own Revolution

*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* has sold over a million copies and pushed the word "paradigm" into ordinary speech. Kuhn's framework started with a personal reversal: a summer afternoon in 1947 when Aristotle suddenly made sense to him. This last module turns the framework on you.

## Kuhn on yourself

Kuhn developed his ideas about whole scientific communities, and he was cautious about stretching them to individuals. Still, his own story began with a personal gestalt switch: the summer of 1947, when Aristotle's physics suddenly made sense. Use that as permission to test his framework on your own thinking.

Pick a belief you once held firmly and now don't, about a subject, a person, yourself, or how something works. Then ask:

- **Paradigm.** What did the old belief let you explain or predict? What questions did it make seem important?
- **Anomaly.** What didn't fit, and how long did you explain it away? Did you, like the Bruner and Postman subjects, see the anomaly as something normal at first?
- **Crisis.** Was there a period when the old view stopped working but you had nothing to replace it with?
- **Switch.** Did you change because of evidence, a new explanation, a person, or something else? Was it sudden, like a gestalt switch, or gradual?
- **Loss.** Did the new view lose anything the old one gave you, a Kuhn loss?

Then test the test: where does your story *not* fit Kuhn's pattern? An honest misfit is more useful than a forced match.

## The map

| Thinker | Core claim |
|---|---|
| Popper (1934; 1963) | Falsifiability marks science; progress comes from bold conjectures and severe tests; theories can gain verisimilitude. |
| Kuhn (1962) | Normal science within paradigms, punctuated by revolutions; progress "from," not "toward." |
| Lakatos (1970) | Research programmes are progressive or degenerating. |
| Feyerabend (1975) | No universal method; proliferation of rival ideas is healthy. |
| Longino (1990) | Objectivity is a property of communities that permit transformative criticism. |

The game asks you to place each on two axes:

- **Source of trust:** method and logic ← → community and history
- **Aim of science:** truth ← → successful prediction and problem-solving

Then place yourself.

## Every map is a framework

A two-axis map is a small paradigm. It makes comparison easy by deciding in advance which differences matter. Lakatos ends up in the middle of both axes, which may say more about the map than about him, and Feyerabend fits no box well. When a thinker resists placement, that is an anomaly for the map, not necessarily a flaw in the thinker.

## The next edition

Science changes its textbooks after revolutions. This course could change too. Using your Anomaly Log, write the first three changes you'd make to a second edition: what to add, what to cut, which story to tell less tidily.

Why it matters now: You'll change your mind about big things more than once. Kuhn's pattern gives you words for it: what didn't fit, how long you explained it away, and what finally made you switch.

**Weird but true:** Before 1962, "paradigm" was best known as a grammarian's term for a model example. Kuhn's book made it, and "paradigm shift," into everyday vocabulary, often used far more loosely than he meant. A word's meaning shifting after a book like this is itself a small Kuhnian event.

Side View: Philosophers of science today rarely accept any single thinker on this map whole. Many draw on several, accepting Kuhn's history, Popper's demand for testing, Lakatos's attention to programmes over time, and Longino's focus on communities. Suppose a surprising science claim shows up in the news tomorrow. Which thinker on the map would you trust most to help you decide whether to believe it, and why?
