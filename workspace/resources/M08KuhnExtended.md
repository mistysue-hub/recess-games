# Module 8: Talking Past Each Other

In March 1930, eleven-year-old Venetia Burney of Oxford suggested the name "Pluto" for the newly found ninth planet. Her grandfather passed it to an astronomer, who cabled it to Lowell Observatory. Seventy-six years later the same object was voted out of the category. Nothing about Pluto had changed.

## Same object, different word

Giuseppe Piazzi found Ceres on January 1, 1801. It, and soon Pallas, Juno and Vesta, were counted as planets for over half a century. As dozens of similar bodies turned up between Mars and Jupiter, astronomers gradually stopped calling them planets; by the 1860s "asteroid" was the standard term. Nothing about Ceres changed. The category did.

Clyde Tombaugh discovered Pluto in 1930. In 2005 Mike Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz announced Eris, slightly smaller than Pluto in diameter but about 27 percent more massive. Either Eris was the tenth planet, or "planet" needed rules. On August 24, 2006, the International Astronomical Union in Prague adopted a definition: a planet orbits the Sun, has enough mass to be nearly round, and has **cleared the neighborhood** around its orbit. Pluto fails the third criterion, so it became a **dwarf planet**, a category Eris and Ceres also fall into.

The decision remains contested. Alan Stern, who led the New Horizons mission to Pluto, argues the third criterion is incoherent: Neptune hasn't cleared Pluto from its zone either. Some planetary scientists prefer a "geophysical" definition based on a body's own properties, under which Pluto, Eris, Ceres and even large moons count as planets.

## Incommensurability

Kuhn's word for this situation was **incommensurability**, from a mathematical term for quantities with no common measure. His claim was that after a revolution, key terms change their meaning, so the old and new frameworks don't translate into each other cleanly. A key example was **mass**. In Newtonian physics, mass is conserved: you can't make or destroy it. In Einstein's physics, mass can be converted into energy. The word is the same; the concept isn't. A Newtonian and an Einsteinian can't simply compare answers, because they mean different things by the question.

Critics heard this as saying rival theories can't be compared at all, which would make theory choice arbitrary. Kuhn rejected that reading. Incommensurable frameworks, he said, can still be compared, just not by word-for-word translation. Scientists learn the other side's language, the way a bilingual person does, and judge using shared values. (Module 10 returns to those values.)

The Pluto debate is a small, friendly case. People on both sides agree on every measurement of Pluto. They disagree about what the word should do.

Why it matters now: Definitions decide real things. The planet vote rewrote textbooks, and the way scientists define a "species" affects which animals count as endangered and get protected.

**A complication:** The 2006 definition was not the only serious one. The planetary scientist Alan Stern has argued that planets should be defined by their own properties, such as being large enough to be round, not by what orbits nearby. By that standard Pluto and many other worlds would count. Both sides can agree on what Pluto is like and still disagree about what the word "planet" is for.

**Weird but true:** Venetia Burney's suggestion travelled from a breakfast-table conversation with her grandfather, Falconer Madan, to the astronomer Herbert Hall Turner, who cabled Lowell Observatory in Arizona. Names, like definitions, are decided by people.

## Terms

- **Incommensurability:** the absence of a common measure between two frameworks, so that their key terms don't translate directly.
- **Dwarf planet:** under the 2006 IAU definition, a nearly round body orbiting the Sun that has not cleared its neighborhood.
- **Constructive empiricism:** the view that science aims only at theories that are accurate about what can be observed.

Side View: **Scientific realists** hold that our best theories are approximately true, including about things we can't observe, like electrons. Hilary Putnam (1975) gave the "no miracles" argument: realism is the only view that doesn't make the success of science a miracle. Bas van Fraassen, in *The Scientific Image* (1980), defended **constructive empiricism**: science aims at theories that are "empirically adequate," true about observable things, and accepting a theory needn't mean believing everything it says about the unobservable. Kuhn's incommensurability sharpened this debate: if the meaning of "mass" changed, in what sense was Newton's mass ever "approximately" Einstein's? Back to Pluto: nothing measured about it changed in 2006, only the word "planet." Did what we know change, or only how we sort it?
