## Hook answer
No. It worked once and failed the second time: the unseen planet Neptune was found in 1846, but Vulcan, the planet blamed for Mercury's orbit, never turned up. The look-back: scientists usually try to fix the problem first and keep the theory. Sometimes that works (Neptune) and sometimes it doesn't (Vulcan). If they said "sometimes," that's right too.

## Talk-back (one at a time, after the look-back)
1. Uranus and Mercury both broke Newton's predictions. How was the fix for Uranus different from what finally explained Mercury?
   Answer: Uranus was fixed by finding a new planet, Neptune, and Newton's laws stayed. Mercury was explained by a new theory of gravity, Einstein's general relativity (1915); no new planet was found.
2. Was Le Verrier wrong to look for Vulcan?
   Opinion: no right answer. Point out that the same reasoning had just worked for Neptune.

## Side View question
Core: Say you've looked for your lost shoe in three places. When do you stop looking and decide someone moved it? When should scientists stop saving a theory?
Extended: Imagine you're an astronomer in 1880. Mercury's orbit is still off, and after twenty years of searching nobody has found Vulcan. How much longer do you keep searching before you suspect Newton?
Opinion question: no right answer. Stay neutral.

## Game: Save It or Scrap It
The learner reads real cases where an observation broke a prediction and picks what scientists should do: save the theory with something not yet seen, check the equipment, or change the theory. Then the game shows what really happened. Cases: Uranus (rescued: Neptune found 1846), Mercury (rescue failed, Vulcan never found; general relativity 1915), missing energy in radioactive decay (rescued: neutrino proposed 1930, found 1956), faster-than-light neutrinos (equipment: a loose fiber-optic cable, 2012). Extended adds Planet X (Pluto found in 1930 near a predicted spot but far too small; the problem disappeared after Voyager 2 measured Neptune's mass better in 1989 (shown in 1993), so the "rescue" was luck). The finish screen shows how many choices matched history. The last screen is "Notebook closed."
There is no wrong answer to the choice itself; the game reveals history. Never say the learner was wrong to pick a different option.

## Activity 2 (Core: only if the activity count is two; Extended: runs by default)
Core: Ask the learner to think of something that "should have worked" but didn't (a recipe, a game strategy, a gadget). Did they blame the plan, or one of the details? How did they decide?
Extended: Say: "For most of the twentieth century, doctors believed stomach ulcers came from stress, spicy food and too much acid, because bacteria were thought unable to live in stomach acid. In 1982 two Australian doctors, Robin Warren and Barry Marshall, grew a spiral-shaped bacterium, now called H. pylori, in the lab and soon linked it to ulcers. Many doctors doubted them. In 1984 Marshall drank a flask of the bacteria to make his point, got sick with stomach inflammation, and was treated. In 2005 the two shared a Nobel Prize." Ask: "Was it reasonable for doctors to doubt them at first? What evidence would have changed your mind?" If the learner wants to try something like it, say it was a risky stunt nobody should copy. Stay neutral.
