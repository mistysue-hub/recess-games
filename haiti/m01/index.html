LIBERTÉ OU LA MORT

Liberty or Death
Day IThe Richest ColonySaint-Domingue, 1789

In 1789 the western third of the island of Hispaniola was a French colony called Saint-Domingue. It was often called the richest colony in the world. Its plantations grew about 40 percent of the world's sugar and perhaps half of its coffee. There were some 730 sugar plantations, more than 3,000 coffee plantations, and about as many growing indigo, a blue dye.

All of that wealth came from forced labor. Around 1789 the colony held about 30,000 white colonists, about the same number of free people of color, and roughly 465,000 enslaved people, perhaps as many as 500,000. That is nearly eight enslaved people for every free person.

The work was so deadly that the enslaved population kept shrinking: more people died than were born. By some estimates, as many as one in three Africans died within their first few years on the island. To replace the dead and to expand, planters bought nearly 30,000 newly enslaved Africans a year in the late 1780s. Sugar and coffee profits paid French merchants, and the merchants paid for more slave ships. That loop is the machine you will test today.

Three Groups, Three Demands

  • White colonists were split between rich planters and merchants (the grands blancs, "big whites") and poorer whites (the petits blancs, "little whites"). Many wanted more say over how the colony was run. Almost none wanted equality for anyone else.
  • Free people of color included people who had been freed and people born free, many with both African and European ancestors. Some were well off and owned plantations and enslaved workers; many others were poor. Colonial rules still barred them from many jobs and public offices, and white colonists meant to keep them out of power.
  • Enslaved people did almost all the work. About half, perhaps as many as two-thirds, had been born in Africa. They resisted in many ways: slowing the work, breaking tools, and running away. People who escaped to the mountains and forests were called maroons, and some formed their own bands and networks. One maroon became a legend. Flip his card.
CAST CARD
François Makandal
died 1758 · Maroon leader of the North
Tap to see his choice, and what it cost.

WHO

Born in Africa (historians still debate where) and enslaved on a plantation near Limbé, not far from Cap-Français (today Cap-Haïtien). Accounts say he lost a hand or arm in a sugar mill.

THE CHOICE

He escaped (some accounts say around 1748) and lived as a maroon for years, perhaps ten or more. He became a leader, healer and diviner (someone people believed could see hidden things) whose network reached many plantations.

THE COST

In 1757–58 colonists accused him of leading a plot to poison planters. He was captured and executed by burning in Cap-Français in January 1758. Many enslaved people said he had escaped death.

THE DEBATE

Historians still argue about the poison plot. Some see a real conspiracy; a 2023 study by historian John Garrigus argues many deaths blamed on poison were really disease. Either way, colonists' fear of him shows how much they feared enslaved people organizing. In the late 1960s Haiti put an image of him on a coin.

The Rulebook

Since 1685 a royal law, later called the Code Noir ("Black Code"), had set the rules of slavery in France's colonies. It was registered in Saint-Domingue in 1687. As you read these articles, notice two things at once: the law calls people property, and it also treats them as people with souls, marriages and children.

Words to know before you read the rulebook

  • edict: an order from a king
  • baptized: made a member of the church in a ceremony
  • instructed: taught
  • Catholic, Apostolic and Roman: the Catholic Church
  • forbid: do not allow
  • expressly: clearly, on purpose
  • consent: permission
  • compulsion: force
  • heirs: people who inherit property after someone dies
  • free their slaves: release them from slavery (called manumission)
  • privileges: special rights
  • immunities: protections from certain taxes or punishments
  • mutilation: cutting off part of the body
  • branding: burning a mark into the skin
  • registered: officially recorded
EDICT OF THE KING · MARCH 1685 · SELECTED ARTICLES

Art. 2. All slaves who are in our islands shall be baptized and instructed in the Catholic, Apostolic and Roman religion.

Art. 11. We very expressly forbid priests to perform weddings for slaves unless their masters' consent is shown. We also forbid masters to use any compulsion on their slaves to marry them against their will.

Art. 12. Children born of marriages between slaves shall be slaves …

Art. 28. We declare that slaves can have nothing that does not belong to their master …

[section omitted] Article 38 set the punishments for people who escaped: mutilation and branding, and death for a third escape.

Art. 44. We declare slaves to be meubles …

Art. 55. Masters aged twenty may free their slaves … without being required to give a reason for it …

Art. 59. We grant to freed people the same rights, privileges and immunities enjoyed by persons born free …

French text: royal edict of March 1685 (public domain). English: translated for this course. "…" marks words left out.
Meubles means movable property: the same legal category as furniture or livestock, things that could be bought, sold and divided among heirs. (Article 47 set one limit: a husband, wife and children not yet in their teens, owned by the same master, were not to be sold apart.)

Article 59 promised a freed person the same rights as someone born free. By 1789 new colonial rules had taken away much of that promise. Free people of color wanted it kept, and one of them fought for it. Flip his card.

CAST CARD
Vincent Ogé
c. 1757 – 1791 · Merchant of Cap-Français
Tap to see his choice, and what it cost.

WHO

A prosperous free man of color, born in Dondon in the North. In Paris in 1789–90 he joined Julien Raimond and others asking France's new National Assembly (its parliament) for the rights of free people of color.

THE CHOICE

In March 1790 the Assembly set rules for who could vote in the colonies. The rules didn't mention race, so free men of color believed the rules included them. The colony's white leaders refused to let them vote. In October 1790 Ogé and about 300 armed free men of color rose in the hills about 12 miles from Cap-Français. He demanded the vote for free men of color. He did not call for an end to slavery.

THE COST

The revolt was crushed. He fled to the Spanish side of the island, was returned to the French, and was publicly executed in Cap-Français in February 1791. Six months later, enslaved people in the North rose up themselves.

That same year, 1789, the French Revolution's Declaration of the Rights of Man said that men are born free and equal in rights. Everyone in Saint-Domingue read that sentence differently. Tomorrow you'll see how.
