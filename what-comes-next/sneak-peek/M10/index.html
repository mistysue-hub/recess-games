Now showing: a plot twist

Here's a twist most people don't see coming: you don't have to wait until after graduation to take college classes. Lots of high schoolers take real college classes right now, while they're still homeschooling. It's called dual credit, and lots of Recess students have already done it. (Rocky has asked them for their reviews. Five stars, mostly. One student said the campus vending machine was "a lot.")

This isn't a decision for today. It's a preview, so that when the time comes, you know the door is there.

2

What is dual credit?

Dual credit means one class counts two times:

  1. As college credit, on a real college transcript.
  2. As high school credit, on the homeschool transcript your parents keep.

You might hear other names for the same idea: dual enrollment, concurrent enrollment or early college. Most dual-credit classes come from a community college, in person or online.

3

Why students love it

  • It can save a lot of money. In many states, the state or the college pays some or all of the tuition for high schoolers. Every class you finish now is one you may not pay full price for later.
  • It can save time. Some students finish a semester or a year of college early. A few finish a whole two-year associate degree by graduation!
  • It's proof. A college grade shows colleges, programs and employers that you can do college-level work.
  • It's a test drive. You find out what college feels like, with your family still right there to cheer you on.
  • It's for every lane. Community colleges teach welding, nursing basics, coding, business and much more, not just English and math. A trade-school student can get a head start too.
4

The fine print (every good trailer has some)

  • Your grades stick. Dual-credit grades start a real college transcript that follows you. That's great when they're good. So start small: one class, not five.
  • Pick classes that travel well. Basic classes like English composition, college algebra, intro psychology or U.S. history usually transfer (count) at the most places. Before you sign up, check that a college you might want accepts it. (The main course calls this the golden rule.)
  • Every state and college has its own rules. Some programs welcome homeschoolers; some don't yet. Many have a minimum age or grade, and many ask for a placement test (a short test that shows which class fits you) and a transcript from your parents.
  • Sign-ups happen early. Colleges often want applications months before classes start. Fall classes might need paperwork in spring.
  • You'll be in class with adults. Class discussions can get grown-up, and professors expect you to speak up for yourself. The college will usually talk to you, not your parents, about your grades.
5

When do people start?

Many students take their first class around ages 15 to 17, often the year after Sneak Peek. Your job right now is just to find out what's possible where you live, so you can make a plan with your family.

6

🏡 Homeschool Edge

You already know how to learn on your own, manage your time and talk to adults. Those are exactly the skills that make dual-credit students shine. And your parents can fit college classes right into your homeschool plan.

7

👪 Ask Your Parents

  2. "If I wanted to try one class, what would make sense, and when?"
