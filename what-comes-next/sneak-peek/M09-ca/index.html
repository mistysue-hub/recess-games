Now showing: the behind-the-scenes extras

You know the bonus features after a movie? The bloopers, the "making of," the stuff nobody notices but everybody's glad exists? This module is that. Small habits you start now that make life much easier in a couple of years.

None of this is required. All of it is a head start. (Rocky promised not to say "calendar" more than three times today. Counting starts now.)

2

Habit 1: Keep a running list

Start a running list of what you learn and do:

  • Courses (math, history, that co-op biology class)
  • Books you read, for school or for fun
  • Projects (the birdhouse, the short film, the coding game)
  • Volunteer hours (how many, where, and what you did)

Start it in today's Field Notes, then keep adding to it with a parent. Why bother? Because in a couple of years, your parents will build your homeschool transcript, and you might fill out applications for college, university, a trade program, a job or a scholarship. Trying to remember everything from three years ago is hard. A running list is future transcript gold.

Here's a Canadian bonus: many universities ask homeschoolers for extra documents, like course descriptions, a list of the books and textbooks you used, or a portfolio of your work. McGill, for example, asks for a list of all the texts you studied, including editions. Your running list makes that easy.

3

Habit 2: Early tests? (Canada edition)

Good news: Canada doesn't have a PSAT-style early test, and you don't need one. Most Canadian universities admit students on their grade 11 and 12 marks.

Homeschoolers are the exception. Because your parents write your transcript, some universities ask for outside proof of what you know, such as SAT or ACT scores, AP exams, or courses from an accredited school. That's a grade 11–12 thing, not a now thing. If a parent thinks you'll need it, trying a free SAT or ACT practice test around grade 10 is plenty.

Rules and test dates change, so check the official site with a parent when the time comes. If there's a sign-up date that matters, Rocky will flag it. (Calendar mention #1.)

4

Habit 3: Know that early credit exists

There are a few ways to earn credits early. They're optional, and most students try them later in high school:

  • AP exams: you take them at a host school that offers AP, and many Canadian universities give credit for high scores.
  • Provincial high-school courses: these can give you an official mark from outside your home. Ontario's TVO Independent Learning Centre takes students 14 and older (it also posts a notice for homeschool students, so check it). BC families can look at Distributed Learning schools, and Alberta families at courses through a supervising school board.
  • University courses: some open universities let teens take a real course. That gets its own module: head to M10 for the full trailer.

Here's the golden rule from the main course: check what schools accept before spending time or money. Every college and university decides which courses and scores it accepts. A credit your future school won't take is just a fancy piece of paper. (One more tip: US credit-by-exam tests like CLEP and DSST are rarely used in Canada, so ask before paying.)

5

Habit 4: Earn a little, save a little

Earning: lots of teens start with jobs they make themselves: babysitting, pet sitting, shovelling snow, mowing lawns, tutoring younger kids, or selling things they make.

Working for a boss? In Canada, your province or territory sets the rules, and they're quite different:

  • Ontario: 14 for jobs like stores, offices and restaurant serving areas; 15 for factories and restaurant kitchens; 16 for construction.
  • British Columbia: 14- and 15-year-olds can do "light work" (like stocking shelves or cashiering) with a parent's written permission, with limits on hours. At 16, the regular rules apply.
  • Alberta: 13- and 14-year-olds can do certain listed jobs (like retail clerk or flyer delivery) with a parent's consent and short hours on school days. From 15, most jobs are open.
  • Quebec: the minimum is generally 14, with exceptions and limits on hours during school weeks.

Check your own province's official page with a parent. Most provinces don't use a "work permit" form, but you'll need a Social Insurance Number (SIN), which is free, and you can apply for yourself at 12 or older. A parent sets up any job with an adult outside your family, and a real job never asks you to pay to start.

Saving: whenever money comes in (a paycheque, a birthday card, a snow-shovelling gig), save a small part of it. A jar works. A youth savings account a parent helps you open works too. You could also ask your parents whether you have an RESP (a savings plan for school after high school). The habit matters more than the amount. Future You, who might want a tool kit, a laptop, a class or a plane ticket, says thanks.

6

Paths that start early

Most paths don't need planning yet. A few do. If you're even a little curious about any of these, say so in your Field Notes:

  • ROTP: the Canadian Armed Forces' plan where you get a university degree (at the Royal Military College or a civilian university), get paid while you study, and then serve.
  • Big national scholarships, like the Loran Award, which closes in the fall of grade 12.
  • Playing a sport after high school, in Canada or the US.

These paths have early timelines. ROTP applications start around September of your final year, and strong marks in math and science help, so keep them strong now. For Canadian university or college teams, athletes often start talking with coaches in grades 11 and 12. If you might play at an NCAA Division I or II school in the US, the courses you take from grade 9 on can count, so tell a parent now and they can keep the kind of course records the NCAA asks homeschoolers for. Being curious now doesn't commit you to anything. It just means no surprises later. (Calendar mention #2. Rocky is doing great.)

7

Extra support? Keep the records

If you get extra time on tests or other help with learning, ask your parents about keeping records of it, like evaluations or notes about what helps you. Later, those records can make it easier to ask a college or university's accessibility services for accommodations, like extra time on exams. This is only if it applies to you, and it's nothing to worry about. Lots of people learn in lots of ways.

8

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Your parents already track your learning. A running list just makes it easy to share. And because homeschoolers often learn through projects, trips and real-world work, your list might be more interesting than you expect. Ask your Recess friends and guides how they keep track. Someone always has a clever system.

🌍 Heading south someday?

If you might study in the US, the US version of this module covers early tests like the PSAT and the US credit-by-exam options. The big one to know now: NCAA sports eligibility can start counting in grade 9. Everything else can wait.
