You might already be a business owner

Ever run a lemonade stand? Mowed a neighbor's lawn for cash? Sold bracelets, cookies or digital art? Congratulations: you've already been self-employed. You were the CEO, the head of sales, the chief lemon squeezer and the person who cleaned up the sticky table.

Some people love that feeling so much they build a whole career around it. That's the 🚀 work-for-yourself flag, and remember: you can fly it on any lane. A college student who freelances. An electrician who opens their own company. A baker who someday opens a bakery.

This module is an honest, exciting look at being your own boss.

2

Is it for you? The honest version

The awesome parts:

  • You choose your work, your schedule and your customers.
  • You build something that's yours.
  • Your earnings can grow with your effort and ideas.

The tricky parts:

  • Income can go up and down. One month is great; the next is quiet.
  • You do every job: making, selling, bookkeeping, customer service, and fixing the printer. (There is always a printer.)
  • Nobody tells you when to start work. That's freedom and a challenge.
  • No employer-provided health insurance or paid vacation unless you plan for it.

Neither list is "good" or "bad." The question is: does the awesome list excite you enough to handle the tricky list?

3

Kinds of self-employment

  • 🛠️ Service businesses: tutoring, lawn care, pet sitting, cleaning, photography, music lessons
  • 🎨 Making and selling: crafts, baked goods, art, clothing, 3D prints
  • 💻 Freelancing: writing, graphic design, video editing, web design (more in M12)
  • 🔧 Trades contractors: many electricians, plumbers and carpenters open their own shop once they're licensed
  • 🌐 Online businesses: digital products, online stores, content creation
4

From idea to first customer

Here's the biggest secret of smart business owners: test your idea cheaply before you spend real money.

Meet Maya. (Made up, but this is exactly how it should go.) Maya wanted to start a dog-walking business. Instead of buying fancy leashes and printing 500 flyers, she asked ten neighbors with dogs, "Would you pay someone to walk your dog after school? How much?" Four said yes. Two said, "Can you start Monday?" She spent $0 and learned she had real customers. Then she bought one good leash.

That's how to test an idea:

  1. Talk to real possible customers. Not your grandma, who will say yes to anything you make. (Thanks, Grandma.)
  2. Try a small pilot. Sell ten, not a thousand. Walk two dogs, not twenty.
  3. Take pre-orders if you can, so customers commit before you spend.

Then write a one-page business plan: what you sell, who buys it, how they'll find you, what it costs, and what you'll charge.

5

Pricing: the part everyone gets wrong

Price = your costs + your time + the value to the customer.

Here's a made-up example for homemade cupcakes:

What it costs to make one Amount
Ingredients $0.80
Cupcake liner and box $0.30
Your time (about 6 minutes per cupcake, valued at $15 an hour) $1.50
True cost $2.60

If you charge $2, you're losing money every time someone says "Yum!" Charge $3.50 and you make a profit. Check what similar sellers charge, too. Too cheap can make people think your work isn't good.

6

Money basics

  • Keep business money separate from your personal money. (A parent can help with a separate account; see below.)
  • Track every dollar in and out. A simple spreadsheet works.
  • Taxes: if you earn $400 or more in net profit in a year, you generally owe self-employment tax, the Social Security and Medicare taxes that an employer would normally help pay. You also generally need to file a tax return, even as a teen. The rate is about 15.3%, figured on most of your net profit. Many business owners set aside a chunk of every payment for taxes so the bill isn't a surprise. Ask your parents to help, and check irs.gov.
  • Form 1099: a client who pays you enough in a year may send you a Form 1099-NEC (for payments made in 2026, the usual trigger rose from $600 to $2,000; check irs.gov for the current number). Either way, you owe tax on all your profit, even if nobody sends you a form.
  • Quarterly taxes: once you're earning steadily, you may need to pay estimated taxes four times a year instead of all at once. Check irs.gov to see when that applies.
7

Legal basics

  • Your business type. The moment you start selling on your own, you're a sole proprietorship. That's automatic, with no paperwork needed. An LLC (limited liability company) can protect your personal stuff if the business gets sued, but it costs money and paperwork, so most people wait until the business is bigger.
  • EIN. If you ever need an EIN (a tax ID number for a business), it's free at irs.gov. Never pay a website for one.
  • Some businesses need to register or get a license or permit. Rules depend on your city, county and state, and on what you sell (food has extra rules). Look up your state's cottage food law if you'll sell homemade food, and ask about a sales tax permit if you'll sell products. Your city or county clerk, your state revenue department and SBA.gov's "Apply for licenses and permits" page are good places to start.
  • Minors usually need a parent to sign contracts, open business bank accounts, or use payment and selling platforms. Many platforms require users to be 18 or to go through a parent's account. 👪 Ask Your Parents.
  • Protect your work. You automatically own the copyright to the art, writing, music and designs you create. The flip side: don't sell things using someone else's characters, logos, music or fonts unless you have the rights. Fonts and stock images come with licenses, so check whether "free" means free to sell.
  • Insurance. 👪 Ask your parents whether the family's insurance covers business activities (like pet sitting or childcare), or price out simple business insurance. If a dog chews a client's couch on your watch, you want a plan.
8

Getting customers

  • Word of mouth (your People Map from M08 is your first customer list!)
  • A simple website or social page
  • Amazing service, which leads to honest reviews. Always real ones. Never ask friends to pose as customers, and never write fake reviews. That's dishonest, and it's often illegal.
9

🚩 Is it a business or a trap?

Watch out for multi-level marketing (MLM) "opportunities." Warning signs: you have to buy a starter kit or inventory first, and the money comes mostly from recruiting other people rather than selling to real customers. Most people who join lose money. If something seems like a scam, tell your parents and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

10

Free help from real experts

  • SCORE (score.org): thousands of volunteer business mentors, many of them retired business owners. Mentoring is free. Involve a parent when setting it up.
  • Small Business Development Centers: free advice and low-cost training; find one through sba.gov.
  • SBA (sba.gov): the US Small Business Administration's free guides on planning, registering and funding a business.
11

The hybrid path

Most business owners don't start by quitting everything. They start on the side, while studying, training or working, and grow it slowly. That's not "playing it safe." That's playing it smart.

12

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Lots of homeschoolers already run small businesses, and that's real experience. It can go in your Experience Log (M09), on your résumé, and possibly on your homeschool transcript as a course or project. (Ask your parents.) Your flexible schedule also means you can serve customers on weekdays when your competition is in class.

13

🚀 In every lane

🎓 College

Many colleges have entrepreneurship programs, business competitions and startup clubs. Some students launch businesses from their dorm rooms.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Many trades workers open their own shop after earning their license and years of experience. Your apprenticeship is your business school.

The usual career ladder: apprentice → journeyworker → master → licensed contractor (the one who can run the business). There are side branches too, like foreman (leading a crew), estimator (pricing jobs), inspector (checking work meets code) and instructor (training the next apprentices). Exact titles and licenses vary by trade and state.

💼 Career

Working for a company first teaches you how a business runs, on someone else's dime. Lots of owners did exactly that.

15

🍁 In Canada?

Self-employed Canadians pay both halves of CPP plus income tax when they file with the CRA. You generally only have to charge GST/HST once your sales pass about $30,000 over four quarters in a row. A Business Number is free from the CRA (never pay a website for one), and business registration and licences are handled by your province and city. For free help, look at Futurpreneur, BDC's guides and your provincial small-business centre.

  3. "Do you know any business owners I could ask about how they started?"
