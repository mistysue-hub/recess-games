Build it yourself

Websites, apps, video games, digital art, animations: some of the most exciting work in the world can be done with a laptop, an internet connection and a lot of determination. People build careers making games alone in their bedrooms, freelancing as web developers for clients across the world, or selling their own apps.

It's real. It's also harder than social media makes it look. This module gives you the honest map: the skills, the free tools, the habits that make or break solo tech careers, and the business side nobody tells you about.

2

The skills you need

Going solo in tech takes three kinds of skills:

  1. Tech fundamentals: programming, design, and problem-solving. (Mostly, problem-solving. A developer's day is about 10% writing code and 90% figuring out why the code doesn't work.)
  2. Business skills from M11: pricing, finding customers, tracking money, taxes.
  3. People skills from M08: emails, video calls, explaining your work to people who aren't techy.
3

🌐 The web development trail

Here's a common, free path that many self-taught developers follow:

  1. freeCodeCamp (freecodecamp.org): a nonprofit with a complete, free, project-based curriculum. A great first stop for total beginners.
  2. The Odin Project (theodinproject.com): a free, open-source curriculum that has you set up real developer tools from the start. Steeper, but it builds a stronger portfolio.
  3. MDN Web Docs (developer.mozilla.org): the reference real developers keep open all day. You'll never memorize it all. Nobody does.
  4. GitHub (github.com): where you save your code, track every change, and show off your projects. Push every project there from day one.

💡 Homeschool bonus: GitHub's free Student Developer Pack is open to students 13 and older in a diploma-granting program, and GitHub's own rules list homeschool as eligible. You'll need proof of enrollment, like a transcript or an enrollment letter. If GitHub asks for a school email, a parent can write a letter saying your homeschool doesn't issue one. (Your homeschool paper trail from M20 helps here.)

4

🎮 The game development trail

  • Godot (godotengine.org): a free, open-source engine for 2D and 3D games. No account needed, and widely recommended for beginners.
  • Unity and Unreal Engine: the big professional engines. Check each one's current license terms and fees before you commit.
  • Ren'Py: a free engine for visual novels and story games.
  • itch.io: a place to publish indie games and the home of hundreds of game jams, including beginner-friendly ones. A game jam is a challenge to make a whole game in a set time (often a weekend or a week), usually around a theme. It's the fastest way to finish something.
5

🤖 AI tools: power tools, not magic wands

AI coding and design tools are now everyday tools for developers and game makers. Use them to learn faster, explain confusing errors, and speed up boring parts. But understand everything you ship. Clients, employers and players still expect real skills, and if AI wrote code you can't explain, you can't fix it when it breaks. (It will break. Usually on a Friday night.)

AI-generated art and code can also come with licensing questions: who owns it, and can you sell it? Check the tool's terms and any contest or platform rules.

6

The habits that make or break solo careers

Talent matters less than you think. Habits matter more. Here's what successful solo creators do:

  • Practice on a schedule. Thirty minutes every day beats a 10-hour binge once a month.
  • Finish small projects. One tiny finished game beats ten giant unfinished ones. Your first project should be embarrassingly small.
  • Escape "tutorial land." Tutorials feel productive, but at some point you have to build your own thing without step-by-step instructions. That's where real learning happens.
  • Commit your code and back up everything. Every day. Losing a month of work to a dead hard drive is a rite of passage you can skip.
  • Read the documentation and debug patiently. Errors aren't failure. They're the job.
  • Share your work and ask for feedback. Scary, but it's how you get better.
  • Join communities and jams. Other creators are your coworkers when you work alone.
  • Track your time. Especially once you're charging clients.
  • Take care of your body. Breaks, sleep, stretching, and looking at something farther away than your screen now and then.
7

The business side of going solo

  • A portfolio site showing your best three to five projects, with what you did on each.
  • First clients or players: your People Map (M08), local businesses and nonprofits that need a website, and game communities.
  • Contracts and "scope creep": a contract says exactly what you'll make, by when, for how much. Without one, clients tend to say "Oh, and can you also just quickly..." until your simple website has become a social network. Get it in writing.
  • Pricing: costs + time + value, just like M11.
  • Getting paid: many payment platforms require users to be 18, so you may need a parent to help. 👪 Ask Your Parents.
  • Taxes: the self-employment tax rules from M11 apply to you, too.
8

The honest reality

Solo tech income usually takes years to build. Most people pair it with a job, school or training while their skills, portfolio and client list grow. That's normal, and it's smart.

There are also structured routes: college computer science or design programs, community college courses, IT apprenticeships, and coding bootcamps. Before paying for any bootcamp, check its real outcomes: how many students finished, and how many got jobs? (Hello again, Source Detective.)

9

🏡 Homeschool Edge

You already know how to teach yourself. That's the number one skill in tech, where tools change constantly and nobody can learn everything in a classroom. Your flexible schedule also gives you time for long, focused building sessions most students only get in summer.

10

🛡️ Staying safe online

Online communities are great, but keep personal information private (your full name, address, school details and phone number). Never send money to someone you met online, and tell your parents if anyone makes you uncomfortable. Get a parent's okay before joining a jam or community.

11

Tech in every lane

🎓 College

Computer science, game design, graphic design and digital media programs, plus clubs, hackathons and research.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

IT apprenticeships and industry certifications in networking, cybersecurity and tech support.

💼 Career

Tech jobs at companies of every kind: every bakery, bank and hospital needs tech people.

🔭 Lane Peek

Pick a lane you weren't considering and imagine how tech skills could help there. A welder who builds a website for their shop? A nurse who designs a health app? Jot down your idea.

👪 Ask Your Parents

Ask your parents:

  1. "Can I set a regular practice time for tech projects?"
  2. "Is it okay if I join a beginner game jam or coding community?"
  3. "Could you help me get proof of homeschool enrollment for GitHub's Student Developer Pack?"
