You'll do more than learn there

In M16 you asked, "Will I learn well there?" Today's question is just as important: "Will I feel at home there?"

You'll spend a lot of time wherever you land next: a campus, a training center, a shop floor, an office or a base. If you feel like you belong, it's easier to make friends, ask for help, stay through the hard weeks and actually enjoy it. Students who feel at home are more likely to stick around and finish.

So today you'll add Social Fit to your Fit Scorecard.

2

The Social Fit clues

Size and setting. Big city, suburb, small town or the middle of the countryside? Two hours from home or two time zones away? Thousands of people or a few hundred? Think about your M05 sliders: small group or big crowd, quiet or busy.

Living there (or not). Will you live on campus, at home, or somewhere else? Residential schools have most students living on or near campus. Commuter schools have most students living at home and driving in. Both can be great. They just feel very different on a Friday night.

If you'll live in a dorm, look at first-year housing, how roommates are matched, and living-learning communities, where students with a shared interest live on the same floor.

Culture and values. Every place has a personality. Is it competitive or collaborative? How does it handle faith life, politics and diversity? Do people seem to help each other? There's no right answer, only your answer.

Things to do. Clubs, teams, music, faith groups, volunteering, intramural sports, maker spaces, gaming nights. Look for at least two or three activities that match your interests. They're the fastest way to find your people.

Safety. Every college that receives federal student aid publishes a yearly Annual Security Report, thanks to a law called the Clery Act (search the school's name plus "Annual Security Report"; College Navigator's Campus Security section shows the numbers too). Look it up, and ask what the school does to keep students safe.

Help with the big change. Look for orientation programs, first-year seminars, peer mentors and counseling services. The best schools make it easy to settle in.

3

The homeschool angle

Moving from learning at home to a campus or workplace is a big, exciting change. Here are the parts to plan for:

  • Big groups and fixed schedules. A 200-person lecture at 8:00 a.m. feels very different from your kitchen table. Many students adjust in a few weeks, especially with a plan (you'll build one in M26).
  • Finding your people fast. Join one or two activities in your first two weeks, before everyone settles into groups. Some schools even have homeschool student groups.
  • One great clue: the first-year retention rate from M16. When most first-year students come back, it usually means they felt at home.
4

How to really find out

Websites and brochures show the sunniest day of the year. Here's how to see the real thing (with a parent's okay for visits and conversations):

  • Visit, and walk around on your own for a while, not just on the tour.
  • Stay overnight if the school offers it.
  • Sit in on a class.
  • Talk to current students. Your People Map (M08) might already know some.
  • Take virtual tours for faraway places.
  • Read the student newspaper. It shows what students actually care about (and complain about).
  • Read reviews like a detective. One loud review isn't the whole story. Look for patterns that several people mention.
5

Social Fit in every lane

🎓 College

Campus visits, overnight stays and admitted-student days. Ask students: "What do people do on weekends?" and "What surprised you about this place?"

Demonstrated interest: some colleges keep track of how much you seem to care, like visits, info sessions and even whether you open their emails. Others say they don't track it at all, so ask. If a school offers an interview, treat it like a friendly chat, not a quiz: start with your 30-second pitch (you'll build it in M21) and bring two questions of your own.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Go to info sessions and shop tours. Ask current students and apprentices about the crew culture: Do people help newcomers? How do instructors and supervisors treat beginners?

Programs that open the door: many areas have pre-apprenticeship programs and union outreach that help newcomers get ready, including groups just for women in the trades and programs for other people who are underrepresented on job sites. Apprenticeship.gov, your local union halls and your state's apprenticeship office can point you to ones nearby.

💼 Career

Job shadows and visits show you a team's real culture. Notice how coworkers talk to each other. (Your M10 skills help here.) For the military, talk to people who serve in the jobs and branches you're curious about, not only recruiters.

🚀 Work for yourself

Working alone can get lonely. Look for coworking spaces, local business meetups and online communities where you can find your people.

🌍 Crossing borders

Living in a new country brings culture shock, even when it's exciting. Check whether the school has an international student office, and look for student groups that can help you settle in.

🍁 In Canada?

Canadian schools don't publish US-style campus safety reports, but many provinces (including Ontario, BC and Quebec) require colleges and universities to have a sexual-violence policy and support office, so ask about it on your visit. For trades, colleges, unions and your provincial apprenticeship office run pre-apprenticeship and women-in-trades programs.

👪 Ask Your Parents

  1. "Which places could we visit together, and when?"
  2. "How far from home feels okay to you? How about to me?"
  3. "What do you think I'll need to feel at home somewhere new?"
