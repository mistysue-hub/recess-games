1

Networking is just making friends with a purpose

"Networking" sounds like something done by people in suits, holding tiny plates of cheese, saying "Let's circle back" to each other. Relax. That's not what we're doing.

Networking is making friends with a purpose. You meet people, you're curious about what they do, you're kind and helpful, and you stay in touch. That's it. No fake smiles, no pushy sales pitch, no tiny cheese required.

Why does it matter so much? Because in every lane, lots of opportunities travel through people. Jobs, apprenticeships, internships, first customers, recommendation letters, a heads-up that a program is accepting applications: many of them come from someone who knows you and thought, "Oh! You should talk to my friend." Employers love hiring people someone they trust can vouch for, and some jobs get filled that way before they're ever posted online.

That's why this module can't be skipped. Whatever road you take, your people help you find it.

2

Your network already exists

Good news: you're not starting from zero. You already know more people than you think. Your network includes:

  • Your Recess crew: friends, guides and mentors who already want you to succeed
  • Family and family friends (and the friends of those friends)
  • Co-op, church, sports, clubs and community groups
  • Coaches, tutors and online teachers
  • Neighbors (the one who's always fixing a car in the driveway counts)
  • Anyone you've worked or volunteered with
  • Online communities where you share your interests

Each of these people knows other people. That's the magic: your network is much bigger than the people you know personally.

Here's how it can play out. (Jordan is made up, but this kind of chain happens all the time.) Jordan loves animals and mentions it to her Recess mentor. The mentor says, "My sister's best friend is a vet tech. Want me to ask if she'd talk to you?" One video call later (with Jordan's mom in the room), the vet tech invites Jordan to shadow for a morning. Six months later, the clinic needs a part-time kennel assistant, and guess whose name comes up first?

Nobody in that story was "networking." They were just being friendly, curious and helpful. Three links, one job. Not bad for a conversation that started with "I really like dogs."

Your People Map

Today you'll build a People Map with Rocky. It's a simple list in three rings:

  1. Inner ring: people you know well and could message today.
  2. Middle ring: people you know a little (a friend's parent, a coach from two years ago).
  3. Outer ring: people you'd like to know, like someone who does a career on your "ooh, maybe" list. You may not know them yet, but someone in your inner ring might.

4

The informational interview

An informational interview is a short conversation (often 15 to 20 minutes) where you ask someone about their work. You are not asking for a job. You're asking for their story. Most people love being asked about their work, because almost nobody ever asks.

How to ask: a short, friendly message. Say who you are, how you found them, what you're curious about, and ask for a little of their time. Rocky will help you draft it, but the words should sound like you.

Good questions to ask:

  • "What does a normal day look like for you?"
  • "How did you get into this work?"
  • "What do you enjoy most? What's hardest?"
  • "What would you do if you were my age and starting out?"
  • "What's typical starting pay in this field?" (This is fine. Asking about their salary is not.)
  • The golden question: "Who else do you think I should talk to?"

What not to do: don't ask for a job, don't ask what they personally earn, and don't go over the time you asked for. And don't forget a thank-you message within a day. It's the easiest way to be remembered well.

5

Keeping relationships alive

Meeting someone once is nice. Staying in touch is what turns a contact into a mentor. Every few months, send a real update: "I took your advice and tried a welding class. I loved it!" Share something useful. Celebrate their wins. Offer help back when you can.

And if it's been a long time? Reach out anyway. "Hi! It's been a while, but I've been thinking about our conversation..." works great. Nobody is keeping score.

Quality beats quantity. If ten conversations give you one or two real mentors, that's a huge win.

6

Sounding like a pro

A few skills make you sound confident and capable at any age:

  • Professional email: a clear subject line, a greeting ("Hi Ms. Lopez,"), a short message, a polite sign-off, and your name.
  • Phone calls: yes, actual phone calls. Say who you are and why you're calling right away. Have your question written down. Smile while you talk. (It really does change your voice.)

  • Voicemail: your name, your reason, your number said slowly, and your number again.

    • ❌ "Um, hi, it's me? Um... call me back... okay bye." (Who is "me"? Nobody knows. The mystery will never be solved.)
    • ✅ "Hi, this is Sam Rivera. I'm a homeschooled student interested in carpentry, and I'd love to ask you a few questions about your work. My number is 555-0142. Again, 555-0142. Thanks so much!"

  • Video calls: good light in front of you, camera at eye level, mute when you're not talking, and a quick check of what's behind you.

  • Replying: answer messages within a day or two.

Rocky can run a practice game called The Phone Call (a horror story in three rings). It's not actually scary. Mostly.

7

Your online presence

Before you reach out, imagine the person searches your name. What would they find? Check what's public on your accounts, and tidy up anything you wouldn't want a future boss to see. Later, a simple professional profile (like LinkedIn, once you're old enough to use it) or a portfolio page can show off your work.

8

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Many homeschoolers are used to talking with adults of all ages: at co-op, at church, at work, at the library desk. That's a real advantage in networking. And your flexible schedule means you can do informational interviews and visits on weekdays, when most working adults are actually available.

Your people in every lane

🎓 College

Professors, admissions reps, current students and alumni can tell you what a school or major is really like.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Union training centers, trade associations, local contractors and current apprentices know where the openings are.

💼 Career

Local business groups, industry events, recruiters and people already in the job you want.

🚀 Work for yourself: early customers, fellow creators, and online maker and developer communities.

🌍 From far away: online communities, video calls, and international student groups help you build a network before you arrive.

10

🔭 Lane Peek

Look at your People Map and find one person who works in a lane you're not leaning toward. Ask them one question about their work. You might learn something surprising, or meet the perfect contact for a friend.

11

👪 Ask Your Parents

Two important things for your parents:

  1. Safety first: before you meet, call or video chat with any adult outside your family, tell your parents who it is and when. Meet in public places or on video calls, with a parent involved.
  2. Ask them: "Who do you know who works in [a career on my list]? Would you introduce me?"

Your parents' friends, coworkers and old classmates are a huge part of your network. And ask your Recess guides and mentors the same question.
