The money map

Money can feel like the scariest part of what comes next. Here's the most important thing to know: the price tag is almost never the price you pay. There's help in every lane, and today you'll learn where to find it.

Back in M13 you looked at what each path costs. Now you'll find the money to pay for it. (Rocky has a spreadsheet for this. Of course he does. It has seven tabs and a color key.)

2

Four kinds of money

Every money plan mixes these:

  1. Free money: grants and scholarships. You don't pay them back.
  2. Earned money: work-study, part-time jobs, and apprenticeship wages.
  3. Borrowed money: loans. You pay them back later, with interest. Federal student loans usually have better terms than private loans. Parents can borrow too (Parent PLUS loans), and federal loan limits changed in 2026, so check studentaid.gov for the current rules.
  4. Family and savings: whatever your family plans to contribute (a conversation for your parents, not a test).

The goal is simple: use free and earned money first, and borrow carefully.

3

Sticker price vs. what you actually pay

Remember M16? The sticker price is the published cost. The net price is what's left after free money. A school with a scary sticker price can end up cheaper than one that looks affordable, so never cross a place off your list until you see the real number.

Money Fit: for each place on your list, give it a score from 1 to 5 for how well its real cost fits what your family has talked about. It goes in the last column of your Fit Scorecard, next to Academic Fit (M16) and Social Fit (M17).

4

The FAFSA: the key that unlocks aid

The FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is how you apply for federal grants (like the Pell Grant), work-study and federal student loans. Many states, colleges and some trade programs use it for their own aid too.

  • It's free at studentaid.gov. If a site charges you to file, it's not the real one.
  • You'll create a StudentAid.gov account first, and so will each parent the FAFSA asks to help (often just one parent, plus a stepparent if that parent is married). If your family situation is complicated (divorced or separated parents, a parent without a Social Security number, a parent who can't take part), studentaid.gov explains who counts as your parent, and each college's financial aid office can help.
  • Each person on the form (you and any parent it asks for) is a contributor, and each one must give consent for the IRS to share their tax info. If anyone skips it, you won't be eligible for federal aid. Studentaid.gov has the details.
  • It asks how you finished high school, and homeschool is one of the options.
  • Your answers produce a Student Aid Index (SAI) that schools use to figure out your aid.
  • You file it every year you want aid, and some aid runs out, so file early (it's on your M19 calendar).
5

Scholarships

  • Start local. Community groups, local businesses, faith groups and civic clubs often have smaller applicant pools.
  • Check each school's own scholarships, including merit scholarships.
  • Look for homeschool-friendly scholarships and ones that fit your interests, heritage, lane or talents.
  • Reuse your writing. Your M21 stories can be adapted for many essays (in your own words, of course).
  • Search free tools: CareerOneStop's Scholarship Finder, your state's higher-education grant agency (many state grants use the FAFSA and have early deadlines) and each school's financial aid page.
  • Track them like applications: name, amount, deadline, status.
6

🚨 Money scam warnings

  • Never pay to apply for a scholarship or to "find" you scholarships.
  • Anything "guaranteed" is a red flag.
  • Never share bank logins or your StudentAid.gov password.
  • If a call or email pressures you to act today, stop and ask a parent.
7

Money in every lane

🎓 College

Your aid offer may include grants, scholarships, work-study and loans, all mixed together. Read it carefully: loans aren't free money, even when they're listed in the same box. Some private colleges also use the CSS Profile (M19). It charges a fee for each school you send it to, but many families qualify for a fee waiver, and some colleges also ask for info from a noncustodial parent (a parent you don't live with). Check cssprofile.collegeboard.org for current costs. Merit scholarships may require a minimum GPA to keep each year, so read the fine print.

Colleges that cost little or nothing: at work colleges like Berea or College of the Ozarks, every student works on campus, and tuition is covered for students who qualify with financial need. Many states also have "Promise" programs that pay community or technical college tuition, often with deadlines in the fall of senior year. Housing, food and fees can still cost money, so compare the full price.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Many accredited trade programs accept federal aid through the FAFSA, and Workforce Pell is a brand-new kind of Pell Grant for some short training programs. It's just getting started, so only a few programs qualify so far. Check studentaid.gov and ask each program directly. Look for trade scholarships from trade associations and foundations. The big one: registered apprenticeships pay you while you learn, often with little or no tuition.

More free training money: WIOA funds can pay for job training for some young people and adults. Rules vary by state, so ask your local American Job Center (find one on CareerOneStop). If you or a parent served, the GI Bill can also cover approved trade schools and apprenticeships for eligible people (check va.gov).

💼 Career

Many employers offer tuition assistance, so ask about it in interviews (M23). Some big employers pay tuition directly while you work: for example, Walmart at partner schools, Amazon's Career Choice for many hourly workers, and Starbucks for a first bachelor's degree online through Arizona State University. These deals only cover certain schools and programs and can change, so read the details (including what happens if you leave the job) before you sign up. The military has several paths: ROTC scholarships (M19), the GI Bill for veterans, and tuition help while serving. Get every promise in writing and check it on the official site.

🚀 Work for yourself

Start small: savings, pre-sales (customers pay before you make the thing), small grants, and youth business competitions (like those from Junior Achievement, ja.org, and DECA, deca.org). Try not to take on debt for an idea you haven't tested yet (M11).

🌍 Crossing borders

International students usually can't get US federal aid, so look for each school's own scholarships for international students. (Green-card holders and some other eligible noncitizens can get federal aid. Students without legal status usually can't, but some states offer state aid, so check your state.) US students heading abroad can sometimes use federal loans at participating foreign schools. Check each school early.

8

Asking for more

Here's something many families don't know: you can ask a college to take another look at your aid offer. This is called an appeal.

  • If your family's situation changed (a job loss, big medical bills, a divorce), colleges can adjust aid for special circumstances. Explain what changed and send documents.
  • Some colleges will also reconsider if you show them a better offer from a similar school.
  • Be polite, be specific, and ask what they need from you. The worst they can say is no.
9

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Homeschool families are often great at budgeting and finding creative ways to learn. Those same skills help here: hunting for local scholarships, comparing offers carefully, and earning money with a side business.

👪 Ask Your Parents

  1. "Can we fill out the FAFSA together, and when?"

  3. "Can you help me look for local scholarships?"
