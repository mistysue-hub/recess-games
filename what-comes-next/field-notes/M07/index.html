Put on your detective hat

You've got a list of "ooh, maybe" careers from M02. Now it's time to investigate them like a detective: gather clues, check your sources, and figure out what each career is really like before you commit to anything.

Because here's the thing. Most of what we "know" about careers comes from TV, movies and social media. TV lawyers win dramatic courtroom battles every episode. (Real lawyers spend a lot of time reading documents.) TV doctors solve medical mysteries in 42 minutes. Social media chefs plate beautiful food. (Real chefs also mop the kitchen at midnight.) Detectives look past the highlight reel.

2

Where real career clues live

These are the best free places to investigate a career:

📘 The Occupational Outlook Handbook (bls.gov/ooh), from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. It's the official source most other career websites copy from. For hundreds of jobs it tells you:

  • what the work is actually like day to day,
  • typical pay (median pay, which means half earn more and half earn less),
  • how much training you need,
  • whether the job is expected to grow or shrink.

🧭 My Next Move (mynextmove.org), from the US Department of Labor. You used it in M02. Click any career to see the skills, knowledge, personality traits and technology it needs.

🎥 Roadtrip Nation (roadtripnation.com). Video interviews with real people describing their jobs and the twisty paths that got them there. (Some videos need school access, but plenty of free clips are on its site and YouTube.) Perfect for hearing what a job feels like, not just what it pays.

🗣️ Real people. The best clue of all is talking to someone who does the job. You'll learn how to do that safely and confidently in M08.

3

The Source Detective

The internet is full of career advice. Some of it is great. Some of it is written to sell you something. Some of it is just wrong. A good detective checks every source with four questions:

  1. 🏢 Who runs it? A government agency, a school, a nonprofit, a company, or a random person? Can you even tell?
  2. 💰 How do they make money? If a site earns money when you sign up for a program, buy a course, or click an ad, it may lean toward telling you to do exactly that.
  3. 🔍 Do other sources agree? If one site says a job pays $90,000 and the official handbook says $45,000, trust the one with receipts.
  4. 📅 How recent is it? Job information gets old fast. A 2015 article about "the hottest jobs" might be describing jobs that have totally changed.

Then give the source a rating:

  • 🟢 Trust it. Official, independent, and backed up by others.
  • 🟡 Good, with a caveat. Useful, but there's a catch you should know (it sells something, it's a survey of opinions, it's a little old).
  • 🟠 Use with care. Unclear who's behind it, wild claims, or no way to check.

Rocky uses these exact ratings whenever he recommends a site, so now you know his secret system. (It's not very secret. He's been dying to tell someone.)

Watch out for these sketchy signs: "Make $10,000 a month from home!", "This one weird job nobody knows about," a site that won't say who runs it, a "free" career quiz that asks for your phone number, and anyone who promises a guaranteed job if you pay first.

4

🤖 AI and the future of work

You're heading into the working world at a time when AI is changing a lot of jobs. That can sound scary. It's actually something a good detective can investigate, so let's.

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 surveyed employers around the world about what they expect by 2030. A few big clues:

  • Employers expect about 39% of the key skills workers need to change by 2030. So the most important skill of all might be learning new skills.
  • They expect strong growth in hands-on frontline jobs like construction, farm work, delivery, care work and teaching, and in tech jobs like AI, data, cybersecurity and green energy.
  • They expect fewer jobs in some routine roles, like cashiers, data entry and some administrative work.
  • The human skills employers value most include analytical and creative thinking, resilience, flexibility, leadership and working well with others.

A detective's note: this report is a 🟡. It's respected and widely cited, but it's a survey of what employers predict, not a guarantee. Always pair it with the Occupational Outlook Handbook (🟢) for US pay and growth.

So for every career on your list, ask: How might AI change this job? Will it replace parts of the work, help with the boring parts, or barely touch it? Some careers will use AI as a power tool. Some are mostly about hands, hearts and human judgment. (A robot can't yet climb into your attic to fix the wiring. It's working on it. Slowly. Badly.)

Curious about the bigger questions, like whether AI is fair, safe or good for the planet? Recess has a whole course called Debating the use of AI.

5

🔭 Lane Peek

When you look up a career in the Occupational Outlook Handbook, check the "How to Become One" tab. You'll often find more than one road: a degree, an apprenticeship, on-the-job training, or the military. A detective notices all the doors, not just the front one.
