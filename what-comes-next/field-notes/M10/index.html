Welcome to life inside a company

Most people, in every lane, work for someone else at some point. College students take campus jobs. Apprentices work for a contractor or a union shop. Business owners often start by working for someone else and learning how it's done. So whatever road you take, it helps to know how workplaces actually work.

This module is your behind-the-scenes tour: who's who, how to be great to work with, what's on a paycheck, and what your rights are. (No, there won't be a test. There will, however, be a break room. There is always a break room.)

2

The day-to-day

Every workplace has a rhythm:

  • Schedules and shifts. Some jobs are 9 to 5. Others have shifts that change every week, start at 6 a.m., or end at midnight. Many jobs post the schedule a week or two ahead.
  • Breaks. Some jobs have set break times. Some let you take them when it's quiet.
  • On-site, remote or hybrid. Some jobs happen at a workplace, some from home, some a mix. Remote work means more freedom and more need to manage yourself.
3

Who's who

  • Your manager or supervisor gives you tasks, sets your schedule, and is the first person to go to with questions or problems.
  • Your team or crew are the people you work with every day.
  • HR (Human Resources) handles hiring, pay questions, benefits, and serious problems at work.
  • The org chart shows who reports to whom. In a small business, the org chart might be one person: the owner, who is also the cashier, the janitor and the guy who fixes the printer.
4

Being great to work with

Being skilled matters. But being great to work with is what gets you trusted, promoted and recommended. Here's how:

  • Show up on time (or a few minutes early). If you're going to be late, tell your manager before your shift, not after.
  • Ask questions. Nobody expects you to know everything on day one. Asking is smart, not weak.
  • Take feedback well. Listen, say thanks, and try it. You don't have to agree with everything, but stay calm and curious.
  • Own your mistakes. "I made a mistake on that order. Here's what I'll do to fix it" earns huge respect.
  • Get along with coworkers of all ages. You might work alongside a 17-year-old and a 70-year-old on the same shift.
  • Handle conflict calmly. Talk privately, stick to the facts, and ask your manager for help if you need it.
  • Follow the dress code and the phone rules. Even if the rule is "phones in lockers," which may feel like a tragedy.

If you're used to working at your own pace, fixed shift times and set tasks can feel strange at first. That's normal. It gets easier, and planning ahead helps. Pay and benefits, decoded

  • Hourly vs. salary. Hourly workers are paid for each hour worked. Salaried workers get a set amount per year, no matter the exact hours.
  • Overtime. Under federal law, most hourly workers earn time and a half for hours over 40 in a workweek.
  • Your pay stub. Your gross pay is what you earned. Your net pay (take-home pay) is what's left after deductions. The first time you see a paycheck can be a shock. Everyone gasps. It's a tradition.
  • Benefits. Many full-time jobs offer health insurance, paid time off, and retirement plans. Some employers match what you save for retirement, which is basically free money. (Fun fact: in the US, you can usually stay on a parent's health plan until age 26.)
  • 401(k) and 403(b) plans. These are workplace retirement accounts (403(b) is the version at schools, hospitals and nonprofits). If your employer offers a match, try to save at least enough to get the full match. Check the vesting schedule, too: it says how long you have to stay before the employer's share is fully yours.
  • Raises and promotions usually come from doing great work, learning new skills, and asking at the right time. (You'll practice asking in M25.)

🧾 A pay stub, translated

Here's a made-up paycheck for two weeks at a part-time job: 25 hours at $16 an hour.

Line on the stub Amount What it means
Gross pay $400.00 Everything you earned. Enjoy this number. It's about to shrink.
Social Security −$24.80 6.2% of your pay, toward Social Security benefits later in life
Medicare −$5.80 1.45% of your pay, toward health coverage later in life
Federal income tax −$9.00 Made-up number. For small part-time pay it's often $0; it depends on what you earn and your W-4
State income tax −$6.00 Only if your state has an income tax (not every state does)
Net pay $354.40 What actually lands in your account

Rocky's money tip: if you earn only a small amount in a year, you often don't owe federal income tax at all, so filing a tax return can get that withheld money back. (The Social Security and Medicare parts don't come back. Those are your contribution to the system.) Ask your parents to help you file your first return.

Your rights at work

You have rights from your very first job:

  • Minimum wage. There's a federal minimum wage, and many states and cities set a higher one. Federal law also lets employers pay workers under 20 a lower "youth wage" for their first 90 days, but some states don't allow it. Check dol.gov.
  • Safety. Your employer must provide a safe workplace, training, and safety gear when it's needed. You have the right to report hazards, and to refuse truly dangerous work, without being punished for it. OSHA's young workers page (osha.gov/young-workers) explains your rights in plain language.
  • Workers' comp. If you get hurt on the job, workers' compensation usually helps pay for medical care and lost pay. Tell your manager right away, even if it seems small.
  • Extra rules for workers under 18, such as limits on hours and on dangerous jobs (from M09).
  • Breaks. Federal law doesn't require breaks for most workers, but many states do.
  • Fair treatment. Federal and state laws protect workers from discrimination and harassment. The EEOC (eeoc.gov) is the federal agency that takes those complaints.

What "at-will" means. Most US jobs are at-will: you can quit any time, and your employer can let you go any time, for almost any reason that isn't illegal (like discrimination or punishing you for reporting a safety problem). Giving two weeks' notice before you quit isn't required by law, but it's polite and keeps your references happy.

If something feels wrong, unsafe or unfair, tell your parents first, then your manager or HR. The US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division (dol.gov/agencies/whd) takes pay complaints for free and keeps them confidential, and your state's labor department handles state rules.

Different kinds of workplaces

  • Big companies: training programs, clear rules, room to move up, more paperwork.
  • Small businesses: you learn a bit of everything and work closely with the owner.
  • Startups: fast, exciting, sometimes chaotic.
  • Government: stable, with clear pay scales and good benefits.
  • Union shops: a union negotiates pay, benefits and safety rules for workers. Common in many trades.
  • The military: a workplace with its own very clear chain of command, and very early wake-up calls.
11

Research an employer like a detective

Before you apply anywhere, investigate:

  • The careers page: what the company says it values, its benefits, and its training programs.
  • Employee reviews on sites like Glassdoor and Indeed: 🟠 use with care. Reviews can lean positive and can be influenced by employers. Look for patterns that show up again and again, and ignore one super-angry or super-glowing review.
  • Real people: talk to someone who works there (your People Map from M08), or look at employees' career paths on LinkedIn.
  • Try it: some companies have free job simulations on Forage (from M09).
12

🤖 AI at work

Many workplaces now use AI tools. Smart habits:

  • Learn each employer's rules about AI. They vary a lot.
  • Never paste private company or customer information into an AI tool.
  • Double-check anything AI produces before you use it.
  • Be honest about what you did yourself.
13

🚩 Red flags

Watch out for: having to pay to work, a vague job description, people constantly quitting, and pressure to decide on the spot.

🍁 In Canada?

Your pay stub looks a little different: instead of Social Security and Medicare you'll see CPP (the Canada Pension Plan, which starts at 18) and EI (Employment Insurance), plus federal and provincial income tax, and you'll fill out a TD1 form instead of a W-4. Minimum wage, overtime and breaks are set by your province. Health care comes from your provincial plan, and workplace safety claims go to your province's board (like WSIB in Ontario or WorkSafeBC).
