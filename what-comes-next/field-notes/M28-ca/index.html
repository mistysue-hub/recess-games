The stuff nobody teaches you

Nobody hands you a manual when you become an adult. Suddenly there are leases, bank accounts, credit scores and forms with names like "authorization." It can feel like everyone else got the manual and you didn't.

Good news: this is the manual. Today you'll learn the everyday skills that make independent life work, from budgets to IDs to what changes when you become a legal adult. (Rocky has laminated his. He won't say how many times.)

2

Your first budget

A budget is just a plan for your money before you spend it. Start simple:

  1. Money in: your pay after CPP, EI and taxes (M13), plus any regular help.
  2. Needs: rent, food, transportation, phone, insurance.
  3. Savings: even a little, every paycheque. Build an emergency fund for surprises.
  4. Wants: fun stuff. Yes, it belongs in the budget too.

If money in is less than money out, something has to change. Track your spending for a month. Most people are surprised where it goes.

3

Saving for later

Compound interest means your savings earn money, and then that money earns money too. Start young and small amounts can grow into big ones.

  • TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account): open one at 18 with a SIN (19 in some provinces, with your room from 18 carried over). What it earns isn't taxed. The 2026 limit is about $7,000; check canada.ca.
  • FHSA (First Home Savings Account): saving for a first home someday.
  • RRSP and workplace plans: earned income builds RRSP room. If your employer matches your pension or group RRSP savings, put in enough to get the full match. It's free money (M10).
4

Getting around and a place to live

Transportation: compare the real cost of a car (payment, insurance, gas, repairs, parking) with transit, biking or carpooling. (Insurance, accidents and traffic stops are in M29.)

Housing: before you sign a lease, read it with a parent. Know the rent, deposit, length, and who pays utilities and repairs. Tenant rules are provincial, so look up your province's landlord and tenant board or residential tenancy office. Get a roommate agreement in writing (quiet hours, chores, guests, splitting bills). Tenant insurance is usually cheap and protects your stuff. Take photos of every room on move-in day. They help you get your deposit back when you leave.

5

Speaking up for yourself

At school or work, nobody will check in on you the way your family did. Self-advocacy means speaking up for what you need:

  • Professors and instructors: go to office hours. Ask questions early, not the night before the test.
  • Bosses: ask when you don't understand, and tell them early if there's a problem.
  • Offices (financial aid, housing, accessibility services, HR): be polite, specific and persistent. Write down who you talked to and when.
6

Making friends in a new place

Say yes to things in the first few weeks: orientation (sometimes called "frosh") events, clubs, a study group, a crew lunch, a faith group, a pickup game. Go more than once, because friendships grow from showing up again. Everyone else is new too.

7

Adult paperwork

  • SIN (Social Insurance Number): free from Service Canada (apply yourself at 12+). There are no plastic cards anymore. Give it only to your employer, bank and the government.
  • ID: get a provincial photo ID card or driver's licence. A passport is handy too. Keep your birth certificate somewhere safe at home, not in your wallet.
  • Bank account: a chequing account for bills and direct deposit, and a savings account for your goals. Look for no-fee student accounts, and watch out for overdraft fees.
  • Health card: carry your provincial health card (like OHIP in Ontario, MSP in BC, AHCIP in Alberta or RAMQ in Quebec). Studying in another province? Check your home plan's rules first. In Ontario, OHIP+ covers many prescriptions until your 25th birthday, but only if you're not on a private plan.
  • Taxes: if you earn money, file a tax return each spring. Your T4 slip (from employers) shows what you earned. Filing unlocks benefits like the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (it replaced the GST/HST credit), generally from age 19. Free volunteer tax clinics help people with modest incomes.
8

Health care on your own

  • Make your own appointments for the doctor and dentist. Have your health card ready when you call.
  • Know where to go: your family doctor or a walk-in clinic for checkups and ongoing problems, urgent care for things that can't wait but aren't dangerous (a sprain, a bad sore throat), and the ER (or 911) for emergencies like chest pain. Many provinces also have a free health advice line (Ontario's is Health811), so look up yours.
  • Refill prescriptions before you run out.
  • Mental health counts too. Talk to your doctor or your campus counselling centre. In a crisis, call or text 9-8-8 any time, in English or French. Kids Help Phone is there too.
9

Credit basics

Your credit score is a number lenders use to guess how likely you are to pay back money. Your credit history can affect renting an apartment and getting a car loan.

  • Paying on time matters most. Every time.
  • Use only a small part of your limit, and pay the full balance if you can.
  • Safe ways to start: a secured card (you put down a deposit), or being added as an authorized user on a parent's card.
  • Debt traps to avoid: carrying a balance on high-interest cards, "buy now, pay later" stacked on top of each other, and payday loans.

You'll usually need to be the age of majority (18 or 19) to get your own credit card.

10

Staying safe online

Identity theft is when someone uses your info to open accounts or borrow money in your name. Protect yourself:

  • Watch for phishing: messages that rush you to click a link or "verify" your account. Go to the real site yourself.
  • Use strong, different passwords for every account (a password manager helps), and turn on two-factor login.
  • Check your credit report: Canada has two main credit bureaus, Equifax and TransUnion, and both let you see your report free online.
  • Freeze or lock your credit if your province allows it. Quebec has offered free freezes since 2023, and Ontario has added them too. Elsewhere, ask about a fraud alert.
  • If it happens, report it to your local police and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, and tell your bank and both bureaus.
11

🎂 You're an adult! Now what?

In Canada, the age of majority depends on your province or territory: 18 in Alberta, Manitoba, Ontario, PEI, Quebec and Saskatchewan, and 19 in BC, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia and the three territories. That's when contracts, credit cards and some consents become yours.

  • School records: colleges and universities deal with you, not your parents. They usually need your permission to share your grades with your parents.
  • Health information: there's no single Canadian form, and rules vary by province. Ask your doctor how to give permission to share with your parents. Many families also set up a power of attorney for personal care so a trusted adult can help in an emergency.
  • Voting: at 18 you can vote in federal and provincial elections. At 14 to 17, you can join Elections Canada's Register of Future Electors online, and you'll be added to the voters list when you turn 18.
  • Jury duty: adult citizens may get a summons. Don't ignore it; contact the court if you need a delay.
  • No draft registration: Canada doesn't have one. (If you're also a US citizen, US Selective Service still applies at 18. Check sss.gov.)

Launch skills in every lane

🎓 College

Learn your school's portal, email and student ID in week one. Find advising, financial aid, the health centre, accessibility services and tutoring.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

You may need your own tools, boots, safety gear and a ride to job sites. A starter tool set plus boots and safety glasses can run a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars. Ask whether your employer offers a tool allowance, and check your province's apprenticeship site for any tool grants.

💼 Career

Set up direct deposit, fill out your TD1 forms, read your first pay stub (M10), and enrol in benefits before the sign-up window closes.

🚀 Work for yourself

Keep business and personal money separate, set aside money for taxes and CPP (M11), and track your income and expenses every month.

🌍 Crossing borders

Heading to the US? Get a passport early, and keep copies of important documents. Your provincial health plan pays little outside Canada, so buy your school's plan or student travel insurance. US earnings can mean filing taxes in both countries, so ask your school's international office. The US version of this module covers US paperwork.
