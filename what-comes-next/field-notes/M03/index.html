Strengths are bigger than grades

Last time, we hunted for what pulls you in. Today's question is different: what are you good at?

If your first thought was a list of grades or test scores, pause right there. Grades are one tiny window into your strengths, and honestly, not even the most interesting one. Some of your biggest strengths might never have shown up on a report card:

  • calming down a little sibling in the middle of a meltdown,
  • fixing the Wi-Fi when nobody else can,
  • organizing the co-op bake sale and actually making money,
  • explaining something confusing so it finally makes sense,
  • sticking with a hard project for months.

Those are real skills. Employers, colleges and training programs care about them a lot. Today we're going to find yours.

(And if you're thinking "I'm not good at anything," Rocky would like a word. Everybody is good at something. Sometimes it's just hiding behind a skill you think is "normal" because it comes easily to you. Easy for you is often hard for other people. That's the definition of a strength.)

2

Three kinds of strengths

It helps to sort strengths into three buckets:

🧠 Knowledge. Stuff you know. History facts, how engines work, music theory, three programming languages, every Pokémon ever. You can learn knowledge from books, videos, classes, and experience.

🛠️ Skills. Stuff you can do. Writing clearly, building furniture, editing video, cooking for a crowd, solving equations, speaking to a group, fixing bikes. Skills get stronger with practice.

💪 Traits. How you tend to work. Patient, curious, reliable, creative, calm under pressure, detail-oriented, brave enough to ask questions. Traits are harder to teach, which is exactly why people value them.

You want a mix from all three buckets. A student who says "I know a lot about animals (knowledge), I'm great at handling nervous dogs (skill), and I'm patient (trait)" just described someone who could thrive as a vet tech, a dog trainer, a zookeeper, or a pet-sitting business owner.

3

Find your strengths three ways

1. Look back at what you've done

Homeschoolers usually have a long list of real experiences. Think about:

  • Projects you finished (science fairs, a novel, a garden, a robot, a business, a YouTube channel)
  • Co-op classes, dual enrollment, or online courses where you did well or helped others
  • Jobs and volunteering (babysitting, mowing lawns, church nursery, the library, the farm stand)
  • Hobbies and sports (what did you get better at over time?)
  • Responsibilities at home (running the household schedule, caring for animals, teaching younger siblings, fixing things)

For each one, ask: What did I actually do? What was I good at?

2. Take the Skills Matcher

CareerOneStop's Skills Matcher (careeronestop.org, made by the US Department of Labor) asks you to rate yourself on 40 work skills, from "reading" to "repairing" to "persuading," and then shows careers that use your strongest skills. It takes about 15 minutes. Be honest, not humble and not boastful. Rate yourself the way a fair coach would.

3. Ask other people what they see

This is the secret weapon. Other people often notice our strengths before we do. Ask at least two people who know you well, outside your own head:

"What do you think I'm really good at? What do people come to me for?"

Good people to ask: parents, grandparents, a Recess guide or mentor, a co-op teacher, a coach, a boss, a friend. Write down nothing. Just listen, say thanks, and tell Rocky what they said. (Bonus: hearing nice things about yourself is a surprisingly great way to spend an afternoon.)

4

🎖️ Optional: the ASVAB Career Exploration Program

The ASVAB Career Exploration Program is a free aptitude test plus an interest inventory, run by the US Department of Defense. It measures things like word knowledge, math reasoning, mechanical understanding and electronics, and connects your results to careers in every lane: college, trades, apprenticeships, the military, and more.

A few things to know:

  • Taking it does not sign you up for anything. There's no military obligation.
  • You take it at a school or group testing session, not on your own. Homeschoolers may be able to join a local school's session or ask a homeschool group to request one. Ask a nearby public school's counselor, or use the contact page on asvabprogram.com to find the testing office near you.
  • Part of the program's official purpose is helping military recruiters find interested students. The testing school chooses whether results are shared with recruiters, and your parent can file an opt-out form (it's on asvabprogram.com). Taking the test never obligates you to anything.

It's optional, but it's a great extra source of clues, especially if you like hands-on, mechanical or technical work.

5

🔭 Lane Peek: same strength, different roads

Strengths travel across every lane. Here's one example:

"I'm great at explaining things."

  • 🎓 College: teacher, professor, science communicator, speech therapist
  • 🔧 Trade: training new apprentices, becoming a master electrician who teaches, a medical assistant who helps patients understand instructions
  • 💼 Career: corporate trainer, customer success specialist, tour guide, military instructor
  • 🚀 Work for yourself: tutoring business, YouTube educator, online course creator

When you find a strength, try this game: name one way to use it in each lane. It stretches your thinking, and it means you'll always have a Plan B (and advice for a friend on a different path).

6

🚫 Skip the comparison game

Looking at someone else's strengths and feeling small? Remember you're comparing your behind-the-scenes to their highlight reel. The only fair comparison is you now vs. you a year ago. What can you do now that you couldn't then? That's growth, and it's worth being proud of.

7

Strengths can be built

One more important thing: strengths aren't fixed. You're not born with a set number of skill points that you're stuck with forever. Most skills grow with practice, feedback and time. So when Rocky asks about strengths, he'll also ask about growth areas: things you're not great at yet but want to get better at. That word "yet" is doing a lot of work, and it should.

8

🍁 In Canada?

Swap the Skills Matcher for Job Bank's free Abilities quiz (about 10 minutes) at jobbank.gc.ca. There's no Canadian version of the ASVAB career program for students; the Canadian Armed Forces give their own aptitude test only when you apply to join, so you can skip that section.

9

👪 Ask Your Parents

Your parents probably have the longest list of evidence about you. Ask them:

  1. "What's something you've seen me get really good at over the years?"
  2. "Is there a skill you think I undersell?"

And one practical item: if you're curious about the ASVAB Career Exploration Program, ask them to help you find a local school or homeschool group that offers a testing session. Rocky can help you write a quick note.
