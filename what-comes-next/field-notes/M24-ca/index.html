The money map

Money can feel like the scariest part of what comes next. Here's the most important thing to know: the price tag is almost never the price you pay. There's help in every lane, and today you'll learn where to find it.

Back in M13 you looked at what each path costs. Now you'll find the money to pay for it. (Rocky has a spreadsheet for this. Of course he does. It has seven tabs and a color key.)

2

Four kinds of money

Every money plan mixes these:

  1. Free money: grants, bursaries and scholarships. You don't pay them back.
  2. Earned money: part-time jobs, summer jobs, and apprenticeship wages.
  3. Borrowed money: loans. You pay them back later. The federal part of Canada Student Loans is interest-free, but provincial loans and private lines of credit may charge interest.
  4. Family and savings: an RESP your family may have, plus whatever your family plans to contribute (a conversation for your parents, not a test).

The goal is simple: use free and earned money first, and borrow carefully.

3

Sticker price vs. what you actually pay

Remember M16? The sticker price is the published cost. What you actually pay is what's left after free money. A school with a scary sticker price can end up cheaper than one that looks affordable, especially once entrance scholarships are added, so never cross a place off your list until you see the real number.

Money Fit: for each place on your list, give it a score from 1 to 5 for how well its real cost fits what your family has talked about. It goes in the last column of your Fit Scorecard, next to Academic Fit (M16) and Social Fit (M17).

🍁 Student aid: one application through your province

In Canada there's no FAFSA. Instead, you fill out one student-aid application through your province or territory, and it checks you for both federal and provincial grants and loans at the same time.

  • Ontario: OSAP. BC: StudentAid BC. Alberta: Alberta Student Aid. Quebec runs its own separate program (Aide financière aux études), and so do the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. Everyone else: search "student aid" plus your province's name on its official government site.
  • It's free. If a site charges you to apply, it's not the real one.
  • You'll need a Social Insurance Number (SIN), which is free from Service Canada.
  • For most students right out of high school, parents' income counts, so you'll fill it out together.
  • You apply every year you want aid. Apply once you know your program; applications for the fall usually open in spring or early summer (it's on your M19 calendar).

Federal help, 2026–27 (check the current amounts):

  • Canada Student Grant for full-time students: up to about $4,200 a year, depending on family income. You're checked for it automatically when you apply.
  • Canada Student Grant for Students with Disabilities: up to about $2,800 a year (you'll send proof of your disability with your application).
  • Canada Student Loans: the federal part is interest-free.
  • Most private, for-profit schools no longer qualify for the federal grant, so ask before you enrol.

Provincial heads-up: in Ontario, for programs starting on or after August 1, 2026, the provincial grant is capped at about 25% of your provincial aid, so much more of OSAP is now loan. BC, Alberta and Quebec have their own rules. Check your province's site for the current mix.

5

RESP, grants and the Canada Learning Bond

Many families save in a Registered Education Savings Plan (RESP). The government adds the Canada Education Savings Grant (20% of the first $2,500 saved each year, up to about $7,200 in total), and lower-income families may also have received the Canada Learning Bond (CLB).

Two things you can act on:

  • The Canada Learning Bond (up to $2,000 total): if you were eligible as a kid but nobody claimed it, you can request it yourself at 18, up until the day before you turn 21, even if your parents never opened an RESP. You'll need a SIN and an RESP in your name. Check canada.ca for the steps.
  • RESP withdrawals while you study count as your income, not your parents'. Since students usually earn little, you may owe little or no tax on them. Your parent requests the money; you show proof you're enrolled.
6

Scholarships

  • Entrance scholarships first. Many Canadian universities and colleges give automatic scholarships based on your admission average. Ask each school how it works out an average for homeschool applicants.
  • Start local. Community groups, local businesses, faith groups and service clubs often have smaller applicant pools.
  • Look for homeschool-friendly scholarships and ones that fit your interests, heritage, lane or talents.
  • National awards have early deadlines. For example, the Loran Award closes around mid-October of your final year, and the Schulich Leader scholarship (STEM) needs a school nomination, so homeschoolers should ask early how they can be nominated.
  • Reuse your writing. Your M21 stories can be adapted for many essays (in your own words, of course).
  • Search free tools like ScholarshipsCanada, plus each school's awards page.
  • Track them like applications: name, amount, deadline, status.
  • Bursaries are need-based money that many schools let you apply for after you're admitted. Ask the financial aid office.
7

🚨 Money scam warnings

  • Never pay to apply for a scholarship or to "find" you scholarships.
  • Anything "guaranteed" is a red flag.
  • Never share bank logins, your SIN, or your student-aid account password.
  • If a call or email pressures you to act today, stop and ask a parent. You can report scams to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.
8

Money in every lane

🎓 College

Your provincial aid notice and your school's offer may include grants, bursaries, scholarships and loans, all listed together. Read carefully: loans aren't free money, even when they're in the same box. Renewable scholarships often need a minimum average each year, so read the fine print.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Apprenticeships pay you while you learn. For your in-school training blocks, eligible apprentices can get Employment Insurance (EI), and the Canada Apprentice Loan offers up to about $20,000, interest-free (you can apply up to about 3 months before training starts). Some provinces add their own grants or tool programs, so check your provincial apprenticeship site. (The federal Apprenticeship Incentive and Completion Grants closed in March 2025.) College trade programs before an apprenticeship can qualify for regular student aid, and trade associations often offer scholarships.

💼 Career

Many employers offer tuition assistance, so ask about it in interviews (M23). These deals can cover only certain programs, so read the details (including what happens if you leave the job). The Canadian Armed Forces pays you while you train, and ROTP pays for a degree in exchange for years of service. Get every promise in writing and check it on forces.ca.

🚀 Work for yourself

Start small: savings, pre-sales (customers pay before you make the thing), small grants, and youth business programs in your province. Try not to take on debt for an idea you haven't tested yet (M11).

🍁 First Nations, Inuit and Métis students

First Nations students can ask their First Nation (or its designated organization) about the Post-Secondary Student Support Program, which sets its own deadlines. Inuit and Métis students can ask their own organizations and governments about education funding.

🌍 Crossing borders

Heading to the US? Canadian citizens can't get FAFSA aid, so look for each US school's own scholarships for international students and athletic aid. Some US schools are designated for Canada Student Loans, and RESP money can be used at qualifying schools abroad, so check each school early. The US version of this module explains US aid in detail.

9

Asking for more

Canadian schools don't usually "negotiate" offers the way some US colleges do. But you still have ways to ask:

  • If your family's situation changed (a job loss, big medical bills, a separation), ask your provincial aid office about a reassessment, appeal or change-of-circumstances form, and send documents.
  • Ask the school's financial aid office about bursaries and emergency funds.
  • Be polite, be specific, and ask what they need from you. The worst they can say is no.
10

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Homeschool families are often great at budgeting and finding creative ways to learn. Those same skills help here: hunting for local scholarships, comparing offers carefully, and earning money with a side business. And because homeschoolers are a bit unusual to admissions offices, a short, friendly email asking "How do you calculate scholarship averages for homeschoolers?" can pay off.

👪 Ask Your Parents

  1. "Can we fill out my provincial student-aid application together, and when?"
  2. "Do I have an RESP? And what can our family contribute, and what should I plan to cover?" (A gentle conversation, not a test.)
  3. "Can you help me look for local scholarships?"
