You're your own school

When a student at a big high school applies somewhere, a school office sends their transcript, a counselor writes a letter, and a records clerk handles the paperwork. You have something better: your own family. For homeschoolers, your parents are your school's official office. They create and sign the documents that tell your story.

Today you'll learn which documents you need, what goes in each one, and how to help your parents build them early. (Rocky would like to point out that "early" and "the night before the deadline" are not the same thing. He has checked a dictionary.)

2

The core documents

Here's what most homeschoolers put together:

  • Transcript: a list of your high school courses with grades, credits, a grading scale and a GPA, plus your parent's signature and your expected graduation date.
  • Course descriptions: a short paragraph for each course: what you studied, the main books or materials, and how it was graded.
  • Reading list: the books you've read for school (and often for fun too). Colleges love these.
  • School profile: a one-page overview of your homeschool: your family's approach, your grading scale, and any outside classes or co-ops.
  • Counselor letter: many college applications ask for a letter from your school counselor. For homeschoolers, a parent usually writes it.
  • Diploma: in most states, your family issues it when you graduate (if you're in an umbrella school, it may come from them).
  • Outside records: official test scores (sent by the testing company), dual-enrollment transcripts (sent by the college), and certificates from other courses.
  • Portfolio (optional): samples of your best work: writing, art, projects, a business you ran, things you built.
  • Activities list: your hobbies, clubs, sports, arts, volunteering, jobs and awards, with the years, about how many hours a week, and one thing you accomplished in each. Applications for college, scholarships and jobs almost always ask for this, and homeschool lists are often wonderfully unusual. Count the things you do for fun, too: the robot you built, the 4-H ribbon, the YouTube channel, the years of piano.
3

What counts as a credit?

High schools measure courses in credits. A full-year course usually earns one credit. Many homeschool families count about 120 to 180 hours of work as one credit, and a half-year course as half a credit. Your state's rules and the schools you apply to may have their own expectations, so your parents should check.

Homeschoolers often learn in ways that don't look like a textbook class, and that's great. Building a chicken coop and tracking the egg business could become part of an agriculture or business course. Months of serious robotics could be an engineering elective. The trick is describing it honestly and clearly.

4

How each lane uses your paper trail

🎓 College

College applications usually ask for a transcript, a school profile and a counselor letter, and some ask for course descriptions too.

How applying works: many colleges use the Common App, some use the Coalition application, and others have their own or a state system (like ApplyTexas or the University of California application). On the Common App, you mark yourself as homeschooled in the Education section, then invite a parent as your counselor. That parent submits your transcript, school profile and counselor letter, and later a mid-year report if a college asks for one. (Check commonapp.org for the current steps.)

Each college may want something extra from homeschoolers, so check the list you made in M16.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Many programs ask for proof of a high school diploma (or equivalent) and a transcript. Some want to see specific classes, like algebra. A clear homeschool transcript and diploma usually do the job. ("Equivalent" means a test-based credential like the GED or HiSET. Most places accept a parent-issued homeschool diploma, so only get one of those if a specific program says it needs it.)

💼 Career

Employers sometimes ask for proof of graduation. The military accepts homeschool diplomas, but recruiters may ask for extra documents, like a transcript or proof your homeschool followed your state's rules, so have them ready.

🚀 Work for yourself

Your portfolio and records of your business (what you made, sold and learned) are your paper trail. They can even become a course on your transcript.

🌍 Crossing borders

If you're applying from outside the US, or heading to a school abroad, your records may need a credential evaluation from a service the school names. Some schools also want translations. Ask each school early, because it takes time.

5

🏅 Special paperwork

  • College athletes: the NCAA Eligibility Center asks homeschool families for a signed administrator statement, a transcript and core-course worksheets. Start by sophomore or junior year (M19).
  • Accommodations: if you have a learning difference or disability, keep copies of any evaluations or past support plans. Colleges and testing companies may ask for them (M16, M18).
  • Your state's records: homeschool record-keeping rules vary by state. Your parents can check your state's requirements.
6

Start now, not senior year

The easiest transcript is the one you build as you go. Here's why starting early matters:

  • You'll remember what you studied, which books you read, and how many hours you spent.
  • You can save samples of your best work while they're fresh.
  • You'll spot gaps early (like a missing science or language course) while there's still time to fill them.
7

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Your transcript can show things a regular school transcript can't: deep-dive projects, real-world work, a business, or a course designed around your passion. That makes you memorable. Admissions officers read thousands of transcripts that look the same. Yours won't.

9

👪 Ask Your Parents

This module needs your parents more than any other. Ask them:

  1. "Can we start (or update) my transcript and course descriptions together?"
  2. "Would you write my counselor letter and school profile when the time comes?"
  3. "Do we know what our state requires for homeschool records?"
  4. "Can we set a date to finish the first draft?"

Rocky can help you make a checklist to share with them.
