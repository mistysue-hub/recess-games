Unfold the whole map

Back in M01, you met the three lanes. Today we unfold the whole map, side trails and scenic routes included. Why look at roads you're sure you won't take? Because you can't choose a road you don't know exists, roads connect more than you'd think, and someday a friend or cousin will ask you. Grab your imaginary compass (and maybe a toque).

2

🎓 College and university roads

Universities offer bachelor's degrees in hundreds of programs. A general degree can take three years at some schools, and an honours degree usually takes four.

Colleges, polytechnics and institutes offer shorter, hands-on programs: certificates (often about a year), diplomas (usually two to three years) and, at some schools, applied degrees. They usually cost less than university, and many have transfer agreements into university degrees: a smart route, not a "lesser" one.

Quebec's CEGEPs are their own road. After Secondary V, Quebec students usually go to CEGEP for a two-year pre-university DEC or a three-year technical DEC that leads straight to work. Quebec bachelor's degrees are then usually three years. Applications for many CEGEPs go through regional services like SRAM, and the first round usually closes around early March.

Online and open universities like Athabasca University and Thompson Rivers University Open Learning let you study from home. To check that any school is real, look it up in the CICIC directory (cicic.ca); M16 shows you how.

Dual credit in Canada is mostly for students enrolled in public high schools, so homeschoolers usually take a different door, like an open-university course or a provincial online high-school course. Lots of Recess students have taken early courses, so ask them what it was like. Grades stick, so start with one course and check that it will transfer (M16).

Graduate school comes after a bachelor's degree (doctor, lawyer, therapist). Just know it's there.

🔧 Trade school and apprenticeship roads

College trade programs teach a specific skill, like welding, hairstyling, automotive service or carpentry, often in a few months to two years. Many colleges also run pre-apprenticeship programs (some are free) to get you ready for the real thing.

Apprenticeships are real jobs with structured training, run by your province or territory. You find an employer (your sponsor), register as an apprentice, and get paid while you learn, with raises as you go. Most of your time is on the job, plus blocks of classroom training. Most apprenticeships take about 2 to 5 years. (Yes, it's a lot. You're also getting paid the whole time.)

Journeyperson is the finish line: fully trained, full pay, certificate in hand. In many trades you can also write the Red Seal exam. Pass it and your provincial certificate gets a Red Seal endorsement, which shows employers across Canada that you meet the national standard.

How you start depends on where you live:

  • Ontario: you need to be at least 16, be allowed to work in Canada (a SIN), and have a sponsor. At 16 or 17, you and a parent fill out a paper form; at 18+ you apply online through Skilled Trades Ontario.
  • Alberta: each trade needs certain high-school courses. Homeschooler without them? You can write an entrance exam instead (or use a CAEC/GED). Check tradesecrets.alberta.ca.
  • BC: start at SkilledTradesBC. Youth trades programs run through school districts, so ask whether a distributed-learning school could enrol you.
  • Quebec: construction trades usually start with a vocational diploma (DEP) before you can work as an apprentice. Other trades have their own routes.

Some apprenticeships run through a union, others directly through employers, so compare pay and benefits. And in some trades (like electrician in most provinces) you must be registered or certified to do the work at all. Always check your province.

💼 Career roads

Jobs that train you. Many companies hire right out of high school and train you, in retail, banking, logistics, manufacturing, customer service and more. Some even help pay for school later.

The Canadian Armed Forces (CAF). You can usually apply at 17 (with a parent's consent if you're under 18) if you're a citizen or permanent resident with at least Grade 10 (Secondary IV in Quebec); some jobs need more. Applying includes an aptitude test (the CFAT), a medical and an interview. Always talk to recruiters with a parent involved, and start at forces.ca.

The Reserve Force is the part-time option, training near home while you work or study. ROTP pays for a degree at the Royal Military College or a civilian university, then you serve as an officer for a set number of years (RMC looks for about a 75% average).

⚠️ Early-deadline paths. ROTP applications open around September of your final year and are usually due by late January, and pilot and some other air jobs by mid-December. Big national scholarships like the Loran Award close as early as mid-October of grade 12. Sports: for Canadian universities (U SPORTS) or colleges (CCAA), contact coaches by grade 11. If you might play at an NCAA Division I or II school in the US, the courses you take from grade 9 on can count, and homeschoolers register with the NCAA Eligibility Center (eligibilitycenter.org). If you're even a little curious about any of these, note it on the page now.

A planned gap year. A year between high school and your next step, used on purpose: working, volunteering, traveling or trying a field. With a goal, it makes you a stronger applicant. Without one, it can turn into a very long nap.

Straight into a job you love. Many creative, tech, sales and hospitality jobs mostly need skill and a portfolio, not a degree.

5

🛤️ More roads worth knowing

  • Volunteer service. The Canada Service Corps connects youth (about 15 to 30) with volunteer placements, part-time or full-time, and offers micro-grants (from about $250 to $5,000) for service projects you design yourself. Placements aren't a paycheque, so plan for that. Check canada.ca for current details.
  • Ships and the sea. The Canadian Coast Guard Academy (renamed from the Coast Guard College in 2025) in Sydney, Nova Scotia runs a four-year officer program with free tuition, room and board.
  • Fast-start helper jobs. Personal support worker (PSW) or health care aide programs are often under a year, and many 911 communicators train on the job. Lots of nurses and paramedics started here.
  • Outdoor and seasonal work. Provincial wildfire crews (with a tough fitness test) and seasonal jobs with Parks Canada, ski hills and summer camps. Summer hiring often happens in winter.
  • Driving. A big-truck licence (Class 1, or Class A in Ontario) usually means being 18+, and Ontario and some other provinces require Mandatory Entry-Level Training (MELT) first.
  • Faith-based service. Missions and church volunteer years. Ask who pays for travel, housing and insurance.
  • The family business or farm. A real path too, with an honest family talk about roles and pay.

Rules change, so check each one's official site with a parent.

🌍 Crossing borders

Heading to the US? Canadians don't need a visa stamp to study there, but you'll still need the school's I-20 form and to pay the SEVIS fee. Student status limits most work to on-campus jobs, so US apprenticeships and "jobs that train you" mostly aren't open to you, and US federal aid (FAFSA, Pell) isn't for Canadian citizens. The US version of this module covers the US roads; also check studyinthestates.dhs.gov.

Elsewhere. Some countries have shorter degrees (many in England take three years). International Experience Canada has youth mobility ("working holiday") agreements with more than 35 countries, generally for ages 18 to 35, depending on the country. You'll need savings, a passport and a parent double-checking every program.

8

🧭 Brand-new trails (and how to check them)

New programs pop up all the time, from tuition-free fellowships to bootcamps to gap-year programs. The honest part: new isn't the same as stable. Programs sometimes close, change their rules or promise more than they deliver. Before you sign up for anything, ask (with a parent):

  • Who pays for it, and for how long? Is "free" guaranteed?
  • How many groups have finished it, and where are they now? Real results, not just success stories.
  • Is it recognized by a province, and will credits count? Get it in writing.
  • What if it shuts down halfway? What's the refund policy?
  • Any loans or "pay us later from your salary" deals? Those are still debt.

Brand-new can still be a great fit. Just walk in with your eyes open.

9

Roads cross more than you think

Here's the best secret on the map: the roads connect. A college diploma leads to a university degree, an apprenticeship to your own business, the Reserves to help with school, a job to a company-paid course, and yes, a university degree to an apprenticeship. There are very few dead ends. Mostly there are detours, and Rocky loves a good detour.
