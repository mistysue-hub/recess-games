Your story is already good

Here's a secret about applications: the people reading them aren't looking for a perfect person. They're looking for a real one. They want to know what you care about, what you've done, and how you think.

You already have the material. It's in your Experience Log, your strengths from M03, your try-outs from M09, and all those things you've told Rocky along the way. Today you'll learn how to turn it into the pieces every lane asks for: an essay, a résumé, a portfolio, an interview answer, a pitch. (Rocky has been rehearsing his own story in the mirror. It's mostly about calendars. He's working on it.)

2

Your words, your voice

Let's be clear about AI before we start. AI tools can help you brainstorm, organize your notes, and check for typos. They should not write your story for you.

  • The Common App counts submitting AI-generated writing as your own work as fraud, the same as plagiarism.
  • Colleges set their own rules. Some allow AI for brainstorming or proofreading; some don't allow it at all. Employers and scholarship committees may have rules too. Check each one.
  • Readers can often tell. AI writing tends to sound smooth and generic, and generic is the one thing your story can't be.

The best reason is simpler: nobody else has lived your life. Your voice, with its quirks and its exact details, is the point. Rocky will ask you questions, help you find your best material and give feedback, but every word is yours.

3

Show, don't tell

The number one trick for every kind of story: give the specific detail instead of the big claim.

  • Telling: "I'm a hard worker."
  • Showing: "I got up at 5:30 every Saturday for two summers to open the farmers market stand, and by August I was running the register alone."

The second one proves the first, and it's way more interesting. Look for numbers (how many, how long, how often), actions (what you did), results (what changed), and moments (one scene, not your whole life).

4

Making homeschooling a strength

You don't need to explain or defend homeschooling. Show what it let you do: the deep-dive project, the business you started, the sibling you taught to read, the co-op class you helped lead, the schedule you managed yourself. Homeschoolers often have unusual stories. Unusual is memorable.

5

Your story in every lane

🎓 College

The personal essay is your chance to be a person, not a list of activities. The Common App essay has a 650-word limit and a set of prompts (one is "any topic you choose"). Many colleges add shorter supplemental essays, like "Why us?"

A good process: brainstorm with Rocky, pick one story that shows who you are, write a messy first draft, set it aside for a few days, revise, then read it aloud. A parent or mentor can give feedback, but they don't rewrite it. It should still sound like you talking on a good day.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Programs often care more about your interview and hands-on proof than an essay. Build a skills portfolio: photos of things you've built, fixed or made, with a sentence about each (what it was, what tools you used, what went wrong and how you solved it). Practice telling the story of one project in under a minute.

💼 Career

Your résumé is a one-page highlight reel. List experience with action verbs and results ("Trained three new volunteers," not "Responsible for training"). Many jobs also want a short cover letter that says why this job and why you. A LinkedIn profile can help once you're old enough (LinkedIn requires users to be at least 16).

🚀 Work for yourself

You need a portfolio site (or a simple page) showing your best work, and an "about me" pitch: who you help, what you make, and why you're the person to do it. Clients hire people they trust, so be clear and friendly.

🌍 Crossing borders

Applications in other countries can look very different. Some ask focused questions about the subject you want to study. For example, the UK's UCAS personal statement is three short questions instead of one open essay. Read each school's instructions carefully, and explain your background clearly, since readers may not know how US homeschooling works.

6

🏅 Activities & Honors

On the Common App (🎓), you can list up to 10 activities (the section is now called "Activities and experiences"), each with a description of up to 150 characters, plus up to 5 honors. Check commonapp.org, since details can change. Homeschool things count: a business you run, tutoring your siblings, a co-op role, a robotics team, a part-time job. Lead with action verbs and numbers: "Built and sold 40 birdhouses; managed a $600 budget" beats "Woodworking hobby."

7

Your 30-second pitch

Every lane needs this one. It's how you answer "So, tell me about yourself" in an interview, a networking chat, or a line at the grocery store. Keep it to three parts:

  1. Who you are right now ("I'm a homeschooled senior who loves fixing things").
  2. One proof ("I rebuilt two lawn mower engines and run a small repair business").
  3. Where you're headed ("I'm applying to HVAC apprenticeships this spring").

Practice it out loud until it feels natural, not memorized.

8

🏡 Homeschool Edge

You've probably spent more time talking with adults than most teens your age, and you've had time to go deep on things you love. Both make for great interviews and great stories. Use them.

👪 Ask Your Parents

  1. "Would you read my draft and tell me where it doesn't sound like me?" (Feedback, not rewriting.)
  2. "What's a story about me you think shows who I am?" Parents remember great details you forgot.
  3. "Can you help me take photos of my projects for a portfolio?"
