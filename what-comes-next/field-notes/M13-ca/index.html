M13 · The Money Talk 🍁

This is the Canadian version of M13. Costs, aid and wages differ by province and territory, so always check your own province's official sites.

1

Not that money talk

Relax. This isn't the talk where someone says "Money doesn't grow on trees" and turns off the lights in a room you're still sitting in.

This is the fun, useful one: how much each path costs, how much it might pay, and how to make smart choices so money helps your future instead of chasing you around it. Money isn't the only thing that matters in a path. But it matters enough that you deserve real numbers, not guesses.

2

Sticker price vs. what people actually pay

Schools post a sticker price (the published tuition). But many students pay less, because of grants, bursaries and scholarships (free money you don't pay back).

Here are Statistics Canada's average yearly undergraduate tuition figures for Canadian full-time students in 2025–26 (preliminary numbers):

Where Average tuition (one year)
Canada (national average) about $7,734
Newfoundland and Labrador (lowest) about $3,746
Quebec about $3,963
New Brunswick, Saskatchewan, Nova Scotia close to $10,000
International students (national average) about $41,746

A few detective notes on that table:

  • Quebec charges students from other provinces more than Quebec residents, so check the school's tuition page if you're crossing into Quebec.
  • College diplomas usually cost less per year than university degrees.
  • Housing, food, books and travel cost extra, and those can add up to more than tuition.

The lesson: never cross a school off your list just because of its sticker price. Canadian schools don't have one standard "net price calculator," so do this instead: read the school's tuition page, look for its automatic entrance scholarships (many are based on your admission average), and try your province's student-aid estimator. You'll dig into this in M24.

3

What different paths might pay

Statistics Canada tracks what employees earn by education. Here are average hourly wages in 2024 for employees aged 25 and up, from the Labour Force Survey. (Rocky did the "full time all year" math: hourly pay × 40 hours × 52 weeks.)

Highest education Average hourly wage About per year, full time
High school diploma or less $28.82 $60,000
Postsecondary below a bachelor's (trades, apprenticeship, college) $34.38 $71,500
Bachelor's degree or higher $44.67 $93,000

Three important detective notes:

  1. These are averages across millions of people, not promises. What you study or train for matters enormously, and so does where you work. Look up your specific careers on Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca), which shows wages for your region (M07).
  2. The middle row hides a big story. It lumps trades, apprenticeships and college together. In the 2021 Census, men with an apprenticeship certificate earned more on average before the pandemic (about $69,700) than any other group of men or women with education below a bachelor's. Earnings vary a lot by trade and province, so check the trade you're curious about.
  3. Starting and not finishing can leave you with costs but without the credential. Finishing what you start matters. That's one reason choosing the right fit (M14–M17) is such a big deal.
4

Earn while you learn

Some paths pay you while you train:

  • 🔧 Apprenticeships: you're paid from day one. Apprentices usually earn a rising percentage of a journeyperson's wage, set by the trade and province, with raises as you finish hours and school blocks. Ask for the wage schedule. While you're away at in-school technical training, eligible apprentices can get Employment Insurance (EI). The Canada Apprentice Loan offers up to about $20,000, interest-free, for those training blocks. (The old federal apprenticeship grants closed in March 2025, so ignore older websites that still list them.)
  • 💼 Employer tuition help: some companies help pay for courses or credentials while you work.
  • 💼 The Canadian Armed Forces: you're paid while you train, and the ROTP program pays for a degree in exchange for years of service afterward. Check forces.ca for current details.
  • 🎓 Money-saving school moves: starting at a college and transferring (BC's transfer system is especially strong), living at home, and earning entrance scholarships with strong marks.
5

Borrowing: the honest version

A student loan is money you borrow and pay back later. In Canada, most full-time aid comes as a combined federal and provincial package, which you apply for through your province or territory (Quebec, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut run their own programs).

Here's the good news: the federal part of Canada Student Loans has been interest-free since April 2023. The provincial part may charge interest, depending on where you live. Repayment generally starts after a 6-month break once you finish or leave school, and the Repayment Assistance Plan can lower or pause payments if your income is low.

One big recent change: in Ontario, for programs starting on or after August 1, 2026, much more of OSAP is now loan instead of grant (the provincial grant share is capped at about 25%). Check ontario.ca/osap for your own numbers.

The key rule doesn't change: know the total before you sign, and compare it to what your career pays. A common rule of thumb: try to keep your total student debt below what you expect to earn in your first year of work. And be careful with private lines of credit, which don't have the same protections.

6

🚀 Money when you work for yourself

If you're flying the 🚀 flag:

  • Startup costs: start small and test first (remember Maya's $0 dog-walking test from M11?). Avoid going into debt for an idea that hasn't found customers yet.
  • Income goes up and down: keep a cushion of savings for slow months.
  • Taxes: self-employed Canadians pay income tax and both halves of CPP, so set money aside from every payment (M11).
7

🌍 Money across borders

Heading to the US? Canadian citizens can't get FAFSA aid (US federal grants and loans). Some US schools are designated for Canada Student Loans, so check your school, and RESP money can be used at qualifying schools outside Canada. Your best bets are each US school's own international scholarships and athletic aid. The US version of this module covers US prices and aid in more detail.

8

What does minimum wage actually buy? 🍁

Let's do the math with Ontario's general minimum wage, about $17.95 an hour from October 1, 2026. (Every province sets its own, so look up yours.) Working full time, 40 hours a week, 52 weeks a year:

  • $17.95 × 40 hours = $718 a week
  • $718 × 52 weeks = $37,336 a year
  • That's about $3,111 a month before CPP, EI and income tax come off (M10's pay stub, remember?).

After deductions, it's less. Then come rent, food, a phone bill, transportation, insurance... and the surprise bill that always shows up at the worst moment. (The car makes a noise. The car always makes a noise.)

9

🏡 Homeschool Edge

Homeschoolers have some serious money-saving tools: open university courses and provincial high-school courses taken early can lighten your load later (Peek M10 has the options), and many homeschool families are already great at budgeting and finding deals. One extra tip: since many entrance scholarships are based on admission averages, ask each school early how it calculates an average for homeschool applicants. Bring those skills with you.

10

Every hard number has a next step

If a number in this module made your stomach drop, that's okay. Here's the good news: there are lots of ways to make a path affordable. Grants, bursaries, scholarships, an RESP your family may have saved, starting at a college, apprenticeships that pay, employer help, working part-time, and choosing a school that wants to help you. We'll go through all of them in M24. You're not doing this alone.

👪 Ask Your Parents

Money talks can feel awkward, so here's a gentle way to start:

  1. "I'm learning about costs for different paths. Can we talk about what our family is thinking for after graduation?"
  2. "Is there anything you'd want me to know about paying for school or training, like whether I have an RESP?"

Every family's situation is different, and that's okay. Whatever your parents share, Rocky will help you find options that fit.
