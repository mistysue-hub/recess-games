Decision time (the friendly kind)

You've spent thirteen modules collecting clues: what pulls you in, what you're good at, the life you want, how you work best, every road on the map, real careers, real people, real try-outs and real numbers. That's a detective's whole case file.

Today you put the clues together and choose your lanes. Here's the good news before we start: choosing a lane is not a life sentence. You can follow more than one lane, switch later, or add one next year. Nothing you've done gets erased. You're choosing where to point your flashlight next, not carving your future into stone.

(Rocky would like to point out that stone is very hard to edit. Flashlights are much better.)

2

Look how much you already know

Before you decide anything, Rocky will read back what you've told him so far:

  • Interests (M02): your top interest types and "ooh, maybe" careers
  • Strengths (M03): what you're good at, and what other people see in you
  • Values (M04): the life you want, from money to location to free time
  • Work style (M05): your sliders, like hands-on or books, solo or team
  • Roads and careers (M06–M07): the paths that made you go "ooh" and your career one-pagers
  • People and try-outs (M08–M10): who you talked to and what you test-drove
  • Money (M13): what your top paths might cost and pay

You might notice a pattern already. That's a great sign. You might also notice two clues that disagree, like "I love working outdoors" and "my top career is mostly at a desk." That's normal, and it's exactly what a decision matrix is for.

3

The decision matrix

A decision matrix is a fancy name for a simple table that helps you compare options fairly. Here's how it works:

  1. Pick two to four options. Make them specific. Not "college," but "a nursing degree at a state university." Not "a trade," but "an electrician apprenticeship."
  2. Pick five or six things that matter to you. Use your values from M04: pay, debt, hands-on work, time with family, adventure, helping people, being your own boss.
  3. Give each thing a weight from 1 to 5. A 5 means "this matters hugely." A 1 means "nice, but not a big deal."
  4. Score each option from 1 to 5 on each thing. How well does it deliver?
  5. Multiply each score by its weight, then add them up. Highest total wins round one.

Meet Sam. (Made up, but Sam would like you to know he's very real in spirit.) Sam is choosing between three options:

  • A: an electrician apprenticeship
  • B: an engineering degree
  • C: an HVAC certificate, then his own business

Each box shows his score, then the weight-times-score points:

The HVAC business wins... by one point. Hmm. One point is not exactly a landslide.

4

The wobble test

When the top two are close, give your weights a little wobble. Change one weight up or down by 1 and see whether the winner changes.

Sam tries it. He wonders whether "being my own boss" is really a 3, or more like a 2. If he lowers it to 2, the apprenticeship scores 71 and the HVAC business scores 70. The winner flips!

That tells Sam something useful: this is a close call, and a few more clues will help. The best tie-breaker is a real-world try-out from M09: a day shadowing an HVAC business owner, a talk with an electrician apprentice, or a weekend helping someone on a job site.

If your winner survives every wobble, congratulations: you can feel confident about it.

5

The gut check

Numbers are great, but you're not a spreadsheet. (Rocky has checked. You're definitely not a spreadsheet.)

When the matrix picks a winner, ask yourself one more question: does the winner feel right?

  • If yes, wonderful. Your head and your heart agree.
  • If you feel a little disappointed, pay attention. That feeling is a clue. Ask: which weight is lying to me? Maybe pay matters less than you said, or adventure matters more. Adjust it honestly and run the numbers again.
6

Choosing your lanes

Now choose which lanes to follow for the rest of the course:

  • 🎓 College / University
  • 🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship
  • 💼 Career (including the military and gap years)
  • 🚀 Work for yourself is a flag you can turn on alongside any lane.

You can follow one lane, two, or all three. If you're still undecided, choose Exploring, which keeps all three lanes on. Plenty of people choose Exploring, and it's a smart move, not a cop-out.

Then name two things:

  • Your first-choice path: the specific option you're aiming for right now.
  • Your Plan B: a strong second path you'd be glad to take. A Plan B isn't a consolation prize. It's a different good road, and lots of people end up loving theirs. (Rocky's Plan B was to be a lighthouse keeper. He still thinks about it on foggy days.)
7

🏡 Homeschool Edge

You've already helped make big choices about your own education: what to study, how to learn, sometimes even what your days look like. Weighing options with your family is something you've practiced for years. This is the same skill, just with a bigger trail map.

8

🔭 Lane Peek

Take one option from a lane you didn't pick and run it through your matrix anyway. Did it score higher or lower than you expected? Sometimes the "never" option surprises you. Sometimes it confirms you're right. Either way, you learn something.

9

👪 Ask Your Parents

Your parents know you well, and this choice affects your whole family. Ask them:

  1. "Here's my decision matrix. Is there anything you'd add to my list of things that matter?"
  2. "Do any of my scores look off to you?"
  3. "What do you think of my first choice and my Plan B?"

Rocky can help you put your matrix into a short note to share.
