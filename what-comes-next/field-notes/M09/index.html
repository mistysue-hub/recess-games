Try before you buy

You wouldn't buy a car without a test drive. (Well, you might, but your parents would make a face.) Careers work the same way. Reading about a job is great. Trying it is how you find out whether you love it, like it, or want to run screaming in the other direction.

All three of those are wins. Finding out a career isn't for you at 16 is much cheaper and faster than finding out at 26.

Today you'll learn the Try-It Ladder: lots of ways to test-drive a career, from five-minute peeks to real work.

2

The Try-It Ladder

Start on any rung. Climb as high as you like. Each step gives you a better look at the real work. The 🥾 boots show how big a commitment each rung is, from "do it in your pajamas tonight" to "this is basically a job."

  1. 👀 Watch 🥾 (an evening). Career interview videos (like Roadtrip Nation from M07) show what people do all day and how they feel about it. Pajamas allowed.
  2. 🎮 Simulate 🥾 (a few hours). Free virtual job simulations, like the ones on Forage (theforage.com), are built with real companies. You do the kinds of tasks a new intern would, with videos from real employees. Most take a few hours. 🟡 They're aimed mostly at college students but open to anyone, and finishing one is something you can list on a résumé.
  3. 🗣️ Talk 🥾 (15 minutes). An informational interview (from M08) with someone who does the job.
  4. 🧑‍🏭 Shadow 🥾🥾 (a morning or a day). Follow someone at work. You'll see the parts nobody mentions in videos, like the paperwork, the waiting, and the really good coffee machine.
  5. 🤝 Volunteer 🥾🥾 (a few hours a week). Animal shelters, hospitals, food banks, libraries, museums, camps, community gardens and building projects all welcome volunteers. Real responsibility, real people, real experience.
  6. 💵 Part-time job 🥾🥾🥾. Any job teaches showing up on time, working with customers and coworkers, and handling money. A job in a field you're curious about is even better.
  7. 📋 Internship 🥾🥾🥾. A set period of real work in a field, sometimes paid and sometimes not. You learn, you contribute, and you meet people who can vouch for you later.
  8. 🔧 Pre-apprenticeship or youth apprenticeship 🥾🥾🥾🥾. A structured start in a skilled trade, often with training and pay. Apprenticeship.gov explains them and lists openings. The legal minimum age for a registered apprenticeship is 16, but most construction trades require you to be 18, so check each listing. Many union programs give an aptitude test that often covers algebra and reading; a few weeks of review beforehand can really help.
  9. 🚀 A small project or side gig 🥾 to 🥾🥾🥾🥾 (you decide). Mow lawns, sell your art, build a website for a local business, run a pet-sitting service. Being your own boss for a summer is a test-drive too.

A test-drive can change your whole route

Meet Eli. (Made up, but very real-feeling.) Eli was sure he wanted to be a veterinarian. Then he spent a morning shadowing at a vet clinic and discovered two things: he got woozy watching surgery, and he absolutely loved the part where the vet tech calmed a terrified golden retriever. A few months later he was volunteering at a shelter, learning about animal behavior and dog training, and much more excited than before.

Eli didn't fail his test-drive. He aced it. A test-drive that says "not this, but something next door" is one of the most useful things that can happen to you. (Also, the golden retriever was fine. Rocky checked.)

4

🏡 Homeschool Edge

This is where homeschoolers can really shine. Your schedule is flexible. Most students can only shadow, volunteer or intern on weekends or in summer. You may be able to do it on a Tuesday morning, when the workplace is actually busy and people have time to show you around. That's a big advantage. Use it!

5

Finding internships as a homeschooler

Homeschoolers don't usually have a school career office posting internships. That's okay. Here's where to look:

  • Your People Map (M08). This is usually the most effective route. Ask your people, "Do you know anyone who might let me shadow or help out?"
  • Contact local businesses directly. A short, polite email to a small business ("I'm a homeschooled student interested in veterinary work. Could I shadow for a day?") works more often than you'd think. Rocky will help you draft it.
  • Youth programs at museums, zoos, libraries, hospitals, research labs and government agencies. Many run teen volunteer or internship programs. (The Smithsonian's natural history museum in Washington, D.C., for example, has run a paid summer internship for local high schoolers.) Search "teen volunteer" or "high school internship" plus your town's museums, zoo and hospital. ⚠️ Deadlines for summer programs are often in winter or early spring, so check early.
  • Free internship databases for high schoolers (Rocky can help you search and run the Source Detective questions from M07 on each one). A heads-up: some sites list internships for free but also sell a paid "matching" service. Use the free part only.
6

🚨 The golden rule: never pay to work

Never pay money to get an internship, a job or an "opportunity." Real employers pay you, or at least don't charge you. If someone asks you to pay a fee to start, buy a "starter kit," or pay for "training" before you're hired, that's a big red flag. Walk away and tell your parents.

Other safety basics:

  • Get a parent's okay before starting anything.
  • Your parents should know where you'll be, who you'll be with, and how you're getting there.
  • You should never be alone with an adult in a private place.
  • If anything feels off, you can always leave. You don't need a reason.
7

Work permits and rules for teens

There are special rules for workers under 18. Under federal law, 14 is the youngest age for most non-farm jobs, and 14- and 15-year-olds have limits on how many hours they can work and how late (with tighter limits during the school year). At 16, most of those hour limits go away. Some jobs, like most driving for work or using certain power machines, are off-limits or tightly limited until 17 or 18 because they're more dangerous.

Federal law doesn't require a work permit, but many states do, and states can add stricter rules of their own. Where the state rule is stricter, the state rule wins. Depending on the state, permits come from the school district, the state labor department, or (for homeschoolers) sometimes your parent. Your parents can find your state's rule on the US Department of Labor's state child labor laws page. The US Department of Labor's YouthRules and "Workers Under 18" pages are a good starting point, and your state's labor department has the final word.

(Rocky would love to tell you exactly what your state requires, but rules change, so he'll help you find the official page instead. He considers this the responsible choice, even though it physically hurts him not to know.)

Your Experience Log

Every rung you climb goes into your Experience Log: what you did, when, what you learned, and who you worked with. Rocky keeps it in your profile.

Why bother? Because this log later becomes:

  • your résumé (M21),
  • your list of people who can recommend you (M22),
  • your best stories for job and program interviews (M23),
  • and evidence for choosing your lanes (M14).

Even small things count. Babysitting, helping at your church's food pantry, running your family's online shop, tutoring a younger sibling's co-op friend: write it all down by telling Rocky.

🌍 Going global

International volunteer and work programs exist, but quality varies a lot. Check who runs the program, how they make money, and what past participants say (hello, Source Detective). Be extra careful with programs that charge high fees for "volunteering."

🔭 Lane Peek

Pick one rung of the Try-It Ladder and imagine trying it in a lane you weren't planning on. A college-bound student shadowing an electrician. A future welder trying a day at a lab. You might find a surprising connection, or a great idea for a friend.

11

🍁 In Canada?

Work rules for teens are set by your province. For example, Ontario allows many jobs at 14 (older for factories and construction), BC needs a parent's written okay under 16, and Alberta limits 13- and 14-year-olds to certain jobs. Most provinces don't use a "work permit," but you'll need a free Social Insurance Number (SIN), which you can apply for yourself at 12 or older. Apprenticeships are provincial too, so check your province's official page with a parent.

👪 Ask Your Parents

Ask your parents:

  1. "Can I try [shadowing, volunteering, a job] this season? What would you need to know first?"
  2. "Can you help me with rides and any paperwork, like a work permit?"
  3. "Do you know anyone who might let me shadow them?"
