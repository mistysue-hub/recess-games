The stuff nobody teaches you

Nobody hands you a manual when you become an adult. Suddenly there are leases, bank accounts, credit scores and forms with names like "authorization." It can feel like everyone else got the manual and you didn't.

Good news: this is the manual. Today you'll learn the everyday skills that make independent life work, from budgets to IDs to what changes on your 18th birthday. (Rocky has laminated his. He won't say how many times.)

2

Your first budget

A budget is just a plan for your money before you spend it. Start simple:

  1. Money in: your pay after taxes (M13), plus any regular help.
  2. Needs: rent, food, transportation, phone, insurance.
  3. Savings: even a little, every paycheck. Build an emergency fund for surprises.
  4. Wants: fun stuff. Yes, it belongs in the budget too.

If money in is less than money out, something has to change. Track your spending for a month. Most people are surprised where it goes.

3

Saving for later

Compound interest means your savings earn money, and then that money earns money too. Start young and small amounts can grow into big ones.

  • Roth IRA: once you have earned income (from a job or your business), you can put some of it in a Roth IRA, a retirement account. Check irs.gov for the current limits.
  • 401(k) match: if your employer matches what you save, put in at least enough to get the full match. It's free money (M10).
4

Getting around and a place to live

Transportation: compare the real cost of a car (payment, insurance, gas, repairs, parking) with transit, biking, carpooling or living close to work or school. (Insurance, accidents and traffic stops are in M29.)

Housing: before you sign a lease, read it with a parent. Know the rent, the deposit, how long you're committed, and who pays for utilities and repairs. Get a roommate agreement in writing (quiet hours, chores, guests, splitting bills). Renters insurance is usually cheap and protects your stuff. Take photos of every room on move-in day. They help you get your security deposit back when you leave.

5

Speaking up for yourself

At school or work, nobody will check in on you the way your family did. Self-advocacy means speaking up for what you need:

  • Professors: go to office hours. Ask questions early, not the night before the test.
  • Bosses: ask when you don't understand, and tell them early if there's a problem.
  • Offices (financial aid, housing, disability services, HR): be polite, specific and persistent. Write down who you talked to and when.
6

Making friends in a new place

Say yes to things in the first few weeks: orientation events, clubs, a study group, a crew lunch, a faith group, a pickup game. Go more than once, because friendships grow from showing up again. Everyone else is new too.

7

Adult paperwork

  • ID: get a state ID or driver's license (your state's DMV lists the documents you need). Ask for a REAL ID (it has a star) if you'll fly within the US and don't have a passport; it needs extra documents. Keep your birth certificate and Social Security card somewhere safe at home, not in your wallet.
  • Bank account: a checking account for bills and direct deposit, and a savings account for your goals. Watch out for monthly fees and overdraft fees.
  • Health insurance: know whether you're covered and carry your card. In the US, you can usually stay on a parent's plan until age 26.
  • Taxes: if you earn money, you may need to file a tax return each spring. Your W-2 (from employers) shows what you earned. Free filing help is available.
8

Health care on your own

  • Make your own appointments for the doctor and dentist. Have your insurance card ready when you call.
  • Know where to go: your primary care doctor for checkups and ongoing problems, urgent care for things that can't wait but aren't dangerous (a sprain, a bad sore throat), and the ER (or 911) for emergencies like trouble breathing or chest pain.
  • Refill prescriptions before you run out. Many pharmacies have apps that remind you.
  • Mental health counts too. Talk to your doctor or your campus counseling center. In a crisis, call or text 988 any time.
9

Credit basics

Your credit score is a number lenders use to guess how likely you are to pay back money. Your credit history can affect renting an apartment and getting a car loan, and some employers look at your credit report too.

  • Paying on time matters most. Every time.
  • Use only a small part of your limit, and pay the full balance if you can.
  • Safe ways to start: a secured card (you put down a deposit), or being added as an authorized user on a parent's card.
  • Debt traps to avoid: carrying a balance on high-interest cards, "buy now, pay later" stacked on top of each other, and payday loans.

If you're under 21, you'll usually need to show your own income to get a credit card. Being an authorized user on a parent's card is another way to start.

10

Staying safe online

Identity theft is when someone uses your info to open accounts or borrow money in your name. Protect yourself:

  • Watch for phishing: texts or emails that rush you to click a link or "verify" your account. Go to the real site yourself instead.
  • Use strong, different passwords for every account (a password manager helps), and turn on two-factor login.
  • Freeze your credit at all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion). It's free, and you can unfreeze it when you need a loan.
  • If it happens, go to identitytheft.gov for a step-by-step recovery plan.
11

🎂 You're 18! Now what?

Turning 18 changes some things you might not expect:

  • School records: once you turn 18, or start taking classes at a college at any age (including dual enrollment), you generally control your records at that school. Colleges usually need your permission to share your grades with your parents (there are a few exceptions, like when parents claim you as a dependent on their taxes).
  • Health information: at 18, your parents no longer automatically get your medical information. A signed permission form (often called a HIPAA authorization) makes it easy for your doctors to talk with them. Many families also set up a health care proxy or power of attorney so a parent can help in an emergency. Rules vary by state.
  • Voting: at 18 you can vote. Many states let you pre-register earlier. Vote.gov shows how to register in your state, and many states let you do it online.
  • Jury duty: as an adult citizen, you may get a summons to serve on a jury. Don't ignore it. Follow the instructions, and contact the court if you need to ask for a delay.
  • Selective Service: almost all young men living in the US must be registered within 30 days of turning 18. Registration is switching to automatic, planned to start by December 2026, but until sss.gov says it's live, you sign up yourself within 30 days of turning 18. Registering is not the same as being drafted. Check sss.gov for how it works now.

Launch skills in every lane

🎓 College

Learn your school's portal, email and student ID systems in week one. Find the offices you'll need: advising, financial aid, the health center and tutoring.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

You may need your own tools, work boots and safety gear, plus reliable transportation to job sites. Budget for them early: a starter tool set plus boots and safety glasses can run a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars. Ask whether your program or employer offers a tool allowance or discount.

💼 Career

Set up direct deposit, read your first pay stub (M10), and learn how benefits enrollment works. Some benefits have short sign-up windows.

🚀 Work for yourself

Keep business and personal money separate, set aside money for taxes (M11), and track your income and expenses every month.

🌍 Crossing borders

Get a passport early (it can take weeks or months), and keep copies of important documents. If you're living abroad, learn how to reach your country's embassy or consulate.
