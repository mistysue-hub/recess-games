Tests are keys, not judges

Let's get this out of the way: a test score doesn't measure how smart you are, how hard you work, or how good a nurse, welder, coder or soldier you'll be. A test is a key. Some doors need one, some don't, and some let you in either way but give you a better seat if you bring one.

Today you'll figure out which keys your doors need, and make a plan to get them without panic. (Rocky has been told he's not allowed to say "test-tastic." He's trying to respect that.)

2

First question: do you even need a test?

It depends on your lanes:

  • 🎓 College: many colleges are test-optional (you choose whether to send scores; you send official scores from your College Board or ACT account, and a common rule of thumb is to send them if they're at or above the school's middle range), some are test-required, and a few have brought their requirements back in recent years. Check every school on your list.
  • 🔧 Trade school or apprenticeship: community colleges often use a placement test, and some apprenticeships give an aptitude test in math and reading.
  • 💼 Career: the military uses the ASVAB, and some employers give skills tests.
  • 🚀 Work for yourself: no admission test, but industry certifications can help clients trust you.

Here's the homeschool twist: because colleges and programs can't compare you with classmates, outside proof counts extra. A good test score, dual-enrollment grades or a credit-by-exam can back up your homeschool transcript. Even at test-optional schools, a strong score can help.

3

🎓 The college admission tests

  • SAT: a digital test you take at a test center on a laptop or tablet, using College Board's Bluebook app. Free official practice tests are in Bluebook too.
  • ACT: a shorter test of English, reading and math. Science is now optional.
  • CLT (Classic Learning Test): taken online with a remote proctor, at home or in any quiet room where you can be alone. (Some schools give it in class to their own students.) Accepted by a growing list of colleges, including some state university systems and the US service academies, so check each school on your list. Homeschoolers usually take it on the at-home dates.
  • PSAT/NMSQT: a practice SAT that can also qualify juniors for National Merit scholarships. It's given only in schools during an October window, so homeschoolers ask a local school to test them as an "away student" well before fall.

Homeschoolers register just like everyone else, but they use a homeschool code instead of a school code. The testing company lists the right one on its registration page. Sign-ups usually close about 2 to 5 weeks before test day, and seats near home can fill up, so pick your dates 2–3 months ahead. Many students take the SAT or ACT in the spring of junior year and again in the fall of senior year. Try a free practice test of each, then pick the one that feels better.

Score-sending smarts: many colleges superscore, combining your best section scores from different test dates (ACT even calculates a superscore for you). Each SAT registration includes four free score sends you can use until nine days after the test, and ACT registration includes free reports too (check act.org for the current number and timing). Some colleges let you self-report scores on the application and only ask for official ones if you enroll, which saves fees.

4

🎓 Earning college credit early

Some tests can earn you college credit before you even start:

  • AP exams: taken in May. Homeschoolers can't order AP exams themselves, so you find a school that will let you test with them (the AP Course Ledger on College Board's site helps). Schools must order exams by about mid-November, and many want you to ask in September or October, so this is a fall job, not a spring one. After that, a school might still add you with a late fee, so it's worth asking.
  • CLEP: 30-plus intro-level subjects, taken at a test center or at home with a remote proctor. Modern States (modernstates.org) offers free prep courses and a voucher for the exam fee once you pass the course (a test center may still charge a small fee of its own; keep your receipt, because Modern States may pay it back; check the current rules).
  • DSST: similar to CLEP, with some different subjects.
  • Dual enrollment: real college classes, often at a community college, that may also count for high school. (Your community college may ask for a placement test first.)

Now the golden rule, which Rocky will repeat until you hum it in your sleep: check each college's credit policy before you spend time or money. Colleges decide which exams they accept, the scores they need, and how many credits they'll take. A credit your college won't accept is just a very expensive certificate.

5

💡 Testing accommodations

If you have a learning difference or disability, you may be able to get testing accommodations, like extra time or a quiet room. Homeschoolers usually request them directly from the testing organization, with documentation. For the ACT, that means ACT's request form, a statement from whoever teaches you (usually a parent) and the diagnostic paperwork. For College Board, you can work with a local school's accommodations coordinator or apply directly with College Board's paper form. Make it a parent ask. Reviews can take weeks, and College Board says to allow up to seven, so ask long before you register.

6

The other lanes

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Community colleges often use ACCUPLACER or a similar placement test, and many accept SAT or ACT scores instead. Some apprenticeship programs give their own aptitude test in math and reading comprehension. Free practice tests are usually online, and a little algebra review goes a long way.

Union apprenticeship aptitude tests often cover algebra and reading comprehension, and your score can count for a big part of where you land on the ranking list (usually along with an interview). A few weeks of steady review can move you up, so ask each program what the test covers and whether you can retake it.

💼 Career

The ASVAB helps decide which military jobs you qualify for. Homeschoolers can take it through a recruiter (with a parent involved) or at a local school's ASVAB testing session if the school allows outside students. Some employers give skills tests (typing, math, customer-service scenarios). Ask the employer what to expect, then practice.

🚀 Work for yourself

Industry certifications (in IT, design software, safety training and more) can show clients you know your stuff. Check that employers or clients in your field actually value a certification before you pay for it. (Hello again, Source Detective.)

🌍 Crossing borders

International students coming to US programs often need an English test such as TOEFL, IELTS or Duolingo English Test. Check which ones each school accepts.

7

Your testing plan

A good testing plan answers four questions:

  1. Which tests do my lanes and schools actually need (or reward)?
  2. When will I take each one, and when is the registration deadline?
  3. How will I prepare? (Free official practice first.)
  4. Who needs to help? (Fees, ID, rides, a host school.) ID tip: test days need an official photo ID. No driver's license or passport? The SAT has a Student ID Form your parent fills out with a notary (US test centers only), and the ACT has its own ID rules, so check both early.

🏡 Homeschool Edge

You already know how you study best, and you can fit test prep into your schedule in a way most students can't. Thirty minutes of practice a day for a few weeks beats a panicked weekend every time.

👪 Ask Your Parents

  1. "Can you help me pay for test fees and get to the test center?"
  2. "Can we call local schools together about testing for the PSAT or AP?"
  3. "Do we have records of any testing accommodations I might need?" (Only if it applies to you.)
