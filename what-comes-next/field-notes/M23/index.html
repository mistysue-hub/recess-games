What job hunting is really like

Let's start with the truth, because it'll save you some worry: job hunting takes time, silence is normal, and rejection is part of everyone's story. You might send ten applications and hear back from two. That's not a sign you're bad at this. That's just how it works, for everyone, at every age.

The good news: it's a skill, and skills get better with practice. Today you'll learn where jobs really come from, how to apply, how to ace an interview, and how to spot the scams hiding in the crowd. (Rocky once applied to be a professional calendar tester. He's still waiting to hear back. He's being very patient about it.)

2

Where jobs are actually found

  1. Your network first. Many jobs are filled through people who know people. Tell your People Map (M08) what you're looking for.
  2. Company career pages. If you know where you want to work, go straight to its website.
  3. Job boards. Big sites list lots of jobs. Stick to well-known ones and stay alert for scams.
  4. American Job Centers. Free, government-funded help with job searches, résumés and training. Find one through CareerOneStop.
  5. Staffing agencies. They match workers with employers. Most real ones are paid by the employer, not by you. If one asks you to pay up front to find work, that's a red flag.
  6. Walking in. Many local businesses still hire people who ask in person (with a résumé in hand and a smile).
3

Reading a job posting

Every posting has must-haves (requirements) and nice-to-haves (preferred). If you meet most of the must-haves, apply. Postings are wish lists, not tests you have to ace. Pay attention to the hours, the pay (if listed), the location and the age requirements.

4

Apply and track

For each application, tweak your résumé and cover letter (M21) to match what the posting asks for. Then log it in your application tracker: the job, where you found it, the date you applied, a contact name, a follow-up date and the status. Rocky can keep it for you. (Yes, he's thrilled. It's basically a calendar.)

5

Interviews

Interviews come in many shapes: phone, video, in person, a skills test, or a working interview where you try the job for a short time. To get ready:

  • Research the employer (M10).
  • Prepare stories with STAR: the Situation, the Task, the Action you took and the Result. "Tell me about a time you solved a problem" is much easier with a STAR story ready.
  • Have two or three questions for them, like "What does a great first month look like here?"
  • Dress one step nicer than people dress for that job every day.
  • Practice out loud. Rocky runs mock interviews, and he's a tough (but kind) interviewer.
6

After the interview

Send a short thank-you note within a day. If you don't hear back by the date they gave you (or in about a week), send one polite follow-up. If there's still no answer, keep going with other applications. Silence isn't personal.

7

First-job paperwork

When you're hired, you'll fill out forms like the W-4 (for tax withholding) and the I-9 (to prove you're allowed to work in the US; bring the original ID documents your employer asks for, like a passport, or a state ID plus a Social Security card or birth certificate. No ID yet? See M28). Some employers check references (M22) or run a background check. If you're under 18, some states require a work permit, and there are limits on the hours and kinds of jobs teens can do. Rules vary by state, so check with your parents and your state labor department.

8

🚨 Job scams

Scammers love job hunters. Watch for these red flags:

  • They want you to pay for training, equipment or "a starter kit" to get the job.
  • They send you a check and ask you to send some of the money back or buy gift cards. The check will bounce, and you'll owe the money.
  • A job offer comes out of nowhere by text or social media, especially for a job you never applied for.
  • They ask for private info (bank login, Social Security number) before you've had a real interview or offer.

Normal hiring steps can feel weird but are fine: video interviews, reference checks, a background check after an offer, and those W-4 and I-9 forms. If you're unsure, stop and ask a parent before you send anything.

Spotted a scam? Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. It takes a few minutes and helps warn the next person.

9

Job hunting in every lane

🎓 College

Look for campus jobs (libraries, dining halls, labs) and Federal Work-Study if it's in your financial aid package (a part-time job, often on campus, that's part of your aid). Work-study funding can change from year to year, so check what each school actually offers. Internships often have application deadlines months ahead, so check your M19 calendar.

🔧 Trade School / Apprenticeship

Apprenticeship applications often include an aptitude test (M18), an interview and sometimes a ranking list, and some programs open only a few times a year. Watch Apprenticeship.gov, local union training centers and trade job boards, and follow up politely.

Gather your proof early. Many programs (especially construction) ask for:

  • Proof you're 18 (often the minimum age)
  • Your diploma (M20) or equivalent
  • A driver's license and a reliable ride to job sites
  • A drug test
  • Being able to do physical work (lifting, climbing, working outside)

💼 Career

For full-time jobs, treat the hunt like a part-time job itself: a few applications a week, steady follow-ups, and lots of networking.

More places to look:

  • USAJOBS.gov lists federal jobs. Its Pathways program has paid internships for students and starter jobs for recent graduates.
  • CareerOneStop links to your state's job bank.
  • Job Corps offers free job training, often with housing, for ages 16 to 24. Its centers have been changing since 2025, so check jobcorps.gov for what's open near you.

For the military, the recruiting process includes meeting a recruiter, the ASVAB and a medical exam. Bring a parent, ask lots of questions, and remember that nothing is final until you sign.

  • Most first enlistments add up to 8 years of total service: some on active duty or drilling, the rest in the reserves. Ask your recruiter how yours splits.
  • Many recruits join the Delayed Entry Program, which means you sign now and ship to training later.
  • If a recruiter promises a job, bonus or ship date, it must be written in your contract. A spoken promise doesn't count.
  • A parent should read everything with you before you sign. Todaysmilitary.com explains each step.

🚀 Work for yourself

Your first clients usually come from people who know you. Share your portfolio, ask happy customers for referrals, and check local listings. Many freelance platforms require you to be 18 (or to use a parent's account), so check each site's rules. For example (rules change, so read each site's own terms):

  • Etsy: account owners are 18+. Teens 13 to 17 can sell through a parent's account, with their supervision.
  • YouTube: 13+ to have a channel. To earn ad money under 18, you link to a parent's AdSense account.
  • Fiverr: 18+, or 13 to 17 with a parent-owned account.
  • Upwork: 18+.

🌍 Crossing borders

International students in the US have strict work rules, especially for jobs off campus. Talk to your school's international student office before taking any job.

🏡 Homeschool Edge

You can job hunt on a weekday morning when managers have time to talk, and your flexible schedule can be a selling point. Lots of employers need people who can work during school hours.

🍁 In Canada?

Search Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca), your province's job centres (like Employment Ontario or WorkBC), and Canada Summer Jobs postings. On day one you'll give your employer your SIN and fill out TD1 tax forms instead of the I-9 and W-4. Federal student jobs (FSWEP) are for full-time students at a recognized school, so homeschoolers should ask before applying. If you're curious about the Canadian Armed Forces, start at forces.ca with a parent.

13

👪 Ask Your Parents

  1. "Do I need a work permit in our state, and can you help me get one?"
  2. "Can you be my safety check before I send personal info to any employer?"
  3. "Can you drive me to interviews, or help me plan the route?"
